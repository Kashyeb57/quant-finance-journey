Introduction to Derivative (Forwards & Futures)

Where derivatives sit in quantitative finance​

Quantitative finance draws on five things at once:

  1. Mathematics
  2. Statistics
  3. Finance
  4. Economics
  5. Programming

Within that, the working areas are derivatives, risk management (market risk in particular), and quantitative modelling. This session is the first step into the first of those.

Objectives for the session

  • What a derivative is
  • Exchange vs Over-the-Counter (OTC)
  • The four types of derivative: forward, future, option, swap
  • The payoff diagram
  • The three types of trader: hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs

What is a derivative?​

The intuition first. Think about orange juice and oranges.

  • If oranges are very expensive, orange juice is expensive.
  • If oranges are cheap, orange juice is cheap.

Orange juice is deriving its value from oranges. It has no independent value — it moves because the thing it is made from moves.

TermIn the analogy
DerivativeOrange juice
Underlying assetOranges

Definition. A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is derived from the value of an underlying asset.

What can be the underlying asset?​

  • Stocks
  • Bonds
  • Commodities
  • Currencies
  • Interest rates
  • Market indices

Where derivatives trade: Exchange vs OTC​

There are exactly two venues:

  • Exchange
  • Over the Counter (OTC)

Exchange​

An exchange is a marketplace or platform where various financial instruments are traded.

Examples: BSE, NYSE, ICE (Intercontinental Exchange, USA). What trades there: stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities and derivatives.

Key features of an exchange

a) Standardization. Contracts are standardized in terms of size, expiration date and other specifications. If the exchange defines the contract as 100 kg of oranges, everyone trades that same unit — only the price varies (102.5, 107.8, …).

b) Regulation. Exchange markets are heavily regulated, which gives greater transparency between market participants.

c) Clearing house. A clearing house acts as an intermediary between buyer and seller, guaranteeing that both parties honour the contract. Because it stands in the middle, there is far less default risk.

d) Liquidity. Highly liquid instruments — contracts trade in large volume.

Over the Counter (OTC)​

The OTC market is a decentralized marketplace where financial instruments are traded directly between two parties — a buyer and a seller.

Key features of OTC

a) Customization. Contracts can be customized to the specific needs of the two parties, including size, maturity and underlying asset. Where an exchange sells you a fixed 1,000 kg lot at $1/kg, OTC lets you shape the deal.

b) Regulation. These are less regulated, which can lead to a lack of transparency.

c) Clearing house. Historically there was no clearing house in OTC — the two parties faced each other directly, so if one defaulted the other simply lost.

d) Liquidity. Because contracts are customized in nature, they are illiquid — a bespoke contract is hard to hand on to someone else.

What changed after 2007–08​

The financial crisis changed the structure of OTC markets. Traditionally OTC was a web of bilateral agreements: every participant faced every other participant directly, and one failure propagated through the web.

Afterwards (in the USA first), a CCP — Central Counter Party was introduced in the middle of OTC trades as well:

Every line in the left panel is a credit exposure — someone who owes, or is owed by, someone else. One failure anywhere in that web propagates along the lines to everyone connected to it. That is how counterparty risk went systemic in 2008.

Putting a CCP in the middle does not just tidy the picture, it changes the arithmetic. With NNN participants:

bilateral links=N(N−1)2versuslinks through a CCP=N\text{bilateral links} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \text{links through a CCP} = Nbilateral links=2N(N−1)​versuslinks through a CCP=N

For the eight participants drawn above that is 28 exposures collapsing to 8. At 100 participants it is 4,950 versus 100. The CCP becomes the counterparty to both sides of every trade, so both parties are obligated to the contract and the chance of the other side defaulting on you is very low — you are no longer exposed to them at all, only to the CCP.

Swaps are the classic OTC example — IRS (interest-rate swaps) and CS (currency swaps), traded between parties such as commercial banks.

The four types of derivative​

  • a) Forward
  • b) Future
  • c) Option
  • d) Swap

The rest of this note covers futures and forwards. Options are covered in the practical-example note.

Future contracts​

Futures trade on an exchange.

A future contract is a standardized financial derivative that obligates the buyer to purchase, and the seller to sell, the asset at a predetermined future date and a predetermined price.

Note the three words that carry all the weight: standardized, obligates, predetermined.

Worked example — 100 barrels of oil​

An investor enters a future contract to buy 100 barrels of oil at $70 per barrel, expiring 3 months from now.

  • A buys 100 barrels of oil at $70 (from B)
  • B sells 100 barrels of oil at $70

At t=0t = 0t=0 the agreed price is $70. At t=3t = 3t=3 months, whatever the market does, the deal happens at $70:

Market price at t=3t=3t=3Contract priceOutcome for the buyer (A)
$65$70Loss — paying 70 for something worth 65
$70$70No profit, no loss
$75$70Profit — paying 70 for something worth 75

Whatever A loses, B gains, and vice versa. A future is a zero-sum contract.

Worked example — the farmer​

The same idea from the seller's side. Participants can be individuals (a farmer) or financial institutions.

A farmer agrees to sell 1,000 kg of corn at $2/kg to Company A, three months from now.

Market price at t=3t=3t=3Locked priceResult for the farmer
$1$2Profit — sells at 2 what is worth 1
$2$2No profit, no loss
$5$2Loss — could have sold at 5

Characteristics of a future contract​

a) Standardized agreement — fixed in terms of size, expiration date, underlying asset, quantity and quality.

b) Clearing house acts as an intermediary between buyer and seller, making sure the contract is honoured.

c) Marked to market daily. Gains and losses that occur in the contract are settled every day, not just at expiry. If you hold 100 shares' worth at $90 and the price moves to $91, that difference is settled that day.

d) Highly liquid instrument.

e) CCP in between → less counterparty risk: the chance of the other party defaulting is very small.

Why mark-to-market matters​

Marking to market is what stops a large loss from accumulating silently until expiry. Consider a position that drifts against you over three months: on a $100 contract × 1,000 units, a $10 adverse move is

$10×1,000=$10,000\$10 \times 1{,}000 = \$10{,}000$10×1,000=$10,000

If none of that is settled until the end, the losing party may simply be unable to pay — and a default happens. Settling daily keeps the exposure small.

This is enforced through margin:

  • Initial margin — money posted up front to open the position
  • Margin call — a demand for more money when the daily settlement erodes it

Forward contracts​

A forward contract is a customized agreement between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a predetermined date.

Two things follow immediately:

  • Forward contracts are traded OTC only.
  • A forward is also an obligation — like a future, both sides must perform.

What "customized" actually means​

Everything is negotiable between the two parties:

  • Quality can be changed
  • Quantity can be changed
  • Price can be negotiated

Key characteristics of a forward contract​

a) Customizable agreement between two parties.

b) Traded OTC only.

c) Less liquid — a bespoke contract is hard to pass on.

d) Higher counterparty risk.

e) No margin requirement, because there is no CCP in between. (On an exchange, the CCP demands collateral/cash; in a plain forward there is nobody to demand it.)

f) Priced only at maturity — there is no daily settlement.

Futures vs Forwards​

FutureForward
AgreementStandardizedCustomized
VenueExchangeOTC
CounterpartyCCP in betweenNo CCP (sometimes there can be one)
LiquidityHighly liquidLess liquid
SettlementMarked to market dailyPriced at maturity
MarginRequired (initial margin, margin calls)None

Because the two instruments price differently, the same exposure can quote differently in each market — e.g. $1 at Rs 84 in the futures market versus Rs 85 on a forward.

Payoff (forward contract)​

Identical in form to the future:

Long=ST−KShort=K−ST\text{Long} = S_T - K \qquad\qquad \text{Short} = K - S_TLong=ST​−KShort=K−ST​

The difference between forwards and futures is not the payoff — it is the venue, the standardization, the counterparty and when the cash actually moves.

Summary​

  • A derivative takes its value from an underlying asset.
  • It trades either on an exchange (standardized, regulated, clearing house, liquid) or OTC (customized, lightly regulated, historically no clearing house, illiquid).
  • After 2007–08 a CCP was introduced into OTC markets, cutting default risk.
  • A future is a standardized, exchange-traded obligation, marked to market daily with a CCP in between.
  • A forward is a customized, OTC obligation, priced only at maturity, with higher counterparty risk and no margin.
  • Long payoff is ST−KS_T - KST​−K; short payoff is K−STK - S_TK−ST​. They are mirror images.

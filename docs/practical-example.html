Introduction to Derivative (Practical Example)

Recap: forwards and futures​

ForwardFuture
Customized agreement between 2 partiesStandardized agreement between 2 parties
Buy or sell the contract at a predetermined date and predetermined price (KKK, the strike)Buy or sell the underlying asset at a predetermined date and price
OTCExchange
Long position=ST−KShort position=K−ST=−(ST−K)\text{Long position} = S_T - K \qquad \text{Short position} = K - S_T = -(S_T - K)Long position=ST​−KShort position=K−ST​=−(ST​−K)

Long (buy) and short (sell) are exact opposites — see the forwards and futures note for the payoff diagrams.

Why do we use derivatives?​

Four reasons:

  1. Risk management (hedging)
  2. Speculation
  3. Arbitrage
  4. Leverage

1. Hedging — managing or mitigating risk​

The farmer again. He will have 1,000 kg of corn to sell in three months.

  • His risk: the price could decline by the time he harvests.
  • His action: sell a future contract now — 1,000 kg of corn at a strike of $2 per kg, three months from now.

At t=3t = 3t=3 the market price is $1/kg. But he is contractually selling at $2/kg. He has protected himself against exactly the fall he was worried about. The uncertainty is gone.

That is what hedging is: giving up the upside in order to remove the downside.

Note the structural point — with a forward or future, both parties are obligated. A buys at F0F_0F0​, B sells at F0F_0F0​, and neither can walk away. (An option is the instrument where the buyer is not obligated — that is the whole difference, and it is the subject of the second half of this note.)

2. Speculation​

Investors and traders use derivatives to speculate on the future price movement of the asset. This allows for potential profit if the price moves in their favour, though it also carries a high level of risk.

You are betting on a certain position. If XYZ moves from $50 to $100 and you had bought exposure for $25, the gain is large relative to what you put up — and the loss would have been just as sharp in the other direction.

The three types of trader​

TraderWhat they are doing
HedgersRisk management
SpeculatorsBetting on a certain position
ArbitrageursFinding price discrepancies

3. Arbitrage​

Price discrepancies between the underlying asset and the derivative — or for the same asset across venues.

Stock XYZ quotes at:

  • NYSE: $100 → buy here
  • ICE: $105 → sell here

That is a $5 arbitrage opportunity, locked in with no view on direction. The same thing happens across currencies and borders — XYZ at $100 on NYSE against £84 on the LSE.

An arbitrageur's job is to find those discrepancies. In an efficient market they close quickly, which is precisely because arbitrageurs act on them.

4. Leverage​

Derivatives allow an investor to gain a large exposure to an asset with a relatively small initial investment — known as leverage. This can amplify potential returns, but can increase the risk of losses.

With $2,000 to deploy:

  • Buying the stock directly might get you $1,500 of exposure.
  • Buying a derivative (option) on it could get you $7,000 of exposure.

The same move in the underlying is now worth several times more to you — in both directions. Leverage is not free money; it is a magnifier.

Worked hedging example — currency risk​

Question. A US-based company expects to receive €1 million in 3 months.

Risk: the Euro might weaken against the Dollar. If it does, that €1M converts into fewer dollars than it would today. The company's revenue is exposed to something that has nothing to do with its business.

Action: enter a forward contract to lock the exchange rate. That eliminates the uncertainty of the exchange-rate movement.

The same idea in numbers​

Today, 1 USD → Rs 84, and you hold 1,000 USD. Your worry is that the Rupee strengthens (so each dollar buys fewer rupees), say to 1 USD → Rs 80.

RateUSDRupees received
841,00084 × 1,000 = 84,000
801,00080 × 1,000 = 80,000

A Rs 4,000 swing purely from the exchange rate. Now lock it with a future/forward at 1 USD = Rs 83.5:

83.5×1,000=Rs 83,50083.5 \times 1{,}000 = \text{Rs } 83{,}50083.5×1,000=Rs 83,500

You have given up the gain if the rate moves to Rs 85, in exchange for certainty. That is the trade every hedger makes.

Options​

An option is a type of financial derivative which gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying asset at a predetermined price on a predetermined date.

That single phrase — right but not the obligation — is the entire difference from a forward or a future.

Why that matters​

You hold an option contract expiring at t=3t = 3t=3. At expiry you look at the market:

  • The deal is profitable → you exercise it and buy.
  • The deal is a loss → you simply do not execute the deal. You walk away.

With a forward or a future there is no concept of not executing. You are obligated; you execute the contract. With an option you have a choice.

Forward / FutureOption
At expiryObligation — must executeChoice — exercise or not

Key characteristics of an option contract​

a) Underlying asset — stocks, commodities, currencies, bonds, etc.

b) Strike price — the price at which the holder can buy or sell the underlying asset. This is the predetermined price, KKK.

c) Premium — the price paid by the buyer (long position) to the seller (short position). This is what you pay for the privilege of having a choice. It flows one way, at the start:

   Buyer of option  ──── premium ────→  Seller of option
     (long)                                (short)

d) Expiration date — the date at which the option must be exercised; otherwise it expires worthless.

e) Exercise style — see below.

Exercise style​

StyleWhen it can be exercised
American optionAt any point in time up to expiry
European optionOnly at maturity
Asian option(Path-dependent — payoff depends on an average price)

So for a 3-month option: an American option can be exercised at t=1t=1t=1, t=1.5t=1.5t=1.5, or anywhere along the way if it is profitable; a European option can only be exercised at t=3t=3t=3.

Types of option​

There are two: the call and the put.

Call option​

A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price KKK on a predetermined date.

Since you would only want the right to buy at a fixed price if you expect the price to rise, a call is a view that the price of the asset will go up.

PositionWhat you didWhat you holdExpectation
Long callBought the right to buyThe right to buy the underlyingPrice will go up
Short callSold the right to buyAn obligation to sell if exercisedPrice will decline

Put option​

A put option gives the holder the right to sell the underlying asset.

PositionWhat you didWhat you holdExpectation
Long putBought the right to sellThe right to sell the underlyingPrice will go down
Short putSold the right to sellAn obligation to buy if exercisedPrice will hold up

The general rule​

Call→right to buyPut→right to sell\textbf{Call} \rightarrow \text{right to \textbf{buy}} \qquad \textbf{Put} \rightarrow \text{right to \textbf{sell}}Call→right to buyPut→right to sell

and, cutting across both:

  • Long position → you hold the right to buy/sell the underlying asset.
  • Short position → you are obligated to buy/sell if the holder exercises.

The four positions​

Two option types × two directions = four positions. This table is the one worth memorising:

#PositionYou have
1Long callThe right to buy
2Short callThe obligation to sell
3Long putThe right to sell
4Short putThe obligation to buy

In every case the premium flows from the long to the short. The short side is being paid to take on an obligation; the long side is paying for a choice.

Summary​

  • Derivatives are used for hedging, speculation, arbitrage and leverage.
  • Hedging removes uncertainty by locking a price — the farmer locking $2/kg, the exporter locking an FX rate — at the cost of the upside.
  • The three trader types are hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs.
  • An option gives the holder a right, not an obligation — the one thing a forward or future never does.
  • An option is defined by five things: underlying asset, strike price, premium, expiration date, exercise style.
  • American = exercise any time; European = exercise only at maturity.
  • Call = right to buy; Put = right to sell. Long = holds the right; Short = carries the obligation, and receives the premium.

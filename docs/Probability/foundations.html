Probability Foundations

Probability is about determining the likelihood of an event. It's the native language of quantitative finance: a price isn't a number you predict, it's a random outcome you assign probabilities to. This page builds the ground floor — what a probability is, and the first rule for combining events: the addition rule for "or."

What is a probability?​

A probability is a number between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain) measuring how likely an event is. When every outcome is equally likely, it's just a ratio:

P(event)=number of favorable outcomestotal number of outcomesP(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}P(event)=total number of outcomesnumber of favorable outcomes​

The set of all possible outcomes is the sample space; an event is any subset of it.

  • Toss a coin — sample space {H,T}\{H, T\}{H,T}.   P(H)=12=50%\;P(H) = \tfrac{1}{2} = 50\%P(H)=21​=50%,   P(T)=12=50%\;P(T) = \tfrac{1}{2} = 50\%P(T)=21​=50%.
  • Roll a die — sample space {1,2,3,4,5,6}\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}{1,2,3,4,5,6}.   P(X=1)=16\;P(X=1) = \tfrac{1}{6}P(X=1)=61​.

A few rules follow immediately and are worth stating:

  • 0≤P(A)≤10 \le P(A) \le 10≤P(A)≤1 for any event AAA.
  • The probabilities of the whole sample space sum to 111.
  • Complement: P(not A)=1−P(A)P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)P(not A)=1−P(A) — the chance something doesn't happen.

What a probability means: four interpretations​

The ratio above is one interpretation of probability — but not the only one, and which you hold quietly shapes how you model. Four stances have competed for centuries:

  • Classical — probabilities come from symmetry: nnn equally likely outcomes each get 1/n1/n1/n, exactly the ratio above. Clean for dice and cards, but silent the moment outcomes are not symmetric (there is no "equally likely" split for a stock going up).
  • Frequency — a probability is the long-run relative frequency of an event over infinitely many repeatable trials. This is the interpretation behind classical (frequentist) statistics — and, as the Law of Large Numbers makes precise, that relative frequency really does converge to the probability. It strains, though, for one-shot events: there is no ensemble of this Tuesday's Fed decision.
  • Logical / epistemic — probability measures the degree to which evidence supports a proposition, aspiring to be as objective as logic.
  • Subjective (Bayesian) — a probability is a rational agent's degree of belief, calibrated so the bets you would accept stay consistent (no one can build a sure-loss Dutch book against you). It applies to any proposition, repeatable or not — which is why it fits markets, and why it is the foundation of Bayesian inference.

These are not merely philosophy: the frequency view underwrites confidence intervals and p-values, while the subjective view underwrites priors and posteriors. Most working quants are pragmatic — frequentist for a quick test, Bayesian when they must fold in prior knowledge.

Mutually exclusive events​

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur at the same time. On a single coin toss you get heads or tails, never both — HHH and TTT are mutually exclusive.

For mutually exclusive events, the addition rule is as simple as it looks: the chance of one or the other is the sum.

P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)
  • Coin: P(H or T)=12+12=1P(H \text{ or } T) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1P(H or T)=21​+21​=1 — one of them is certain to happen.
  • Die: P(1 or 5)=16+16=26=13P(1 \text{ or } 5) = \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{1}{3}P(1 or 5)=61​+61​=62​=31​.

Non-mutually-exclusive events: the general addition rule​

Most interesting events can happen together, and then simple addition double-counts the overlap. The classic example: draw one card from a 52-card deck.

  • Event KKK = "it's a King" — 4 cards, P(K)=452P(K) = \tfrac{4}{52}P(K)=524​.
  • Event ♡\heartsuit♡ = "it's a Heart" — 13 cards, P(♡)=1352P(\heartsuit) = \tfrac{13}{52}P(♡)=5213​.

These overlap: the King of Hearts is both. If you just add 452+1352\tfrac{4}{52} + \tfrac{13}{52}524​+5213​ you count that one card twice. The fix is to subtract the overlap once — the general addition rule (also called inclusion–exclusion):

P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B) P(K or ♡)=452+1352−152=1652=413P(K \text{ or } \heartsuit) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}P(K or ♡)=524​+5213​−521​=5216​=134​

3 + 1 + 12 = 16 distinct cards, so P(K or ♥) = 16/52. The 1 in the middle (the King of Hearts) is what you subtract to avoid double-counting.

Mutually exclusive events are just the special case where P(A and B)=0P(A \text{ and } B) = 0P(A and B)=0, and the overlap term vanishes — which is why the simpler rule above worked for coins and dice.

# P(King OR Heart) from a 52-card deck, via the addition rule
kings          = 4/52
hearts         = 13/52
king_of_hearts = 1/52          # the overlap: King AND Heart

naive   = kings + hearts                     # WRONG: double-counts the King of Hearts
correct = kings + hearts - king_of_hearts    # inclusion-exclusion

print(f"just adding:        {naive:.4f}  = {round(naive*52)}/52")
print(f"subtract overlap:   {correct:.4f}  = {round(correct*52)}/52  (correct)")

Why a quant cares​

Every question in markets is really about the probability of an event — P(up day)P(\text{up day})P(up day), P(price breaches a barrier)P(\text{price breaches a barrier})P(price breaches a barrier), P(default this year)P(\text{default this year})P(default this year). Two of these foundations matter more than they first appear:

  • The overlap term is where risk hides. You cannot get P(stock A falls OR stock B falls)P(\text{stock A falls OR stock B falls})P(stock A falls OR stock B falls) by adding the two probabilities — they overlap (both can fall together), and ignoring P(A and B)P(\text{A and B})P(A and B) overstates the chance of "at least one." How big that overlap is comes straight from how the two are related — the covariance and correlation from the Statistics section.
  • The complement rule is a constant shortcut. P(at least one loss)=1−P(no losses)P(\text{at least one loss}) = 1 - P(\text{no losses})P(at least one loss)=1−P(no losses) is almost always easier to compute than the "at least one" side directly.

Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

Where the addition rule handles "or," the multiplication rule handles "and" — the probability that two events both happen. And it forces the single most important question in all of probability: does one event affect the other?

Independent vs. dependent events​

  • Independent — two events are independent if they do not affect one another. Tossing a coin, then tossing it again: the first result tells you nothing about the second — P(H)=12P(H)=\tfrac{1}{2}P(H)=21​ on every toss, no matter what came before. Rolling a die twice is the same: P(1)=16P(1)=\tfrac{1}{6}P(1)=61​ on the first roll and P(2)=16P(2)=\tfrac{1}{6}P(2)=61​ on the next, each unaffected by the other.
  • Dependent — two events are dependent if one changes the probability of the other. Draw a card and keep it, then draw again: the deck has changed, so the second probability depends on the first.

The card case makes dependence concrete. Take a King from a full deck, then a Queen from what remains:

P(K)=452,P(Q after a King is removed)=451P(K) = \frac{4}{52}, \qquad P(Q \text{ after a King is removed}) = \frac{4}{51}P(K)=524​,P(Q after a King is removed)=514​

The denominator dropped from 52 to 51 — the first draw altered the second. That's dependence.

Conditional probability​

The tool for dependence is conditional probability — the probability of BBB given that AAA has already happened, written P(B∣A)P(B \mid A)P(B∣A):

P(B∣A)=P(A and B)P(A)P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)}P(B∣A)=P(A)P(A and B)​

In the card example, P(Q∣K)=451P(Q \mid K) = \tfrac{4}{51}P(Q∣K)=514​: given a King was drawn and set aside, all four Queens still sit in the remaining 51 cards.

The multiplication rule​

To get the probability that both events happen, multiply — with a twist depending on independence.

Independent events — just multiply the two probabilities:

P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B) P(H and then T)=12×12=14P(H \text{ and then } T) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}P(H and then T)=21​×21​=41​

Dependent events — multiply by the conditional probability:

P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B∣A)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B∣A) P(K and then Q)=452×451=162652=4663≈0.006P(K \text{ and then } Q) = \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{4}{51} = \frac{16}{2652} = \frac{4}{663} \approx 0.006P(K and then Q)=524​×514​=265216​=6634​≈0.006

Independence is simply the special case where P(B∣A)=P(B)P(B \mid A) = P(B)P(B∣A)=P(B) — knowing AAA tells you nothing, so the conditional collapses back to the plain multiplication.

from fractions import Fraction

# Independent: two separate coin tosses
p_h_and_t = Fraction(1,2) * Fraction(1,2)
print(f"P(H and T), independent:  {p_h_and_t}  = {float(p_h_and_t):.3f}")

# Dependent: King then Queen from a 52-card deck, no replacement
p_king              = Fraction(4,52)
p_queen_given_king  = Fraction(4,51)          # deck now has 51 cards; all 4 queens remain
p_k_then_q          = p_king * p_queen_given_king
print(f"P(K then Q), dependent:   {p_k_then_q}  = {float(p_k_then_q):.5f}")

Where this leads: Bayes' theorem​

The multiplication rule can be written from either event's point of view — P(A and B)P(A \text{ and } B)P(A and B) is the same thing whether you condition on AAA or on BBB:

P(A) P(B∣A)=P(A and B)=P(B) P(A∣B)P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)P(A)P(B∣A)=P(A and B)=P(B)P(A∣B)

Rearrange, and out falls Bayes' theorem — the rule for flipping a conditional and updating a belief when new evidence arrives:

P(A∣B)=P(B∣A) P(A)P(B)P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(B∣A)P(A)​

That one step is the engine of statistical inference. Its full, hands-on treatment — priors, posteriors, and a live prior→posterior lab — lives in Bayesian Inference.

Why a quant cares​

Independence versus dependence isn't academic — it's the difference between a survivable portfolio and a blown-up one:

  • The 2008 lesson. If mortgage defaults were independent, the multiplication rule makes many defaulting at once astronomically unlikely (multiply many small numbers → near zero). They weren't independent — they were highly dependent, driven by a common housing market. Models that assumed independence priced the joint risk near zero and were catastrophically wrong. Getting P(B∣A)P(B \mid A)P(B∣A) right is the risk model.
  • Conditional probability is how markets actually behave. P(big move tomorrow∣big move today)P(\text{big move tomorrow} \mid \text{big move today})P(big move tomorrow∣big move today) is well above the unconditional P(big move)P(\text{big move})P(big move) — that's volatility clustering, and it's why calm and turbulent periods come in runs.
  • Sampling without replacement — sequential draws, position sizing from a finite pool — is the dependent-events multiplication rule in disguise.

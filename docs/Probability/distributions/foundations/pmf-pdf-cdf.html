The Relationship Between PMF, PDF and CDF

A probability distribution function describes how probability is distributed over the values a random variable can take. Before we meet any named distribution — Bernoulli, Normal, Poisson — we need the three functions that describe every distribution, and how they fit together.

The split starts with the kind of random variable (an idea from the random variables lesson):

  • A discrete random variable takes separate, countable values — a die shows {1,2,3,4,5,6}\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}{1,2,3,4,5,6}, never 2.72.72.7. Its probabilities live in a probability mass function (PMF).
  • A continuous random variable takes any value in a range — an age is really {… }\{\dots\}{…}, a smear on the number line. Its probabilities live in a probability density function (PDF).

And both are summarized by a third function, the cumulative distribution function (CDF), which answers "what's the probability of landing at or below xxx?"

1. The PMF — for discrete variables​

For a discrete variable the PMF gives the probability of each individual value. The cleanest example is a fair die, X={1,2,3,4,5,6}X = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}X={1,2,3,4,5,6}, where every face is equally likely:

P(1)=P(2)=P(3)=P(4)=P(5)=P(6)=16P(1) = P(2) = P(3) = P(4) = P(5) = P(6) = \tfrac{1}{6}P(1)=P(2)=P(3)=P(4)=P(5)=P(6)=61​

Those six equal bars are the PMF. Turning on the cumulative overlay draws the CDF: a staircase that adds one 16\tfrac{1}{6}61​ step at each value until it reaches 111.

The CDF is just a running total of the PMF:

P(X≤2)=P(X=1)+P(X=2)=16+16=26=13P(X \le 2) = P(X=1) + P(X=2) = \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{1}{3}P(X≤2)=P(X=1)+P(X=2)=61​+61​=62​=31​ P(X≤6)=P(X=1)+P(X=2)+⋯+P(X=6)=1P(X \le 6) = P(X=1) + P(X=2) + \dots + P(X=6) = 1P(X≤6)=P(X=1)+P(X=2)+⋯+P(X=6)=1

The CDF must end at 111 — by the time you've included every outcome, the probability of "at or below the largest value" is certain.

2. The PDF — for continuous variables​

Now take a continuous variable — say the ages of shoppers on a website. You can't ask "what's the probability someone is exactly 55.000… years old?" — for a continuous variable that's zero, because a single point has no width. Instead the PDF gives probability density (a height), and probability itself is the area under the curve between two values.

Two facts define a PDF, and they're the only two properties you must remember:

1. Non-negativity: f(x)≥0 for all x\textbf{1. Non-negativity: } f(x) \ge 0 \text{ for all } x1. Non-negativity: f(x)≥0 for all x 2. Total area=1:∫−∞∞f(x) dx=1\textbf{2. Total area} = 1: \quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 12. Total area=1:∫−∞∞​f(x)dx=1

Density can't be negative, and all the probability together is exactly 111 — the continuous echo of "the PMF bars sum to 1." The shape of f(x)f(x)f(x) changes from one distribution to another (a symmetric bell, a right-skewed hump, a flat rectangle), but those two properties always hold.

The link: density is the slope of the CDF​

The CDF of a continuous variable is a smooth S-curve rising from 0 to 1. Reading it is direct — for an age distribution you might find

P(X≤40)=0.5=50%,P(X≤45)=0.8=80%P(X \le 40) = 0.5 = 50\%, \qquad P(X \le 45) = 0.8 = 80\%P(X≤40)=0.5=50%,P(X≤45)=0.8=80%

Here's the punchline that ties the two functions together. The height of the PDF at a point equals the steepness (gradient) of the CDF at that point. Where the CDF rises fastest, probability is piling up fastest, so the density is tallest. Measure the slope of the CDF between two nearby points and you've measured the density:

slope=0.65−0.4544−38=Δ(cumulative prob)Δx  ⇒  probability density\text{slope} = \frac{0.65 - 0.45}{44 - 38} = \frac{\Delta(\text{cumulative prob})}{\Delta x} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{probability density}slope=44−380.65−0.45​=ΔxΔ(cumulative prob)​⇒probability density   Probability density  =  gradient of the cumulative distribution function  \boxed{\;\text{Probability density} \;=\; \text{gradient of the cumulative distribution function}\;}Probability density=gradient of the cumulative distribution function​

Run the two views side by side — a flat density gives a straight-line CDF; a peaked density gives an S-curve that's steepest under the peak:

import numpy as np

# a small continuous sample; estimate PDF (histogram) and CDF (cumulative)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ages = rng.normal(40, 8, size=20_000)

# CDF read-offs, exactly like the lecture
for x in (40, 45):
    print(f"P(X <= {x}) = {(ages <= x).mean():.2f}")

# density ~ slope of the CDF between two nearby points
x1, x2 = 38, 44
c1, c2 = (ages <= x1).mean(), (ages <= x2).mean()
print(f"slope of CDF over [{x1},{x2}] = {(c2 - c1)/(x2 - x1):.4f}  (this is the density)")

Why a quant cares​

Everything downstream is one of these three functions:

  • Discrete payoffs — number of defaults in a bond portfolio, number of fills — are PMFs (Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, next module).
  • Continuous quantities — a return, a price, a P&L — are PDFs (Normal, Log-Normal, later).
  • Risk questions are CDF questions. "What's the probability of losing more than $1m?" is 1−CDF(−1m)1 - \text{CDF}(-1\text{m})1−CDF(−1m). Value-at-Risk is literally an inverse-CDF read-off: the loss at the 5th percentile. Master the CDF and you've master most of risk reporting.

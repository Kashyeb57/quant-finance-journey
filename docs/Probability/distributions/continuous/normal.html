Normal / Gaussian Distribution

The normal distribution (also called the Gaussian distribution) is a continuous probability distribution for a real-valued random variable. It's the famous bell curve — the single most important distribution in statistics and the one most models quietly assume.

Its defining features:

  1. It's a continuous random variable (a PDF), with X∈RX \in \mathbb{R}X∈R — X={… }X = \{\dots\}X={…} smeared across the whole number line.
  2. It's a symmetric distribution — a bell curve, the same on both sides of the center.
  3. Because it's symmetric and single-peaked, mean = median = mode all sit at the center.
  4. The spread is set by the variance: as σ2↑\sigma^2 \uparrowσ2↑, the spread ↑\uparrow↑ — the bell gets wider and flatter.

Examples: the weights of students in a class; the heights of students in a class; the four measurements in the classic IRIS dataset (petal length, sepal length, petal width, sepal width) — the kind of naturally-occurring measurements researchers find are approximately normal.

Notation, parameters, PDF​

X∼N(μ,σ2)X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)X∼N(μ,σ2)

The empirical rule: 68–95–99.7​

Because the shape is fixed, the probability within a given number of standard deviations of the mean is always the same, whatever μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ are. This is the empirical rule (turn on the bands in the explorer to see it):

P(μ−σ≤X≤μ+σ)≈68%(68.27%)P(\mu - \sigma \le X \le \mu + \sigma) \approx 68\% \quad(68.27\%)P(μ−σ≤X≤μ+σ)≈68%(68.27%) P(μ−2σ≤X≤μ+2σ)≈95%(95.45%)P(\mu - 2\sigma \le X \le \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 95\% \quad(95.45\%)P(μ−2σ≤X≤μ+2σ)≈95%(95.45%) P(μ−3σ≤X≤μ+3σ)≈99.7%(99.73%)P(\mu - 3\sigma \le X \le \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 99.7\% \quad(99.73\%)P(μ−3σ≤X≤μ+3σ)≈99.7%(99.73%)

So ~68% of the mass is within one σ, ~95% within two, ~99.7% within three. A move beyond 3σ should happen only about 0.3% of the time — which is exactly why, when markets produce them monthly, you learn something is wrong with the normal assumption.

A quick way to check whether data is actually normal is a QQ plot (quantile–quantile plot): plot the data's quantiles against a normal's. If the points fall on a straight line, the data is normal; systematic curls at the ends reveal skew or fat tails. We'll use QQ plots repeatedly in the heavy-tails module.

The bell in 3D​

The curve above is one variable. Put two independent normals together and their joint density becomes a 3D bell — a perfectly round hill whose height at each (X,Y)(X, Y)(X,Y) is how likely that combination is. Drag to rotate:

3DJoint density of two independent standard normals

Two independent normals give a round, symmetric hill. Introduce correlation and this hill stretches into a diagonal ridge — see the Correlation lesson to rotate that version.

Correlation reshapes this hill: at ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0 it's the round bell above; as ρ→±1\rho \to \pm 1ρ→±1 it collapses into a ridge along the diagonal — the geometry of two variables that move together. There's a rotatable ρ=0.8\rho = 0.8ρ=0.8 version on the Correlation page.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 1, size=1_000_000)   # standard normal

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    inside = np.mean(np.abs(x) <= k)
    print(f"within ±{k}σ: {inside*100:.2f}%")   # ≈ 68.27, 95.45, 99.73
runs in your browser

That round hill assumes the two variables are independent. When they co-move, the joint bell tilts and stretches along the direction they share — the multivariate Gaussian, parameterized by a covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ. It is covered alongside the covariance matrix on Covariance.

Why a quant cares​

The normal is the default assumption across finance — and both halves of that sentence matter:

  • Where it's relied on: returns models, the Black–Scholes option-pricing framework (log-returns normal), portfolio theory (mean–variance), confidence intervals, and Value-at-Risk all lean on normality because it's analytically clean — a whole model collapses to μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ.
  • Where it fails: real daily returns are not normal. They're more peaked in the middle (lots of tiny days) and have fatter tails (extreme days far more often than 68–95–99.7 allows). A "3-sigma" crash should be a once-a-century event under the normal; markets serve them up every few years. Assuming normality therefore understates crash risk — the motivation for the log-normal and power-law / Pareto models later in this module.

The normal is the map; fat tails are the territory. Know the map cold, then never forget it's only a map.

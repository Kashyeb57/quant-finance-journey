Standard Normal Distribution & Z-Score

The normal distribution comes in infinitely many versions — every (μ,σ)(\mu, \sigma)(μ,σ) gives a different bell. The standard normal distribution is the one special reference bell, with

μ=0,σ=1,X∼SND(μ=0, σ=1)\mu = 0, \qquad \sigma = 1, \qquad X \sim \text{SND}(\mu=0,\ \sigma=1)μ=0,σ=1,X∼SND(μ=0, σ=1)

and the z-score is the ruler that maps any normal onto it.

From a raw distribution to the standard normal​

Take a tiny dataset X={1,2,3,4,5}X = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}X={1,2,3,4,5}. Its mean is μ=3\mu = 3μ=3, and its (population) standard deviation is

σ=(1−3)2+(2−3)2+(3−3)2+(4−3)2+(5−3)25=105=2≈1.414\sigma = \sqrt{\tfrac{(1-3)^2+(2-3)^2+(3-3)^2+(4-3)^2+(5-3)^2}{5}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{10}{5}} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414σ=5(1−3)2+(2−3)2+(3−3)2+(4−3)2+(5−3)2​​=510​​=2​≈1.414

Standardizing means re-expressing each value as how many standard deviations it sits from the mean — the z-score:

  z=xi−μσ  \boxed{\;z = \frac{x_i - \mu}{\sigma}\;}z=σxi​−μ​​

The notes round σ≈1.414\sigma \approx 1.414σ≈1.414 to 111 to keep the arithmetic clean, which turns X={1,2,3,4,5}X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}X={1,2,3,4,5} into Y={−2,−1,0,1,2}Y = \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}Y={−2,−1,0,1,2}:

xix_ixi​z=(xi−3)/1z = (x_i - 3)/1z=(xi​−3)/1
1(1−3)/1=−2(1-3)/1 = -2(1−3)/1=−2
2(2−3)/1=−1(2-3)/1 = -1(2−3)/1=−1
3(3−3)/1=0(3-3)/1 = 0(3−3)/1=0
4(4−3)/1=1(4-3)/1 = 1(4−3)/1=1
5(5−3)/1=2(5-3)/1 = 2(5−3)/1=2

The transform slides the center to 0 and rescales the spread to 1 — the whole distribution moves onto the axis running −4⋯+4-4 \dots +4−4⋯+4. The shape doesn't change; only the labels on the axis do. (With the exact σ=2\sigma=\sqrt2σ=2​ the z-scores are {−1.41,−0.71,0,0.71,1.41}\{-1.41, -0.71, 0, 0.71, 1.41\}{−1.41,−0.71,0,0.71,1.41} — same idea, less tidy numbers.)

A z-score is unitless and universal: z=0z = 0z=0 is exactly average, z=+2z = +2z=+2 means "two standard deviations above the mean" whether the raw data was in dollars, kilograms, or basis points. Set μ=0, σ=1\mu=0,\ \sigma=1μ=0, σ=1 below and read the z of a / z of b fields as you move the bounds:

How many σ away? — the lecture's questions​

With a distribution centered at μ=4\mu = 4μ=4 and σ=1\sigma = 1σ=1:

Q. How many standard deviations is 4.25 away from the mean?

z=4.25−41=0.25z = \frac{4.25 - 4}{1} = 0.25z=14.25−4​=0.25

→ a quarter of a σ above the mean.

And for xi=2.5x_i = 2.5xi​=2.5:

z=2.5−41=−1.5z = \frac{2.5 - 4}{1} = -1.5z=12.5−4​=−1.5

→ one-and-a-half σ below the mean (the negative sign means below).

The sign tells you the side; the magnitude tells you how extreme.

Standardization — putting different units on one scale​

The real payoff is standardization for machine-learning models. Consider a dataset whose columns live in wildly different units:

Age (yrs)Weight (kg)Height (cms)Salary (INR)
247017540K
256016050K
265518060K
224013030K
303017520K
312518070K

Salary (tens of thousands) would completely swamp Age (twenties) in any distance calculation. Standardize each column with its own μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ — z=(xi−μage)/σz = (x_i - \mu_{\text{age}})/\sigmaz=(xi​−μage​)/σ, z=(xi−μweight)/σz = (x_i - \mu_{\text{weight}})/\sigmaz=(xi​−μweight​)/σ, and so on — and every feature is recentered to mean 0, spread 1, directly comparable.

This matters because a whole class of models is scale-sensitive:

  1. Clustering algorithms (k-means uses raw distances),
  2. Linear regression (coefficients and regularization depend on scale),
  3. Logistic regression (same),

all behave badly if one feature dominates purely because of its units. Standardization levels the playing field.

import numpy as np

# the lecture's tiny set
X = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
mu, sigma = X.mean(), X.std()           # population std -> sqrt(2)
print("μ =", mu, " σ =", round(sigma, 3))
print("z-scores:", np.round((X - mu) / sigma, 2))

# standardize a multi-unit table, column by column
data = np.array([
    [24, 70, 175, 40_000],
    [25, 60, 160, 50_000],
    [26, 55, 180, 60_000],
    [22, 40, 130, 30_000],
    [30, 30, 175, 20_000],
    [31, 25, 180, 70_000],
], dtype=float)

Z = (data - data.mean(axis=0)) / data.std(axis=0)   # per-column z-score
print("\nstandardized (each column now mean≈0, std≈1):")
print(np.round(Z, 2))
print("means:", np.round(Z.mean(axis=0), 6), " stds:", np.round(Z.std(axis=0), 3))

Why a quant cares​

  • Z-scores are the language of "how extreme." A −4σ day, a factor exposure of +2.5σ, a signal 3σ rich — quants talk in standard deviations precisely because they're comparable across assets and units.
  • Standardization is step zero of most ML pipelines — PCA, k-means, ridge/lasso, neural nets — and of factor models, where raw exposures are z-scored cross-sectionally before they can be combined.
  • The z-score is also the bridge to probability. Once a value is a z-score, the standard normal CDF turns it straight into a tail probability ("a z of −1.65 sits at the 5th percentile") — the mechanics behind Value-at-Risk and hypothesis tests.

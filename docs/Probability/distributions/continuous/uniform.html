Uniform Distribution

The uniform distribution is the picture of "every outcome is equally likely" — a flat shape with no favored region. It comes in two forms, matching the two kinds of random variable:

  1. Continuous Uniform Distribution — a PDF (continuous random variable).
  2. Discrete Uniform Distribution — a PMF (discrete random variable).

1. Continuous Uniform Distribution​

The continuous uniform distributions (or rectangular distributions) are a family of symmetric probability distributions. Such a distribution describes an experiment where there is an arbitrary outcome that lies between certain bounds. The bounds are the parameters aaa and bbb — the minimum and maximum values.

X∼U(a,b),−∞<a<b<∞X \sim U(a, b), \qquad -\infty < a < b < \inftyX∼U(a,b),−∞<a<b<∞

The density is constant on [a,b][a,b][a,b] and zero elsewhere — a rectangle of height 1/(b−a)1/(b-a)1/(b−a) (chosen so the area is exactly 1):

PDF: f(x)={1b−ax∈[a,b]0otherwiseCDF: F(x)={0x<ax−ab−ax∈[a,b]1x>b\text{PDF: } f(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{b-a} & x \in [a, b] \\[4pt] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad \text{CDF: } F(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x < a \\[2pt] \dfrac{x-a}{b-a} & x \in [a, b] \\[2pt] 1 & x > b \end{cases}PDF: f(x)=⎩⎨⎧​b−a1​0​x∈[a,b]otherwise​CDF: F(x)=⎩⎨⎧​0b−ax−a​1​x<ax∈[a,b]x>b​

The CDF rises as a straight line from 0 to 1 across [a,b][a,b][a,b] (using the maximum convention at the endpoints) — which is the PMF/PDF/CDF point made vivid: a constant density has a straight-line CDF, because the slope (the density) never changes.

2. Discrete Uniform Distribution​

The discrete uniform distribution is a symmetric probability distribution wherein a finite number of values are equally likely to be observed — every one of nnn values has equal probability 1/n1/n1/n. Put plainly: "a known, finite number of outcomes, all equally likely to happen."

X∼U(a,b),b≥a,PMF=1n,Mean=Median=a+b2X \sim U(a, b), \quad b \ge a, \qquad \text{PMF} = \frac{1}{n}, \qquad \text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \frac{a+b}{2}X∼U(a,b),b≥a,PMF=n1​,Mean=Median=2a+b​

The canonical example is rolling a fair die, {1,2,3,4,5,6}\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}{1,2,3,4,5,6}. The number of outcomes is

n=b−a+1=6−1+1=6,P(1)=P(2)=⋯=P(6)=16n = b - a + 1 = 6 - 1 + 1 = 6, \qquad P(1) = P(2) = \dots = P(6) = \frac{1}{6}n=b−a+1=6−1+1=6,P(1)=P(2)=⋯=P(6)=61​

Why a quant cares​

The uniform is the distribution of maximum ignorance — no outcome preferred over another — and that makes it foundational in two ways:

  • The raw fuel of Monte Carlo. Every simulation starts with uniform random numbers on [0,1][0,1][0,1]. Feed them through an inverse CDF and you can generate a sample from any distribution (inverse-transform sampling) — so pricing an exotic option by simulation ultimately rests on uniforms.
  • A "no-view" prior. In Bayesian terms, a uniform prior says "before seeing data, I favor no value over another" — the flat starting belief that data then reshapes (see Bayesian inference).
  • Discrete uniform = fair mechanisms — a die, a lottery draw, random tie-breaking, or picking a random row for a bootstrap sample.

Its flatness is also a null hypothesis: if outcomes should be equally likely but aren't, the departure from uniform is the signal.

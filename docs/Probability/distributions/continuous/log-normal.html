Log-Normal Distribution

A log-normal (or lognormal) distribution is a continuous probability distribution of a random variable whose logarithm is normally distributed. That one sentence is the whole idea:

If X∼Log-Normal(μ,σ),thenY=ln⁡(X)∼Normal\text{If } X \sim \text{Log-Normal}(\mu, \sigma), \quad\text{then}\quad Y = \ln(X) \sim \text{Normal}If X∼Log-Normal(μ,σ),thenY=ln(X)∼Normal

and equivalently, going the other way,

If Y∼Normal,thenX=eY∼Log-Normal\text{If } Y \sim \text{Normal}, \quad\text{then}\quad X = e^{Y} \sim \text{Log-Normal}If Y∼Normal,thenX=eY∼Log-Normal

Take a normal variable and exponentiate it and you get a log-normal; take a log-normal and apply the natural log (log⁡e\log_eloge​) and you get back a normal. The two are the same information in two coordinate systems.

The shape: right-skewed and positive-only​

Because X=eYX = e^{Y}X=eY and an exponential is always positive, a log-normal variable is strictly positive (X>0X > 0X>0) — it can never be negative. And it's right-skewed: a fat hump near the low end with a long tail stretching to the right. Increasing σ\sigmaσ (the spread of the underlying normal) makes the skew more extreme.

Let σ\sigmaσ sweep and plot the density over (σ,x)(\sigma, x)(σ,x). At small σ\sigmaσ it's almost a symmetric bell; as σ\sigmaσ grows the peak slides toward 0 and the right tail lengthens into a long ridge — the geometry of skew appearing. Drag to rotate:

Turning it back into a normal​

The defining relationship is also a tool: if data looks log-normal, take logs and it becomes normal — after which every normal-based method (z-scores, confidence intervals, the empirical rule) applies. A QQ plot of ln⁡(X)\ln(X)ln(X) against a normal should fall on a straight line. (The more general version of this trick — a Box–Cox transform — appears in the power-law lesson.)

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

Y = rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=200_000)   # underlying normal
X = np.exp(Y)                          # -> log-normal

def skew(v):                            # simple sample skewness
    z = (v - v.mean()) / v.std()
    return (z**3).mean()

print(f"X (log-normal):  skew = {skew(X):+.2f}  (right-skewed, > 0)")
print(f"ln(X) = Y:       skew = {skew(np.log(X)):+.2f}  (~0, back to normal)")
print(f"median e^μ = {np.exp(0):.2f}   <   mean e^(μ+σ²/2) = {np.exp(0 + 0.5**2/2):.2f}")

Where log-normals show up​

The lecture's examples are all quantities that are positive and right-skewed — a crowd near the low end and a few giants in the tail:

  1. Wealth distribution of the world — most people salaried near the peak, a long tail out to Bill Gates, Adani, Elon Musk.
  2. Discussion forums — the length of comments (many short, a few essays).
  3. Length of chess games (most games a typical length, some marathons).
  4. Dwell time on online articles (jokes, news) — most readers skim, a few linger.
  5. Salaries of employees in a company.

The common thread: a hard floor at zero, a typical range, and a rare-but-huge upper tail.

Why a quant cares​

The log-normal is the distribution of prices. It's not a side-note — it's the backbone of continuous-time finance:

  • Stock prices are modeled as log-normal. The Black–Scholes model assumes log-returns are normal, which makes the price log-normal. This is exactly right for two reasons: prices can't go negative (log-normal is positive-only), and returns compound multiplicatively — a price is a product of many small growth factors, and the log of a product is a sum, which the Central Limit Theorem pushes toward normal. So ln⁡(price)\ln(\text{price})ln(price) is normal ⇒ price is log-normal.
  • Geometric Brownian Motion, the standard price process, produces log-normally distributed prices at every horizon — see stochastic processes.
  • The median-vs-mean gap is a real trap. Because expected wealth eμ+σ2/2e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}eμ+σ2/2 exceeds the median eμe^{\mu}eμ, the "average" outcome of a volatile compounding strategy is pulled up by rare huge paths that a typical investor never experiences — the mathematics behind "average returns" flattering long-run reality.

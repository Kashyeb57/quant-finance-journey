Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is the reason statistics works at all. It rests on one concept — the sampling distribution — and delivers one astonishing result.

The idea: a sampling distribution​

A sampling distribution is the probability distribution of a statistic computed over a large number of samples taken from a population. Here the statistic is the mean. The procedure:

  1. Start with a population with mean μ\muμ and standard deviation σ\sigmaσ.
  2. Draw a sample of size nnn and compute its mean — call it xˉ1\bar{x}_1xˉ1​.
  3. Draw another sample of size nnn, get xˉ2\bar{x}_2xˉ2​. And another, xˉ3\bar{x}_3xˉ3​… up to xˉm\bar{x}_mxˉm​.
S1={x1,x2,…,xn}→xˉ1,S2={x2,x3,…,xn}→xˉ2,…,Sm→xˉmS_1 = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\} \to \bar{x}_1, \quad S_2 = \{x_2, x_3, \dots, x_n\} \to \bar{x}_2, \quad \dots, \quad S_m \to \bar{x}_mS1​={x1​,x2​,…,xn​}→xˉ1​,S2​={x2​,x3​,…,xn​}→xˉ2​,…,Sm​→xˉm​

Those sample means {xˉ1,xˉ2,…,xˉm}\{\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2, \dots, \bar{x}_m\}{xˉ1​,xˉ2​,…,xˉm​} form a distribution of their own — the sampling distribution of the mean, centered on μxˉ\mu_{\bar{x}}μxˉ​.

The theorem​

The sampling distribution of the mean will always be normally distributed, as long as the sample size is large enough — regardless of whether the population is normal, Poisson, binomial, or any other distribution.

That's the magic. It doesn't matter what shape you start from:

  • ① If the population is normal, X∼N(μ,σ)X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)X∼N(μ,σ) — the sample means are normal too (no surprise).
  • ② If the population is not normal — skewed, bimodal, exponential, anything — the sample means still pile up into a normal, provided the sample size is large enough. The rule of thumb is:
 n≥30  ⇒  sampling distribution of the mean is≈Normal \boxed{\,n \ge 30 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{sampling distribution of the mean is} \approx \text{Normal}\,}n≥30⇒sampling distribution of the mean is≈Normal​

The result in a formula​

The sampling distribution of the mean isn't just normal — we know its exact parameters. Starting from a normal population X∼N(μ,σ)X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)X∼N(μ,σ):

Xˉ∼N ⁣(μ,  σn)\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)Xˉ∼N(μ,n​σ​)

where σ\sigmaσ = population standard deviation, μ\muμ = population mean, nnn = sample size. Two facts fall out:

  1. The mean of the sample means is the population mean, μxˉ=μ\mu_{\bar{x}} = \muμxˉ​=μ — averaging is unbiased.
  2. The spread shrinks as n\sqrt{n}n​. The standard deviation of the sample mean — called the standard error — is σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​. More data ⇒ a tighter estimate, but only at the rate n\sqrt{n}n​: to halve your error you need four times the data.

The standard error in 3D​

Here is Xˉ∼N(μ,σ/n)\bar{X} \sim N(\mu, \sigma/\sqrt{n})Xˉ∼N(μ,σ/n​) drawn over both nnn and xˉ\bar{x}xˉ. As nnn grows the bell climbs and narrows into a sharp ridge over μ\muμ — the standard error σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​ collapsing. Drag to rotate:

3DSampling distribution of the mean tightens as n grows

With σ = 1 and μ = 0: at n = 1 the curve is a wide standard normal; by n = 30 it is a tall narrow spike of width σ/√n ≈ 0.18. That narrowing is the √n law of precision.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# a strongly NON-normal population: exponential (right-skewed)
mu, sigma = 1.0, 1.0                       # exponential(1): mean=1, std=1
n, m = 30, 20_000
means = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(m, n)).mean(axis=1)   # m sample means

print(f"population: mean={mu}, std={sigma}  (very skewed)")
print(f"sample means: mean={means.mean():.3f}  (≈ μ)")
print(f"std of sample means = {means.std():.3f}")
print(f"theory σ/√n         = {sigma/np.sqrt(n):.3f}")   # they match

Why a quant cares​

The CLT is the licence to use the normal even when the data isn't normal — and it quietly underwrites most of finance:

  • Averages are trustworthy; single observations aren't. One day's return is drawn from a fat-tailed mess. The average of many is approximately normal, so its uncertainty is governed by σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​. This is why you estimate an expected return or a Sharpe ratio from a long track record, not one trade.
  • The n\sqrt{n}n​ law is everywhere. The standard error of a mean, the precision of a Monte Carlo price (error shrinks like 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​), the √252 volatility annualization — all the same theorem. It's also why more data helps, but with diminishing returns.
  • Diversification. A portfolio of many roughly-independent bets has a P&L that's the average of the individual ones — pushed toward normal and with spread cut by n\sqrt{n}n​. (The catch, as always: the CLT assumes independence. Correlated bets don't diversify, and their tails stay fat — the power-law world.)

The CLT is also the bridge to the next lesson: because xˉ\bar{x}xˉ is normal with a known standard error, we can turn a sample mean into an estimate — a point, and an interval around it.

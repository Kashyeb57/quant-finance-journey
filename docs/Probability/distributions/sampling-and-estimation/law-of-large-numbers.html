Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers (LLN) is the theorem that makes averages trustworthy: as you collect more independent observations, their sample average converges to the true mean μ\muμ. It is why a casino profits with near-certainty over millions of bets while any single spin is a coin-flip — and why a Monte Carlo estimate creeps closer to the right answer the longer you run it.

The statement​

Take independent draws x1,x2,…,xnx_1, x_2, \dots, x_nx1​,x2​,…,xn​ from a distribution with mean μ\muμ, and form the running average

xˉn=1n∑i=1nxi.\bar{x}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i .xˉn​=n1​i=1∑n​xi​.

The LLN says that as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞, xˉn→μ\bar{x}_n \to \muxˉn​→μ. It comes in two strengths:

  • Weak law — for any tolerance ε\varepsilonε, the probability that the average is more than ε\varepsilonε from μ\muμ goes to zero: P(∣xˉn−μ∣>ε)→0P(|\bar{x}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \to 0P(∣xˉn​−μ∣>ε)→0. (Convergence in probability.)
  • Strong law — the running average itself settles onto μ\muμ with probability 1: P(lim⁡n→∞xˉn=μ)=1P(\lim_{n\to\infty} \bar{x}_n = \mu) = 1P(limn→∞​xˉn​=μ)=1. (Almost-sure convergence — the stronger "it will actually lock on" guarantee.)

For everyday purposes they say the same practical thing: average enough independent draws and you land on the true mean.

See it happen​

Watch a running average of fair dice rolls (true mean μ=3.5\mu = 3.5μ=3.5) settle as the sample grows — jumpy at first, then pinned:

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000)          # fair six-sided die, μ = 3.5
running_avg = np.cumsum(rolls) / np.arange(1, len(rolls) + 1)

for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    print(f"n = {n:>7,}:  running average = {running_avg[n-1]:.4f}   (true μ = 3.5)")
runs in your browser

Early on the average swings widely; by n=100,000n = 100{,}000n=100,000 it sits within a whisker of 3.5. Notice it does not snap to 3.5 and freeze — it wanders in, with the size of the wandering shrinking like 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​.

LLN is not the CLT​

These two theorems are constantly confused. They answer different questions about the same xˉn\bar{x}_nxˉn​:

  • LLN — where it goes. The average converges to μ\muμ. A statement about the center.
  • Central Limit Theorem — how it's spread. For finite nnn, the average is approximately N(μ, σ/n)N(\mu,\, \sigma/\sqrt{n})N(μ,σ/n​). A statement about the shape and the error.

The LLN promises you arrive; the CLT tells you how far off you still are after nnn draws (the standard error σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​). Both share one fine-print assumption: a finite mean and independence. In fat-tailed markets a mean can be effectively unstable and bets can be correlated — which is exactly when "just average more" quietly fails.

Why a quant cares​

  • Monte Carlo pricing is the LLN. Estimating an option price as the average payoff over simulated paths works because that average converges to the true expectation; the CLT then sizes the error (∝1/n\propto 1/\sqrt{n}∝1/n​).
  • Edge only shows up in the long run. A strategy with a small positive expectancy is indistinguishable from noise over ten trades and near-certain over ten thousand — provided the trades are roughly independent. Position sizing exists to keep you in the game long enough for the LLN to pay out.
  • The gambler's fallacy is the LLN misread. Convergence of the average does not mean the next draw "corrects" a losing streak — the dice have no memory. The average is dragged toward μ\muμ by accumulating fresh draws that dilute early deviations, not by reversals.

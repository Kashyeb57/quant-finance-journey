Binomial Distribution

Take a Bernoulli trial and repeat it nnn times, counting how many successes you get. That count is the Binomial distribution.

Formally: the binomial distribution with parameters nnn and ppp is the discrete probability distribution of the number of successes in a sequence of nnn independent experiments, each asking a yes–no question, each with its own Boolean outcome — success (probability ppp) or failure (probability q=1−pq = 1-pq=1−p). A single success/failure experiment is a Bernoulli trial; a sequence of them is a Bernoulli process. For a single trial (n=1n = 1n=1) the binomial is the Bernoulli distribution. The binomial is also the basis for the binomial test of statistical significance.

Its three assumptions:

  1. It's a discrete random variable (a PMF).
  2. Every outcome of the experiment is binary (success/failure).
  3. The experiment is performed for nnn trials.

Example: tossing a coin 10 times, each toss {H,T}\{H, T\}{H,T} — here n=10n = 10n=10 and "success" = heads.

Notation, parameters, support​

X∼B(n,p)X \sim B(n, p)X∼B(n,p)
  • Parameters: n∈{0,1,2,… }n \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}n∈{0,1,2,…} = number of trials/experiments;   p∈[0,1]\;p \in [0, 1]p∈[0,1] = success probability for each trial;   q=1−p\;q = 1 - pq=1−p.
  • Support: K∈{0,1,2,3,…,n}K \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots, n\}K∈{0,1,2,3,…,n} = the number of successes (you can get anywhere from 0 to all nnn).

The PMF​

P(K;n,p)=(nK) pK(1−p) n−K,K=0,1,2,…,nP(K; n, p) = \binom{n}{K}\, p^{K} (1-p)^{\,n-K}, \qquad K = 0, 1, 2, \dots, nP(K;n,p)=(Kn​)pK(1−p)n−K,K=0,1,2,…,n

(nK)=nCK=n!K! (n−K)!\binom{n}{K} = {}^{n}C_{K} = \frac{n!}{K!\,(n-K)!}(Kn​)=nCK​=K!(n−K)!n!​

Mean and variance​

Because it's nnn independent Bernoullis stacked up, the mean and variance are just nnn times the Bernoulli's:

Mean=np,Variance=npq,σ=npq\text{Mean} = n p, \qquad \text{Variance} = n p q, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{n p q}Mean=np,Variance=npq,σ=npq​

Worked example 1 — exactly 3 heads in 5 flips​

Coin flip: n=5n = 5n=5 trials, p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5, and KKK (number of heads) varies from 0 to 5. What is the probability of getting exactly 3 heads in 5 flips? Here n=5n=5n=5, K=3K=3K=3:

P(X=3)=(53)(0.5)3(1−0.5)5−3=10×0.125×0.25=0.3125P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3}(0.5)^{3}(1 - 0.5)^{5-3} = 10 \times 0.125 \times 0.25 = 0.3125P(X=3)=(35​)(0.5)3(1−0.5)5−3=10×0.125×0.25=0.3125

Worked example 2 — quality control​

Scenario: inspecting 10 items in a factory where each item has a 10% chance of being defective. So n=10n = 10n=10 trials, p=0.1p = 0.1p=0.1 (a defective item is a "success" for counting purposes), and KKK varies from 0 to 10. What is the probability of finding exactly 2 defective items in a sample of 10?

P(X=2)=(102)(0.1)2(1−0.1)10−2=45×0.01×0.98≈0.1937=19.37%P(X = 2) = \binom{10}{2}(0.1)^{2}(1 - 0.1)^{10-2} = 45 \times 0.01 \times 0.9^{8} \approx 0.1937 = 19.37\%P(X=2)=(210​)(0.1)2(1−0.1)10−2=45×0.01×0.98≈0.1937=19.37%

from math import comb

def binom_pmf(k, n, p):
    return comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)

# Example 1: exactly 3 heads in 5 fair flips
print("P(3 heads in 5) =", round(binom_pmf(3, 5, 0.5), 4))      # 0.3125

# Example 2: exactly 2 defectives in 10, each 10% defective
print("P(2 defective in 10) =", round(binom_pmf(2, 10, 0.1), 4))  # 0.1937

# mean and variance
n, p = 10, 0.1

Seeing the Binomial grow — in 3D​

Fix p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5 and let the number of trials nnn climb. The 3D surface below plots P(X=k)P(X=k)P(X=k) over both nnn (trials) and kkk (successes). Watch the ridge march outward (its peak tracks k=n/2k = n/2k=n/2) and spread and flatten as nnn grows — drag to rotate:

3DBinomial PMF as n grows (p = 0.5)

Each slice at a fixed n is one Binomial PMF. As n increases the hump widens and smooths into a bell — a visual preview of the Central Limit Theorem, which says this shape becomes Normal.

That smoothing-into-a-bell is not a coincidence — it's the Central Limit Theorem arriving early. A binomial with large nnn is well approximated by a Normal with mean npnpnp and variance npqnpqnpq.

Why a quant cares​

The binomial is the natural model whenever you count successes over repeated independent bets:

  • Win rate over a trading month. A strategy with edge ppp over nnn trades: the number of winners is B(n,p)B(n, p)B(n,p), with expected npnpnp winners and spread npq\sqrt{npq}npq​. That spread is why a good strategy can still have a losing month — and why you need enough nnn to tell skill from luck.
  • Defaults in a small loan book where each borrower independently defaults with probability ppp.
  • The binomial options-pricing model builds a price tree from exactly this: up/down moves as repeated Bernoulli steps.

The critical caveat is the independence assumption. Real trades and real defaults are often correlated — and when they are, the true spread is far wider than npq\sqrt{npq}npq​ suggests. (That failure of independence is the heart of the conditional-probability lesson and the 2008 story.)

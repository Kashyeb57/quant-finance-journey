Bernoulli Distribution

The Bernoulli distribution is the simplest discrete probability distribution. It describes a random variable with exactly two possible outcomes: success (with probability ppp) and failure (with probability 1−p1-p1−p). It's the model for any binary outcome — a coin flip, a yes/no question, up-day/down-day, default/no-default.

Two facts define it:

  1. It's a discrete random variable (so it has a PMF).
  2. Its outcomes are binary — exactly two of them.

The outcomes, coded 0 and 1​

We label the two outcomes with K∈{0,1}K \in \{0, 1\}K∈{0,1}:

  • Success ⇒K=1\Rightarrow K = 1⇒K=1, with probability ppp
  • Failure ⇒K=0\Rightarrow K = 0⇒K=0, with probability q=1−pq = 1 - pq=1−p

Examples:

  • Tossing a coin, {H,T}\{H, T\}{H,T}:   P(X=H)=0.5=p\;P(X=H) = 0.5 = pP(X=H)=0.5=p, and P(X=T)=1−0.5=0.5=qP(X=T) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5 = qP(X=T)=1−0.5=0.5=q.
  • Will a person pass or fail?   P(X=Pass)=0.4\;P(X=\text{Pass}) = 0.4P(X=Pass)=0.4, so P(X=Fail)=1−0.4=0.6P(X=\text{Fail}) = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6P(X=Fail)=1−0.4=0.6.

Notice ppp need not be 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ — a Bernoulli variable can be lopsided. Three different Bernoullis:

There's a single formula that produces both bars:

PMF=pK (1−p) 1−K\text{PMF} = p^{K} \,(1-p)^{\,1-K}PMF=pK(1−p)1−K

It's a clever switch. Plug in each value of KKK and one factor collapses to 1:

P(K=1)=p1(1−p)1−1=p1(1−p)0=pP(K=1) = p^{1}(1-p)^{1-1} = p^{1}(1-p)^{0} = pP(K=1)=p1(1−p)1−1=p1(1−p)0=p P(K=0)=p0(1−p)1−0=1⋅(1−p)1=1−p=qP(K=0) = p^{0}(1-p)^{1-0} = 1 \cdot (1-p)^{1} = 1 - p = qP(K=0)=p0(1−p)1−0=1⋅(1−p)1=1−p=q

So the compact formula is just shorthand for the two-line table:

Mean, median, mode, variance​

Mean — the expected value is the probability-weighted sum over K∈{0,1}K \in \{0,1\}K∈{0,1}:

E[X]=∑k=01k⋅P(k)=0×0.40+1×0.60=0.60=pE[X] = \sum_{k=0}^{1} k \cdot P(k) = 0 \times 0.40 + 1 \times 0.60 = 0.60 = pE[X]=k=0∑1​k⋅P(k)=0×0.40+1×0.60=0.60=p

The mean of a Bernoulli is just ppp. (With p=0.6, q=0.4p=0.6,\ q=0.4p=0.6, q=0.4: P(1)=0.6P(1)=0.6P(1)=0.6, P(0)=0.4P(0)=0.4P(0)=0.4.)

Mode — the more likely outcome: if p>qp > qp>q then ppp (the value 1) is the mode; otherwise qqq (the value 0) is the mode.

Variance — with P(K=0)=0.4=qP(K{=}0)=0.4=qP(K=0)=0.4=q and P(K=1)=0.6=pP(K{=}1)=0.6=pP(K=1)=0.6=p, and mean 0.60.60.6:

σ2=0.40×(0−0.6)2+0.6×(1−0.6)2=0.40(0.36)+0.6(0.16)=0.24\sigma^2 = 0.40 \times (0 - 0.6)^2 + 0.6 \times (1 - 0.6)^2 = 0.40(0.36) + 0.6(0.16) = 0.24σ2=0.40×(0−0.6)2+0.6×(1−0.6)2=0.40(0.36)+0.6(0.16)=0.24

And 0.24=0.6×0.4=p×q0.24 = 0.6 \times 0.4 = p \times q0.24=0.6×0.4=p×q. That's no coincidence — for every Bernoulli:

  σ2=pqσ=pq  \boxed{\;\sigma^2 = pq \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{pq}\;}σ2=pqσ=pq​​

Variance is largest at p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5 (a fair coin is maximally uncertain: σ2=0.25\sigma^2 = 0.25σ2=0.25) and shrinks to 0 as p→0p \to 0p→0 or p→1p \to 1p→1 (a near-certain outcome carries almost no uncertainty). Watch σ2\sigma^2σ2 peak as you slide the lab to p=0.5p=0.5p=0.5.

def bernoulli(p):
    mean = p
    var  = p * (1 - p)          # = p q
    return mean, var

for p in (0.5, 0.6, 0.9):
    m, v = bernoulli(p)
    print(f"p={p}:  mean={m:.2f}  variance=pq={v:.2f}  std={v**0.5:.3f}")

# verify the mean formula from the definition, E[X] = 0*q + 1*p
p = 0.6
print("E[X] =", 0*(1-p) + 1*p)          # -> 0.6
print("Var  =", (1-p)*(0-p)**2 + p*(1-p)**2)  # -> 0.24 = pq

Why a quant cares​

The Bernoulli is the atom of every binary bet. Strip any single-shot market event down and it's a Bernoulli trial:

  • an up day vs. down day — ppp is your directional edge; a no-edge coin-flip strategy is p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5.
  • a bond that defaults or doesn't this year — ppp is the default probability.
  • an option that expires in- or out-of-the-money — ppp is P(ITM)P(\text{ITM})P(ITM).
  • an order that fills or doesn't at your limit.

The whole game is estimating ppp and knowing your uncertainty pq\sqrt{pq}pq​. And the moment you repeat the same trial nnn times and count the successes, you've built the Binomial distribution — the next lesson, and literally "Bernoulli, nnn times."

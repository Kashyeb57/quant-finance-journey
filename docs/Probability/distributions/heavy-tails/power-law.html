Power Law Distribution

A power law is a functional relationship between two quantities where a relative change in one produces a proportional relative change in the other — independent of the initial size of those quantities. In one line: one quantity varies as a power of another,

y  ∝  x−αy \;\propto\; x^{-\alpha}y∝x−α

That "independent of the initial size" clause is the deep part: it means the law looks the same at every scale (double xxx and yyy changes by the same factor whether you started small or large). This scale invariance is what produces the signature shape — a few outcomes that utterly dominate, and a long tail of many small ones.

The 80–20 rule and the long tail​

The everyday face of a power law is the 80–20 rule: roughly 80% of the effect comes from 20% of the causes. A handful of items on the left dominate; the rest trail off into a long, thin tail on the right.

The lecture's examples span sports, economics, and engineering:

  1. IPL / sports: ~20% of the team is responsible for winning ~80% of the matches.
  2. Wealth: ~80% of the wealth is held by ~20% of the population.
  3. Oil: ~80% of the total oil is held by ~20% of the nations.
  4. Language: the frequencies of words in most languages (a few words like "the" dominate — Zipf's law).
  5. Software: fixing ~20% of the major defects removes ~80% of the upcoming defects in a product.

The tell-tale sign: straight on log-log axes​

A power law y=c x−αy = c\,x^{-\alpha}y=cx−α becomes a straight line when you plot ln⁡y\ln ylny against ln⁡x\ln xlnx (slope −α-\alpha−α). That's the practical test: histogram your data on log-log axes, and if it's a straight line, it's a power law — not a bell.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# a power-law sample (Pareto): check the 80-20 concentration
x = rng.pareto(a=1.16, size=100_000) + 1     # a≈1.16 gives roughly 80-20
x.sort()
top20_share = x[int(0.8*len(x)):].sum() / x.sum()
print(f"share of total held by the top 20%: {top20_share*100:.0f}%")   # ~80%

# power law => straight line on log-log axes
vals, edges = np.histogram(x, bins=np.logspace(0, 2, 30), density=True)
centers = np.sqrt(edges[:-1]*edges[1:])
mask = vals > 0
slope = np.polyfit(np.log(centers[mask]), np.log(vals[mask]), 1)[0]
print(f"slope on log-log axes ≈ {slope:.2f}  (a straight line => power law)")

Taming a power law: transform back to normal​

Power-law data breaks every normal-based tool (its mean and variance can be unstable or even infinite). The fix is a transformation that pulls the long tail back in:

Power law  →  ln⁡(x)    closer to Normal,Power law  →Box–Cox  Normal  ⇒  QQ plot\text{Power law} \;\xrightarrow{\;\ln(x)\;}\; \text{closer to Normal}, \qquad \text{Power law} \;\xrightarrow{\text{Box–Cox}}\; \text{Normal} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{QQ plot}Power lawln(x)​closer to Normal,Power lawBox–Cox​Normal⇒QQ plot

Taking logs (or the more general Box–Cox transform) compresses the tail so a QQ plot straightens out — after which normal methods can be applied to the transformed data. The named probability distribution that formalizes the power law is the Pareto distribution (the "80–20" distribution), the next lesson.

Why a quant cares​

Finance is a power-law world wearing a normal-shaped costume:

  • Market cap, city sizes, firm sizes, trading volume are power-law distributed — a few mega-caps dominate an index, exactly as a few words dominate a language.
  • The size of market moves has power-law tails. Crash magnitudes don't decay like a thin normal tail; they follow a power law, so "impossible" 10-sigma days keep happening. This is the heart of Mandelbrot's and Taleb's critique of Gaussian finance — the black swan lives in the power-law tail.
  • Concentration risk. If 20% of your positions drive 80% of your P&L (or your risk), diversification is an illusion — the power law says the tail is the portfolio.

Assuming normality where a power law rules is the single most expensive mistake in quantitative risk. Recognizing the straight line on log-log axes is how you catch it.

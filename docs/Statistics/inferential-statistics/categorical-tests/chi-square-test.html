What Is a Chi-Square Test?

Every test so far — z, t — was about a mean. But a lot of data isn't numeric at all: bike colours, yes/no, left/right-handed, product categories. For that we need a different tool — the chi-square test, often written the chi-square test for goodness of fit.

What it is​

The chi-square test for goodness of fit makes claims about population proportions. The moment you hear "proportion," think categorical variable — you're counting how many fall into each bucket, not averaging a measurement.

Formally, the chi-square test is a non-parametric test that is performed on categorical data — both ordinal and nominal. ("Non-parametric" means it makes no assumption that the data follow a normal distribution; it works directly on category counts.)

The idea: theory vs observed​

Two examples make it click. Start with bikes.

There is a population of males who like different-colour bikes. A theory about the population says each colour is equally popular — 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ like yellow, 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ like red, 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​ like orange. Someone then collects a sample and finds 22 like yellow, 17 like red, and 59 like orange.

Two distributions are now sitting side by side, and they have names:

  • The theory (13,13,13\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac1331​,31​,31​) is the theory categorical distribution — what the claim predicts.
  • The sample (22, 17, 59) is the observed categorical distribution — what actually happened.

These two — theory and observed — are the only ingredients the chi-square test uses. The whole job of the goodness-of-fit test is to answer one question:

Does the observed sample information support the theory?

If the sample proportions are close to 13,13,13\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac1331​,31​,31​, the theory fits; if they're lopsided like 22/17/59, maybe it doesn't. Drag the observed counts and watch how far the solid bars pull away from the dashed "theory" bars — that gap is exactly what chi-square will measure:

Goodness of fit asks one thing: how well do the observed (solid) bars match the expected (dashed) bars the theory predicts? Drag an observed count away from its expected and the gap is what the χ² test will measure next.

Setting up a goodness-of-fit problem​

Here's the problem we'll actually solve in the next lesson — for now, just the setup, because getting the Observed (O) and Expected (E) columns right is the whole skill.

In a science class of 75 students, 11 are left-handed. Does this class fit the theory that 12% of people are left-handed?

There are two categories — left-handed and right-handed — so we build an O and an E for each.

Observed (O) comes straight from the sample. 11 are left-handed, so the remaining 75−11=6475 - 11 = 6475−11=64 are right-handed.

Expected (E) comes from the theory. "12% of people are left-handed," applied to 75 students:

Eleft=12100×75=9,Eright=75−9=66E_{\text{left}} = \frac{12}{100}\times 75 = 9, \qquad E_{\text{right}} = 75 - 9 = 66Eleft​=10012​×75=9,Eright​=75−9=66

Notice both columns sum to 75 — the expected counts just redistribute the same 75 people according to the theory. Now the test compares the two columns: are the observed counts a good fit for the expected ones, or too far off to be chance?

There is a formula that turns that comparison into a single number and a p-value — the χ2\chi^2χ2 statistic — but we'll work it, with a full decision and the Python, in the next lesson, the goodness-of-fit test. For now the takeaway is the framing: a chi-square test compares two categorical variables, expressed as a theory (expected) distribution and an observed distribution.

Why a quant cares​

Markets are full of categorical claims that a chi-square test is built to check:

  • "Is this die/model/RNG fair?" Whether it's simulated returns landing in expected buckets, or trade outcomes across regimes, goodness of fit tests whether an observed frequency distribution matches the one your model assumes.
  • Distributional assumptions are testable, not sacred. A risk model that assumes returns fall into certain quantile bins can be checked: bin the realized returns (Observed) against the model's predicted counts (Expected) and let chi-square flag the misfit — a formal version of "the tails are fatter than the model says."
  • It handles the data means can't. Sector, rating, signal-bucket, win/loss — categorical outcomes with no meaningful average. Chi-square is the hypothesis test for counts and proportions, which is a huge share of real trading data.

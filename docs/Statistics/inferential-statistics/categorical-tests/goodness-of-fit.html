Chi-Square for Goodness of Fit — a Worked Problem

Now we actually run the chi-square test from the last lesson — a full hypothesis test, step by step, using the chi-square table.

The problem​

In the 2010 census of a small city, the weights of individuals were distributed as:

Weight<50 kg50–75>75
Proportion20%30%50%

In 2020, the weights of n = 500 individuals were sampled, with these results:

Weight<5050–75>75
Count140160200

Using α = 0.05, would you conclude the population distribution of weights has changed in the last 10 years?

There are three categories, so chi-square is the right tool. The 2010 percentages are the theory — the expected distribution; the 2020 sample is the observed distribution.

Build the expected counts​

The 2010 proportions still hold under the null, but we need them as counts out of the 2020 sample of 500 — so multiply each proportion by 500:

E<50=0.2×500=100,E50–75=0.3×500=150,E>75=0.5×500=250E_{<50} = 0.2\times 500 = 100, \qquad E_{50\text{–}75} = 0.3\times 500 = 150, \qquad E_{>75} = 0.5\times 500 = 250E<50​=0.2×500=100,E50–75​=0.3×500=150,E>75​=0.5×500=250

The five steps​

① Hypotheses.

H0: the data meets the expectationH1: the data does *not* meet the expectationH_0:\ \text{the data meets the expectation} \qquad H_1:\ \text{the data does *not* meet the expectation}H0​: the data meets the expectationH1​: the data does *not* meet the expectation

② Significance level. α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, so C.I.=95%\text{C.I.} = 95\%C.I.=95%.

③ Degrees of freedom. For goodness of fit, df is the number of categories minus one:

df=k−1=3−1=2\text{df} = k - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2df=k−1=3−1=2

④ Decision boundary. Here's what's different from the z/t tests: those used a symmetric bell, but the chi-square distribution is not symmetric — it's right-skewed and can't go negative. So the test is one-tailed: the entire rejection region (area α\alphaα) sits in the right tail. We find one critical value from the χ² table using df and α\alphaα, and reject H0H_0H0​ if the statistic lands beyond it.

Chi-square is not symmetric — it's right-skewed and non-negative, so the test is one-tailed: the whole rejection region (area α) is the right tail. Look the critical value up in the χ² table by df and α (df = 2, α = 0.05 → 5.991), then reject H₀ if the observed χ² lands past it.

if χ2>5.991⇒reject H0,else⇒fail to reject H0.\text{if } \chi^2 > 5.991 \Rightarrow \textbf{reject } H_0, \qquad \text{else} \Rightarrow \textbf{fail to reject } H_0.if χ2>5.991⇒reject H0​,else⇒fail to reject H0​.

χ² adds up the squared gaps (O−E)²/E across categories. A big χ² (small p) means the observed counts stray too far from the theory to be luck — reject the fit.

The verdict​

26.66>5.991 ⇒ reject H0.26.66 > 5.991 \ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{reject } H_0.26.66>5.991 ⇒ reject H0​.

Conclusion: the weights of the 2020 population are different from those expected under the 2010 distribution. Looking at where the counts diverge, the shift is toward the lighter end — the <50 bin came in above expectation (140 vs 100) while the >75 bin came in below (200 vs 250) — so the distribution genuinely changed shape over the decade.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

observed = np.array([140, 160, 200])          # 2020 sample, n = 500
props    = np.array([0.20, 0.30, 0.50])        # 2010 census proportions
expected = props * observed.sum()              # -> [100, 150, 250]

chi2, p = stats.chisquare(f_obs=observed, f_exp=expected)
df = len(observed) - 1
crit = stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, df)                # critical value at α = 0.05

print(f"expected counts : {expected}")
print(f"chi-square stat : {chi2:.2f}")         # 26.66
print(f"df = {df},  critical (α=0.05) = {crit:.3f}")   # 2, 5.991
print(f"p-value         : {p:.6f}")
print("decision        :", "REJECT H0 — distribution changed" if chi2 > crit
      else "fail to reject H0")

Why a quant cares​

The goodness-of-fit test is the formal answer to "has the distribution changed?" — a question that runs through risk and trading:

  • Regime / distribution drift. Bucket returns (or volatility, or volume) into ranges and compare this year's observed counts against last year's — or against your model's assumed distribution. A big χ² is a statistical alarm that the world your model was fit on no longer holds.
  • Backtest sanity checks. If a strategy assumes signals fall into certain outcome categories at certain rates, goodness of fit tests whether the realized frequencies match — catching a broken assumption before it costs money.
  • One category can dominate. Here the <50 bin alone contributed 16 of the 26.66. The per-category (O−E)2/E(O-E)^2/E(O−E)2/E terms tell you where the misfit lives — which bucket drove the rejection — not just that a misfit exists.

Where a z/t test asks "did the average move?", chi-square asks "did the whole shape move?" — often the more important question when tails are what hurt you.

Confidence Interval & Margin of Error

So far every test in this module — z-test, t-test — took a sample mean and asked whether it was too far from a claimed population mean to be luck. For a 95% two-tailed test we drew the familiar picture: the middle 95% is where we accept the null, and the two extreme ends — 2.5% each — are where we reject it.

But there's a question we never actually answered: what values could lie within that confidence interval? That flips the whole exercise from testing a claim to estimating the unknown mean — and it's where the confidence interval earns its name.

The problem with a point estimate​

Recall the point estimate: the sample mean xˉ\bar xxˉ is our single best guess for the population mean μ\muμ.

xˉ  ⟶  μ(the point estimate stands in for the population mean)\bar x \;\longrightarrow\; \mu \qquad (\text{the point estimate stands in for the population mean})xˉ⟶μ(the point estimate stands in for the population mean)

Here's the trouble. Suppose the true mean is μ=3\mu = 3μ=3 and one sample gives xˉ=2.5\bar x = 2.5xˉ=2.5. That's less than 3. Draw a different sample and xˉ\bar xxˉ might come out greater than 3. Every sample lands somewhere slightly different, so a lone number is silent about how far off it might be. That is the problem of using a point estimate.

The fix is to stop reporting one number and instead define a range — "my estimate lies between 2 and 4." That range, tied to a confidence level, is the confidence interval, and it honestly represents an estimate of an unknown population parameter.

The formula​

A confidence interval runs from a lower bound to a higher bound, and it's built by one simple recipe:

  Confidence Interval=Point Estimate  ±  Margin of Error  \boxed{\;\textbf{Confidence Interval} = \textbf{Point Estimate} \;\pm\; \textbf{Margin of Error}\;}Confidence Interval=Point Estimate±Margin of Error​

We use ±\pm± because the point estimate will be a little less than, a little more than, or roughly equal to the true mean — so we reach out on both sides. The point estimate is xˉ\bar xxˉ; the margin of error is where the test machinery comes back:

xˉ⏟point estimate  ±  zα/2 σn⏟margin of error (z-test)\underbrace{\bar x}_{\text{point estimate}} \;\pm\; \underbrace{z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}}_{\text{margin of error (z-test)}}point estimatexˉ​​±margin of error (z-test)zα/2​n​σ​​​
  • zα/2z_{\alpha/2}zα/2​ is the critical value that carves off area α/2\alpha/2α/2 in each tail — read straight from the z-table, exactly as in the z-test.
  • σ/n\sigma/\sqrt nσ/n​ is the standard error.

If instead you're doing a t-test (population σ\sigmaσ unknown), the shape is identical — you just swap the z-table for the t-table and use the sample sss, remembering to compute the degrees of freedom:

xˉ  ±  tα/2 sn(df=n−1)\bar x \;\pm\; t_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \qquad (\text{df} = n-1)xˉ±tα/2​n​s​(df=n−1)

Worked example — the CAT exam​

On the verbal section of the CAT exam, the population standard deviation is known to be 100 (σ is known ⇒ use a z-test). A sample of 30 test takers has a mean of 520. Construct a 95% confidence interval about the mean.

Set up the critical value. With 95% confidence, α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, so α/2=0.025\alpha/2 = 0.025α/2=0.025. From the z-table, the area to the left of the upper cutoff is 1−0.025=0.97501 - 0.025 = 0.97501−0.025=0.9750, which maps to

zα/2=z0.025=1.96(and the lower cutoff is −1.96).z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.025} = 1.96 \qquad (\text{and the lower cutoff is } -1.96).zα/2​=z0.025​=1.96(and the lower cutoff is −1.96).

Lower confidence interval:

Lower C.I.=520−(1.96)10025=520−39.2=480.8\text{Lower C.I.} = 520 - (1.96)\frac{100}{\sqrt{25}} = 520 - 39.2 = \boxed{480.8}Lower C.I.=520−(1.96)25​100​=520−39.2=480.8​

Higher confidence interval:

Higher C.I.=520+(1.96)10025=520+39.2=559.2\text{Higher C.I.} = 520 + (1.96)\frac{100}{\sqrt{25}} = 520 + 39.2 = \boxed{559.2}Higher C.I.=520+(1.96)25​100​=520+39.2=559.2​

Conclusion: I am 95% confident that the mean CAT exam score lies between 480.8 and 559.2. If a claimed mean falls inside this interval we'd accept the null; if it falls outside, we'd reject it — the interval and the test are two views of the same thing.

A slip in the source numbers

The problem says 30 test takers, but the worked arithmetic uses 25\sqrt{25}25​ (i.e. n=25n = 25n=25) to reach the quoted [480.8,559.2][480.8, 559.2][480.8,559.2]. I've kept the notes' published answer. For the record, with n=30n = 30n=30 the margin would be 1.96×100/30≈35.81.96 \times 100/\sqrt{30} \approx 35.81.96×100/30​≈35.8, giving [484.2, 555.8][484.2,\ 555.8][484.2, 555.8] — same method, slightly tighter interval.

The interval is the point estimate x̄ reached out by the margin of error on each side. Shrink it by collecting more data (bigger n ⇒ smaller σ/√n) or accepting less confidence (90% is narrower than 99%). The defaults are the CAT example: 520 ± 1.96·(100/√25) = [480.8, 559.2].

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def conf_interval(xbar, sigma, n, conf=0.95, sigma_known=True):
    alpha = 1 - conf
    se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
    if sigma_known:                      # z-test
        crit = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)
    else:                                # t-test (sigma estimated by s)
        crit = stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha/2, df=n-1)
    moe = crit * se
    return xbar - moe, xbar + moe, crit, moe

lo, hi, crit, moe = conf_interval(xbar=520, sigma=100, n=25, conf=0.95)
print(f"critical z = {crit:.2f},  margin of error = ±{moe:.1f}")
print(f"95% CI = [{lo:.1f}, {hi:.1f}]")     # [480.8, 559.2]
print("I am 95% confident the mean CAT score is in this range.")

Why a quant cares​

The margin of error is the number risk reports quietly drop — and dropping it is how a point estimate masquerades as certainty:

  • Every reported statistic deserves a ± band. A mean return, a volatility, a Sharpe ratio — reporting "0.9 Sharpe" hides that the 95% interval might be [0.1, 1.7][0.1,\ 1.7][0.1, 1.7]. The width, driven by σ/n\sigma/\sqrt nσ/n​, is the honest measure of how much you actually know.
  • The n\sqrt nn​ in the margin is the tax on precision. To halve the margin of error you need four times the data — which is why a short track record produces a uselessly wide interval no matter how good the point estimate looks.
  • Confidence level is a dial with a cost. Demanding 99% instead of 95% widens the interval (the critical value grows from 1.96 to 2.58); more certainty buys you a vaguer range. Choosing it is a judgment call about how badly a miss would hurt.

Report the point, but size your decisions to the interval — the band, not the center, is what tells you whether you can survive being wrong.

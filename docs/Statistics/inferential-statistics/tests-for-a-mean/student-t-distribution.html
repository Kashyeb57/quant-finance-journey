The Student-t Distribution

The z-test has a hidden requirement. When we perform any analysis using a z-score, we require σ\sigmaσ — the population standard deviation — to be already known:

z=xˉ−μσ/nz = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}z=σ/n​xˉ−μ​

But in almost every real problem, we don't know σ\sigmaσ. We only have a sample. So:

How do we perform an analysis when we don't know the population standard deviation?

The answer is Student's t-distribution.

Estimate σ with the sample, and the z becomes a t​

The fix is natural: replace the unknown population σ\sigmaσ with the sample standard deviation sss computed from your data. That single swap changes the statistic — and the distribution it follows:

z=xˉ−μσ/n⏟z-table, σ known⟶t=xˉ−μs/n⏟t-table, σ unknown\underbrace{z = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}}_{\text{z-table, } \sigma \text{ known}} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{t = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{s/\sqrt n}}_{\text{t-table, } \sigma \text{ unknown}}z-table, σ knownz=σ/n​xˉ−μ​​​⟶t-table, σ unknownt=s/n​xˉ−μ​​​

where sss = sample standard deviation. You now look the value up in the t-table instead of the z-table — that lookup is the t-test.

Why isn't it still a normal? Because sss is itself an estimate, wobbling from sample to sample. That extra uncertainty has to go somewhere, and it shows up as fatter tails: the t-distribution admits more extreme values than the normal, so its critical values sit farther out. The smaller the sample, the fatter the tails.

Degrees of freedom​

The t-distribution isn't one curve — it's a family indexed by degrees of freedom (dof):

dof=n−1\text{dof} = n - 1dof=n−1

For a sample of 3 people, dof=3−1=2\text{dof} = 3 - 1 = 2dof=3−1=2. Where does the "−1-1−1" come from? Once you fix the sample mean, the values are no longer all free to vary. Picture 3 people whose average is pinned: you can choose the first two freely, but the third is then forced to whatever value makes the mean come out right.

So n−1n-1n−1 counts the values that were genuinely free to vary after the mean ate up one degree of freedom. It's the same reason sample variance divides by n−1n-1n−1, not nnn.

t versus normal — watch the tails converge​

Below, switch the distribution to t and drag the degrees of freedom. At low dof the t (solid) is visibly shorter and fatter-tailed than the normal (dashed), and its critical value sits farther out. Push dof toward 30–40 and the two curves become nearly indistinguishable — the t-distribution converges to the normal as the sample grows, which is exactly why the z-test is fine once n≥30n \ge 30n≥30.

The rejection region is fixed by α before you see data. If the observed statistic lands in it — equivalently, once its p-value (yellow) is smaller than α (red) — you reject H₀. Switch to t and drag df down: the tails fatten, the critical value moves outward, and small samples get harder to call.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Two-tailed 95% critical value: t (df = n−1) vs the normal's 1.96.
print(f"{'n':>4} {'dof':>4} {'t crit':>8} {'z crit':>8}")
for n in (2, 3, 5, 10, 30, 100):
    dof = n - 1
    t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, dof)
    print(f"{n:>4} {dof:>4} {t_crit:>8.3f} {1.96:>8.2f}")

# Small samples demand a MORE extreme statistic to reach significance:
# t crit shrinks from ~12.7 (n=2) toward 1.96 as n grows.

Why a quant cares​

Finance lives in the small-sample, unknown-σ world the t-distribution was built for:

  • You never know a strategy's true volatility — you estimate it from returns as sss. So a Sharpe-ratio significance test is a t-test, not a z-test, and with only a year or two of data the fatter t-tails mean you need a bigger observed Sharpe to call it real.
  • The t's fat tails are a feature, not a nuisance. Underestimating how often extreme values occur is precisely the error that blows up risk models; the t builds that humility into the critical value.
  • "n≥30n \ge 30n≥30" is a rule of thumb, not a law. With genuinely fat-tailed returns even 30 points can be too few, and the gap between t and z matters most exactly when data is scarce and stakes are high.

The t-distribution is the honest default: use it whenever you had to estimate the spread — which is almost always.

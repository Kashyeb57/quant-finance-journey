Types of ANOVA

There are three types of ANOVA, and which one you use depends on how many factors you have and whether the levels are independent or dependent. (A favourite interview question — be able to explain all three.)

TypeFactorsLevelsExample
① One-wayone≥ 2, independentmedication dosage (10/20/30 mg)
② Repeated measuresone≥ 2, dependentrunning distance over Day 1/2/3
③ Factorialtwo or more≥ 2 each, can be independent and dependentrunning × gender

The whole distinction between the first two is independent vs dependent levels — different subjects in each group, versus the same subjects measured repeatedly:

① One-way ANOVA​

One factor, with at least two levels, and the levels are independent.

A doctor wants to test a new medication to decrease headache. They split participants into 3 conditions — 10 mg, 20 mg, 30 mg — and ask participants to rate their headache from 1 to 10.

Here medication is the factor and the dosages (10/20/30 mg) are the levels. The levels are independent: the 10 mg group and the 30 mg group are different people — one dose has no relationship to another.

10 mg20 mg30 mg
572
346
………

Different participants give different ratings depending on the dose's effect. One factor, independent groups ⇒ one-way ANOVA.

② Repeated-measures ANOVA​

One factor, with at least two levels, but the levels are dependent.

Take running as the factor. Select a sample of people and have each person run on Day 1, Day 2, and Day 3.

The levels are Day 1 / Day 2 / Day 3, and they're dependent because the same person is measured on every day — their Day 2 is linked to their Day 1. (Performance might not improve on Day 2 but jump on Day 3.)

SubjectDay 1Day 2Day 3
Person 1854
Person 2749
…………

Same subjects, repeated across the levels ⇒ repeated-measures ANOVA.

③ Factorial ANOVA​

Two or more factors (each with at least two levels), and the levels can be independent and dependent.

Reuse the running data, but add a second factor: gender (levels Male / Female). Now two factors cross each other:

GenderRunning: Day 1Day 2Day 3
Male854
Male943
Female246
Female783

Male and female are completely separate entities (independent), while the day-to-day running for one person is dependent — so a factorial design mixes both. Two or more factors ⇒ factorial ANOVA.

import pandas as pd

# The three types differ mainly in DATA SHAPE:

# ① One-way: one factor (dosage), independent groups
one_way = pd.DataFrame({
    "dosage":   ["10mg","10mg","20mg","20mg","30mg","30mg"],
    "headache": [5, 3, 7, 4, 2, 6],
})

# ② Repeated measures: one factor (day), SAME subjects across levels
repeated = pd.DataFrame({
    "subject": ["P1","P1","P1","P2","P2","P2"],
    "day":     ["D1","D2","D3","D1","D2","D3"],
    "km":      [8, 5, 4, 7, 4, 9],
})

# ③ Factorial: two factors (gender × day)
factorial = pd.DataFrame({
    "gender": ["M","M","M","F","F","F"],
    "day":    ["D1","D2","D3","D1","D2","D3"],
    "km":     [8, 5, 4, 2, 4, 6],
})

print(one_way.groupby("dosage")["headache"].mean())
# scipy.stats.f_oneway → one-way;  statsmodels AnovaRM → repeated measures;
# statsmodels ols + anova_lm → factorial. The F-test mechanics come next lesson.

Why a quant cares​

The three designs map cleanly onto three shapes of trading question:

  • One-way — compare a metric across independent buckets: mean returns of three separate strategies, or across sectors, where the groups share no members.
  • Repeated measures — the same assets or portfolio measured over repeated periods or conditions (pre/during/post a regime). Because the measurements are linked, treating them as independent overstates your evidence — repeated-measures handles the correlation.
  • Factorial — two drivers at once: does return depend on strategy AND market regime, and do they interact? Factorial ANOVA is the tool for interaction effects, the same idea as interaction terms in a regression.

Picking the right type is really about honoring the dependence structure of your data — the single thing financial time series violate most often.

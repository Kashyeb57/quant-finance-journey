Assumptions of ANOVA

Every test carries assumptions: the z- and t-tests lean on a normal sampling distribution, the chi-square test on a right-skewed one. ANOVA has four. If your problem satisfies them, you can go ahead and use ANOVA — and these are a favourite interview question, so they're worth knowing cold.

① Normality of the sampling distribution of the mean​

The distribution of the sample mean is normally distributed (i.e. it follows a Gaussian). The instant you hear "sampling distribution of the mean" and "normality," the Central Limit Theorem should come to mind — that's exactly what guarantees this, and it's why ANOVA is fairly forgiving here for reasonable sample sizes.

② Absence of outliers​

Outlying scores need to be removed from the dataset. Even a single extreme point can distort group means and variances enough to change the verdict, so you screen for outliers first — with a box plot, the interquartile range (IQR), and other techniques from feature engineering.

③ Homogeneity of variance​

The population variance in the different levels of each independent variable (factor) must be equal — "homogeneity" means homogeneous / the same. For three levels that's:

σ12=σ22=σ32\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma_3^2σ12​=σ22​=σ32​

Each group can have a different mean (that's what ANOVA is testing), but they must share roughly the same spread. Left below satisfies it; right violates it (one group is far wider):

④ Samples are independent and random​

The samples must be independent and randomly selected. One observation shouldn't influence another, and each should be drawn at random from its population — the same backbone of honest sampling that every test in this module rests on.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
g1 = rng.normal(20, 3, 40)
g2 = rng.normal(22, 3, 40)
g3 = rng.normal(21, 3, 40)

# ① normality of each group (Shapiro–Wilk: p > 0.05 => can't reject normality)
for i, g in enumerate([g1, g2, g3], 1):
    print(f"group {i} normality p = {stats.shapiro(g).pvalue:.3f}")

# ② outliers via the IQR rule (flag points outside [Q1-1.5·IQR, Q3+1.5·IQR])
def iqr_outliers(x):
    q1, q3 = np.percentile(x, [25, 75]); iqr = q3 - q1
    return x[(x < q1 - 1.5*iqr) | (x > q3 + 1.5*iqr)]
print("outliers in g1:", iqr_outliers(g1))

# ③ homogeneity of variance (Levene: p > 0.05 => equal variances OK)
print(f"Levene equal-variance p = {stats.levene(g1, g2, g3).pvalue:.3f}")

# ④ independence/randomness is a design property — ensured by how you sample.

Why a quant cares​

The assumptions aren't box-ticking — each one, when it breaks, breaks in a way markets specialise in:

  • Homogeneity of variance is the one that fails first. Financial groups (regimes, sectors, eras) almost never share a variance — volatility clusters and shifts. A calm-vs-crisis comparison violates σ12=σ22\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2σ12​=σ22​ badly, so a naïve ANOVA is untrustworthy; you'd reach for a Welch's ANOVA or a robust alternative.
  • Outliers aren't noise, they're the P&L. "Remove the outliers" is dangerous advice in finance, where the extreme days are the story. Blindly deleting them to satisfy an assumption can erase exactly the risk you were trying to measure — flag and understand them, don't reflexively bin them.
  • Independence is routinely violated by time. Return observations are autocorrelated and non-stationary, so "independent and random" rarely holds for a raw time series. That's why group-comparison tests on financial data need care (or a time-series model) rather than a textbook ANOVA.

Knowing the assumptions is step one; knowing how they fail in markets is what separates a real analysis from a p-value fished out of invalid conditions.

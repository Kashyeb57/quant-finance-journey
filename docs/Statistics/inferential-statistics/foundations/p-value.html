What Is a P-value?

The p-value is a number, calculated from a statistical test, that describes how likely you are to have found a particular set of observations if the null hypothesis were true. P-values are used in hypothesis testing to help decide whether to reject the null hypothesis.

Read that definition twice, because almost everyone misreads it. The p-value is not the probability the null is true. It is the probability of seeing data at least this extreme in a world where H0H_0H0​ holds. Small p-value ⇒ "what I observed would be a weird fluke if the null were true" ⇒ evidence against the null.

The intuition: how often do you land here?​

Picture pressing a space bar 100 times and marking where your finger lands. Most presses cluster in a likely region; a few stray to an unlikely one. If a spot is one where you'd land around 20 times out of 100, its "p-value" is about 0.20.20.2 — perfectly ordinary. A spot you'd hit only rarely carries a tiny p-value — landing there is surprising, and surprise is exactly what makes you doubt the default assumption.

p=P(data this extreme, or more∣H0 true)p = P(\text{data this extreme, or more} \mid H_0 \text{ true})p=P(data this extreme, or more∣H0​ true)

The coin example​

Is a coin fair or not? Flip it 100 times.

Under a fair coin we'd expect the probabilities to be balanced; an unfair coin tilts them:

P(H)=0.5,P(T)=0.5(fair)P(H)=0.6,P(T)=0.4P(H)=0.7,P(T)=0.3\begin{aligned} P(H) &= 0.5, & P(T) &= 0.5 \quad(\text{fair})\\ P(H) &= 0.6, & P(T) &= 0.4\\ P(H) &= 0.7, & P(T) &= 0.3 \end{aligned}P(H)P(H)P(H)​=0.5,=0.6,=0.7,​P(T)P(T)P(T)​=0.5(fair)=0.4=0.3​

Run the mechanism from the previous lesson:

  1. Null hypothesis H0H_0H0​: the coin is fair.
  2. Alternate hypothesis H1H_1H1​: the coin is not fair.
  3. Experiment: flip it 100 times and count heads.
  4. Significance value: choose α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, so the confidence interval is C.I.=1−0.05=0.95\text{C.I.} = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95C.I.=1−0.05=0.95.
  5. Conclusion: compare ppp against α\alphaα.

Where α lives: the 0.025 tails​

With α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05 split across a two-tailed test, the middle 95% is the region where "the coin is fair" is believable, and 0.025 in each tail is the rejection region where we'd conclude "the coin is not fair." A result of ~50 heads sits comfortably in the middle; a result far out in a tail — say 70 heads — is where the null dies.

The rejection region is fixed by α before you see data. If the observed statistic lands in it — equivalently, once its p-value (yellow) is smaller than α (red) — you reject H₀. Switch to t and drag df down: the tails fatten, the critical value moves outward, and small samples get harder to call.

The yellow area is the p-value (how extreme your result is); the red area is α (the bar you set in advance). The whole test collapses to comparing those two areas.

The decision rule​

  p<α  ⇒  Reject the null hypothesis  \boxed{\;p < \alpha \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{Reject the null hypothesis}\;}p<α⇒Reject the null hypothesis​ else  ⇒  Fail to reject the null hypothesis\text{else} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{Fail to reject the null hypothesis}else⇒Fail to reject the null hypothesis

That's the entire payoff. If your observations would be too improbable under H0H_0H0​ (p below your threshold), you reject H0H_0H0​. Otherwise the evidence isn't strong enough and you retain it.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Observed: 70 heads in 100 flips. Is the coin fair?
# H0: p = 0.5 (fair)   H1: p != 0.5 (two-tailed)
heads, n, alpha = 70, 100, 0.05

# exact two-sided p-value under H0 (binomial test)
p_value = stats.binomtest(heads, n, 0.5, alternative='two-sided').pvalue
print(f"observed heads : {heads}/{n}")
print(f"p-value        : {p_value:.6f}")
print("decision       :",
      "REJECT H0 — coin is not fair" if p_value < alpha
      else "fail to reject H0 — no evidence it's unfair")

# 50 heads, by contrast, is the least surprising result possible:
print("\n50/100 p-value:", stats.binomtest(50, n, 0.5).pvalue)  # = 1.0

Why a quant cares​

The p-value is where good intentions go to die, so a quant has to hold two ideas at once:

  • It answers the right conditional the wrong way round. ppp is P(data∣H0)P(\text{data}\mid H_0)P(data∣H0​), never P(H0∣data)P(H_0\mid \text{data})P(H0​∣data). Confusing them — the "prosecutor's fallacy" — is how a 1-in-20 fluke gets sold as a 95%-sure edge.
  • A small p is necessary, not sufficient. With enough data an economically meaningless effect clears p<0.05p<0.05p<0.05; a real edge still has to beat costs and slippage. Statistical significance ≠ tradable significance.
  • Every extra test spends your α. Try 20 strategies at α=0.05\alpha=0.05α=0.05 and you expect one "winner" from noise alone. That's the multiple-testing trap that makes backtests so treacherous — the topic the big-picture page opens with.

Use the p-value as a speed bump — "could this plausibly be luck?" — not as a probability that you're right.

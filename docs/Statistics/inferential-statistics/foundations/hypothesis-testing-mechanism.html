A Hypothesis and the Hypothesis-Testing Mechanism

Inferential statistics is about reaching a conclusion or an inference. Descriptive statistics summarized a sample; inference takes the next, harder step — it uses that small sample to make a defensible claim about the whole population you cannot see.

A hypothesis is just a claim about the population — "the average pass rate is 85%," "this coin is fair," "the medication has no effect." Hypothesis testing is the formal procedure that weighs a sample against that claim and decides whether the evidence is strong enough to overturn it.

The courtroom analogy​

The cleanest way to feel the mechanism is a criminal trial:

Person  ⟶  Crime  ⟶  Court\text{Person} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Crime} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Court}Person⟶Crime⟶Court

A court doesn't start by assuming the defendant is guilty. It begins by assuming innocence and only convicts if the evidence is overwhelming. Hypothesis testing works exactly the same way — and every piece of the trial has a statistical name.

The mechanism, step by step​

① Null hypothesis H0H_0H0​ — "the person is not guilty." This is the assumption you are beginning with — the default, the status quo, "nothing unusual is going on." In statistics it's almost always the no-effect / no-difference statement.

② Alternate hypothesis H1H_1H1​ — "the person is guilty." It is simply the opposite of the null — the claim that there is an effect or a difference, the thing you'd need evidence to establish.

③ Experiment → statistical analysis. You run the experiment and gather evidence — the courtroom's direct proof (DNA test, fingerprint test). In statistics this evidence is boiled down to two numbers: the p-value and the significance value α\alphaα.

④ Accept the null hypothesis, or reject the null hypothesis. The verdict. If the evidence isn't strong enough, you fail to reject H0H_0H0​ (acquit); if it is, you reject H0H_0H0​ in favor of H1H_1H1​ (convict).

The asymmetry that matters

You never "prove the null true" — a court doesn't declare the defendant innocent, only not guilty. Failing to reject H0H_0H0​ means the evidence wasn't strong enough, not that H0H_0H0​ is confirmed. This one-sided burden of proof is the heart of the whole method.

A worked setup​

Colleges in District A state that their average pass percentage of students is 85%. A new college opened in the district, and a sample of 100 of its students had a pass percentage of 90%, with a standard deviation of 4%. Does this college have a different pass percentage?

Setting up the hypotheses is the entire skill at this stage. The district's long-run claim is the status quo, so it becomes the null; "different" (in either direction) is the alternative:

H0:  μ=85%H1:  μ≠85%H_0:\; \mu = 85\% \qquad\qquad H_1:\; \mu \neq 85\%H0​:μ=85%H1​:μ=85%

Note the ≠\neq= — the question asks whether the rate is different, not specifically higher. That "different in either direction" phrasing makes this a two-tailed test, a distinction the z-test lesson turns into an actual number. Everything we've set up — μ\muμ, the sample mean xˉ=90%\bar x = 90\%xˉ=90%, n=100n = 100n=100, the standard deviation — is exactly what the test statistic will consume.

Why a quant cares​

Every claim a quant makes is a hypothesis test in disguise, and the null is almost always the deflating one:

  • "This strategy has positive expected return." H0H_0H0​: the true edge is zero; your backtest is noise. You have to reject that null before risking capital — and markets are a hostile courtroom where flukes are common.
  • "This model's alpha survived out of sample." H0H_0H0​: the out-of-sample mean is zero. Failing to reject isn't proof the model works; it's an acquittal for lack of evidence.
  • The burden of proof protects your capital. Assuming "no edge" until the data forces you to conclude otherwise is precisely the discipline that stops you from trading on randomness.

Set the null to the boring, skeptical claim, and make the data work to overturn it. That habit — not any single formula — is what inference buys you.

Causal Inference & DAGs

Every quant eventually learns the hard way that correlation is not causation — but far fewer learn the tool that tells you what to do about it. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a picture of your causal assumptions: variables are nodes, and an arrow X→YX \to YX→Y says "XXX has a direct causal influence on YYY." Draw the graph honestly and it tells you exactly which variables to control for to get an unbiased effect — and, just as important, which ones you must not touch.

The framing here follows Richard McElreath's Statistical Rethinking, the source my Applied Statistical Methods course drew its DAG material from.

Three building blocks​

Every causal graph is assembled from three elementary shapes. Knowing how information flows through each is 90% of the game.

The fork (confounder) — X←Z→YX \leftarrow Z \to YX←Z→Y A common cause ZZZ drives both XXX and YYY. This manufactures a correlation between XXX and YYY even when neither causes the other. You must control for ZZZ to see the true X→YX\to YX→Y effect. This is the classic trap.

The pipe (mediator) — X→Z→YX \to Z \to YX→Z→Y XXX affects YYY through ZZZ. If you control for ZZZ here, you accidentally erase the very effect you were trying to measure. Leave mediators alone (unless you specifically want the direct-only effect).

The collider — X→Z←YX \to Z \leftarrow YX→Z←Y XXX and YYY both cause ZZZ, but are otherwise unrelated. Here the danger is the opposite: controlling for ZZZ creates a fake correlation between XXX and YYY that wasn't there. Conditioning on a collider is a subtle, common way to fool yourself.

Reading the graph: paths, d-separation, and the backdoor​

Those three shapes combine into a rule for any graph. Trace every path connecting XXX and YYY (following arrows in either direction). A path is open — it carries a possibly-spurious association — unless it is blocked, and the blocking rules come straight from the shapes:

  • a fork or a pipe on the path is blocked when you condition on its middle variable;
  • a collider is blocked by default, and conditioning on it (or on a descendant of it) opens it.

When every path between XXX and YYY is blocked, they are d-separated — the graph implies no association. The recipe for an honest effect estimate is the backdoor criterion: control for the set of variables that blocks every backdoor path (a path leaving XXX through an arrow pointing into XXX) without opening any collider — no more, no less. "Throw everything into the regression" fails precisely because an extra control can unblock a collider or close a pipe.

The confounder, in code​

A market example: an interest-rate regime ZZZ pushes both a bond ETF's returns (XXX) and a utility stock's returns (YYY). Neither causes the other, yet they'll look strongly linked — until you control for the regime.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 1000

Z = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                 # hidden regime (e.g. rate shock)
X = 0.8 * Z + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)     # X is driven by Z
Y = 0.8 * Z + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)     # Y is driven by Z — NOT by X

# 1) Naive: correlate X and Y directly
naive = np.corrcoef(X, Y)[0, 1]

# 2) Control for Z: regress Y on X *and* Z, read X's coefficient
M = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X, Z])
b = np.linalg.lstsq(M, Y, rcond=None)[0]

print(f"naive corr(X, Y)              = {naive:+.2f}")
print(f"effect of X after adjusting Z = {b[1]:+.2f}")
runs in your browser

The naive correlation looks large and convincing (~0.6); the moment you put the confounder ZZZ in the regression, XXX's coefficient collapses to ~0 — the honest answer. The entire skill is knowing that ZZZ needed to be there. A DAG is how you know.

Why this matters for markets​

  • Spurious signals. Two assets co-moving because both react to rates, the dollar, or risk-on/risk-off is confounding, not a tradable relationship. Control for the common driver before believing a pairs trade.
  • Bad controls. Throwing "everything" into a regression is not safe — adding a mediator or a collider can bias the coefficient you care about. More variables ≠ more truth.
  • Backtest survivorship is a collider bias in disguise: selecting on "still-listed" (an outcome of past returns) distorts the very relationships you're studying.

Simpson's paradox​

The most vivid confounding trap is Simpson's paradox: a trend that holds inside every subgroup can reverse once the groups are pooled. A strategy might show a positive exposure-vs-return slope in each volatility regime separately, yet a negative slope when you lump all regimes together — because the regime (a confounder) drives both exposure and returns. It is not a curiosity: it is a warning that the answer depends on which variables you condition on, and only a causal graph — not the data alone — tells you which grouping is the honest one. Aggregate blindly and you can conclude the exact opposite of the truth.

Pearl's ladder of causation​

Judea Pearl arranges causal questions into three rungs — and no amount of rung-1 data answers a rung-2 or rung-3 question without a causal model:

  1. Association — seeing. P(Y∣X)P(Y \mid X)P(Y∣X): what does observing XXX tell me about YYY? Pure correlation; this is all a plain dataset offers.
  2. Intervention — doing. P(Y∣do⁡(X=x))P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(X=x))P(Y∣do(X=x)): what happens to YYY if I reach in and set XXX? The do-operator severs the arrows into XXX, cutting its confounders loose — unlike P(Y∣X=x)P(Y \mid X=x)P(Y∣X=x), which merely observes XXX happening to equal xxx and drags all its confounders along. A correctly-adjusted regression, guided by the DAG and the backdoor criterion, estimates this interventional effect from observational data.
  3. Counterfactual — imagining. P(Yx∣X=x′,Y=y′)P(Y_x \mid X=x', Y=y')P(Yx​∣X=x′,Y=y′): given what actually happened, what would have happened had XXX been different? "Would my headache have cleared without the pill I took?" These are the deepest questions — retrospective, single-case, unanswerable by experiment alone — and they need a full structural model.

Most of statistics lives on rung 1. Causal inference is the discipline of climbing to rungs 2 and 3 with every assumption made explicit in the graph.

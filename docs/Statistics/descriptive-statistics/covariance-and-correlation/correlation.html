Correlation

Covariance gets the direction of a relationship right but leaves its strength uninterpretable — the magnitude is unbounded and changes with units. Correlation fixes this by normalizing covariance into a fixed, comparable range. It's the number quants actually use.

Pearson correlation coefficient​

Divide covariance by the two standard deviations, and the units cancel — squeezing the result into a clean [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1]:

ρX,Y=Cov⁡(X,Y)σX σY\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}ρX,Y​=σX​σY​Cov(X,Y)​

Now the number is directly interpretable:

  • ρ=+1\rho = +1ρ=+1 — perfect positive linear relationship (the points lie on an upward line).
  • ρ=−1\rho = -1ρ=−1 — perfect negative linear relationship (a downward line).
  • ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0 — no linear relationship.
  • The closer to ±1\pm 1±1, the tighter the linear co-movement; the closer to 000, the weaker.

The crucial qualifier is linear. Pearson measures straight-line association specifically. A relationship can be perfectly predictable yet curved, and Pearson will report less than 1 — it only sees the straight-line part.

Seeing correlation in 3D​

Correlation has a shape. If you plot the joint density of two variables — how likely each combination of (X,Y)(X, Y)(X,Y) is — a positive correlation stops being an abstract number and becomes something you can see: the round bell of two independent variables gets stretched into a ridge along the diagonal. Drag the surface below to rotate it.

3DJoint density of two variables with ρ = 0.8

With ρ = 0.8 the bell is pulled into a ridge along the line Y = X: when X is high, Y tends to be high too. At ρ = 0 it would be a symmetric round hill; a negative ρ tilts the ridge the other way. That tilt IS the correlation.

Spearman rank correlation​

Spearman correlation is Pearson computed on the ranks of the data instead of the raw values. Replace each value by its position in sorted order, then correlate those ranks:

ρs=Cov⁡(R(X), R(Y))σR(X) σR(Y)\rho_s = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\big(R(X),\,R(Y)\big)}{\sigma_{R(X)}\,\sigma_{R(Y)}}ρs​=σR(X)​σR(Y)​Cov(R(X),R(Y))​

Because it only cares about order, Spearman captures any monotonic relationship — one where YYY consistently rises (or consistently falls) as XXX rises, even along a bent curve. If YYY always increases with XXX but on a curve, Pearson reads below 1 while Spearman reads exactly 1. Two practical consequences: use Spearman when the link is monotonic-but-nonlinear, and lean on it when outliers would distort Pearson (ranks are robust — the largest value is just "rank nnn," however extreme).

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

x = rng.normal(0, 1, 300)
y = 0.8*x + rng.normal(0, 0.6, 300)             # linearly related to x

print(f"Pearson r (x, y):        {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0,1]: .3f}   (linear strength)")

# a monotonic but strongly NON-linear link
z = np.exp(3*x)
print(f"Pearson  (x, e^(3x)):    {stats.pearsonr(x, z)[0]: .3f}   <- fooled by the curve")
print(f"Spearman (x, e^(3x)):    {stats.spearmanr(x, z)[0]: .3f}   <- =1: perfectly monotonic")

Feature selection: a worked intuition​

Say you're predicting a house price and you have candidate features. Correlation with price tells you which carry signal:

  • Size, number of rooms, location quality → positively correlated with price. Useful features.
  • "Is it haunted?" → negatively correlated with price. Also useful (in the other direction).
  • Number of people currently staying in the house → roughly zero correlation with price. Drop it; it adds noise, not signal.

This is the essence of feature selection: keep the features that correlate with the target, drop the ones that don't — and watch for two features highly correlated with each other, since they carry redundant information and you can often keep just one. (More in Regression & Classification.)

Why a quant cares​

Correlation is woven through the whole job:

  • Diversification and portfolio risk are built on it. Two assets with high positive correlation barely diversify each other; low or negative correlation is what smooths a portfolio's equity curve. A correlation matrix is the raw input to portfolio construction and risk models.
  • Hedging means deliberately holding negatively (or highly positively) correlated positions to cancel a risk.
  • Feature selection, as above, keeps models lean and less prone to overfitting.

The warning that never goes away​

Correlation is not causation. Two series can move together because one drives the other, because a hidden third factor drives both, or by pure chance over a short window. A high ρ\rhoρ is a clue, never a proof. Deciding which variable actually drives which needs more than a coefficient — it needs the causal reasoning in Causal Inference & DAGs.

Measures of Dispersion

Center isn't enough. Two datasets can share the exact same mean and be completely different — one tightly clustered, one wildly spread. Dispersion measures that spread, and for a quant it's where risk lives.

Ages1=[2,2,4,4]Ages2=[1,1,5,5]\text{Ages}_1 = \left[2, 2, 4, 4\right] \qquad \text{Ages}_2 = \left[1, 1, 5, 5\right]Ages1​=[2,2,4,4]Ages2​=[1,1,5,5]

Both have mean 3. But the second is spread twice as far from that center. A measure of central tendency can't tell them apart; a measure of dispersion is exactly what does.

Variance​

Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean. Squaring does two jobs: it makes every deviation positive (so they don't cancel), and it punishes big deviations far harder than small ones.

Work the two examples by hand (population form, dividing by NNN):

  • Ages1=[2,2,4,4]\text{Ages}_1 = \left[2,2,4,4\right]Ages1​=[2,2,4,4], μ=3\mu = 3μ=3: deviations [−1,−1,1,1]\left[-1,-1,1,1\right][−1,−1,1,1], squares [1,1,1,1]\left[1,1,1,1\right][1,1,1,1], so σ2=44=1\sigma^2 = \frac{4}{4} = 1σ2=44​=1.
  • Ages2=[1,1,5,5]\text{Ages}_2 = \left[1,1,5,5\right]Ages2​=[1,1,5,5], μ=3\mu = 3μ=3: deviations [−2,−2,2,2]\left[-2,-2,2,2\right][−2,−2,2,2], squares [4,4,4,4]\left[4,4,4,4\right][4,4,4,4], so σ2=164=4\sigma^2 = \frac{16}{4} = 4σ2=416​=4.

Same mean, variance of 1 vs 4 — the numbers now capture what the eye saw: the second dataset is four times as "spread" in squared terms.

import numpy as np

calm = np.array([0.1, -0.1, 0.2, -0.2, 0.0])   # daily returns (%)
wild = np.array([2.0, -2.1, 1.9, -1.8, 0.0])

for name, r in [("calm", calm), ("wild", wild)]:
    # ddof=1 -> sample variance (divide by n-1); see the next page for why
    print(f"{name}: mean={r.mean():+.2f}%   sample variance={r.var(ddof=1):.3f}")
runs in your browser

Notice the sample formula divides by n−1n-1n−1, not nnn. That's deliberate and important enough to get its own page.

The one catch: units​

Variance has an awkward flaw — its units are squared. If returns are in percent, variance is in "percent-squared," which nobody can interpret intuitively. The fix is to take the square root and get back to the original scale: that's the standard deviation, the number people actually quote.

Why a quant cares​

Dispersion is risk. Two assets can offer the identical average return and be worlds apart:

  • The calm asset drifts gently; the wild one whipsaws. Same mean, but the wild one can be uninvestable — a big enough swing wipes you out before the average ever pays off.
  • Portfolio construction, position sizing, and Value-at-Risk are all built on measures of dispersion. "How much does this bounce around?" is often a more urgent question than "what's its average?"

The next two pages finish the story: why we divide by n−1, and standard deviation — which in finance has a more famous name: volatility.

The 5-Number Summary

Five percentiles capture the entire skeleton of a distribution — its center, its spread, and its extremes — in one compact summary. It's the basis of the box plot and of a beautifully simple, distribution-free way to flag outliers.

The five numbers​

minimum,Q1,median,Q3,maximum\text{minimum}, \quad Q_1, \quad \text{median}, \quad Q_3, \quad \text{maximum}minimum,Q1​,median,Q3​,maximum
  • Minimum — the smallest value
  • Q1 — 25th percentile (bottom of the middle 50%)
  • Median — 50th percentile (the center)
  • Q3 — 75th percentile (top of the middle 50%)
  • Maximum — the largest value

For the sorted set [1,2,2,2,3,3,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,8,8,9]\left[1,2,2,2,3,3,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,8,8,9\right][1,2,2,2,3,3,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,8,8,9]: minimum =1=1=1, Q1=3Q_1=3Q1​=3, median =5=5=5, Q3=7Q_3=7Q3​=7, maximum =9=9=9.

The box plot​

A box plot draws exactly these five numbers: a box spanning Q1Q_1Q1​ to Q3Q_3Q3​ (the middle 50% of the data), a line inside it at the median, and "whiskers" reaching out to the smallest and largest non-outlier values. It turns the five-number summary into a picture you can scan at a glance — and compare across many series side by side.

The IQR and the outlier fences​

The width of the box is the interquartile range — the spread of the middle half of the data, and a robust alternative to standard deviation:

IQR=Q3−Q1\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1IQR=Q3​−Q1​

The IQR also gives a distribution-free rule for flagging outliers. Anything beyond the fences is suspect:

lower fence=Q1−1.5 IQR,upper fence=Q3+1.5 IQR\text{lower fence} = Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}, \qquad \text{upper fence} = Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}lower fence=Q1​−1.5IQR,upper fence=Q3​+1.5IQR

Worked through with Q1=3Q_1=3Q1​=3, Q3=7Q_3=7Q3​=7: IQR=4\text{IQR}=4IQR=4, lower fence =3−1.5(4)=−3=3-1.5(4)=-3=3−1.5(4)=−3, upper fence =7+1.5(4)=13=7+1.5(4)=13=7+1.5(4)=13. So any value below −3-3−3 or above 131313 is an outlier. Append a 27 to that dataset and it lands far past the upper fence — flagged.

import numpy as np

data = np.array([1,2,2,2,3,3,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,8,8,9, 27.0])  # 27 is a suspect
q1, med, q3 = np.percentile(data, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
lo, hi = q1 - 1.5*iqr, q3 + 1.5*iqr
outliers = data[(data < lo) | (data > hi)]

print(f"min={data.min():.0f}  Q1={q1:.1f}  median={med:.1f}  Q3={q3:.1f}  max={data.max():.0f}")
print(f"IQR={iqr:.1f}   fences=[{lo:.1f}, {hi:.1f}]   outliers={outliers}")
runs in your browser

(One footnote: NumPy interpolates quartiles, so its Q1/Q3Q_1/Q_3Q1​/Q3​ can differ slightly from the hand-counted position method — both are accepted conventions, and the outlier verdict is the same.)

Why a quant cares​

The IQR fence is a cheap, assumption-free filter — it doesn't care whether your data is normal, which matters because returns aren't. Quants use it to flag bad ticks and fat-finger prints in raw price feeds, and to spotlight genuinely extreme return days for a closer look. Because it's built on quartiles rather than the mean and standard deviation, a single wild value can't corrupt the very fence that's supposed to catch it — the same robustness logic behind the median and robust statistics.

# Bayesian Inference

Priors, likelihoods, and posteriors for probabilistic modeling.

## The core idea in one formula

$$
P(\theta \mid \text{data}) \;\propto\; P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\, P(\theta)
$$

The **prior** $P(\theta)$ is what you believed before seeing evidence. The **likelihood** $P(\text{data} \mid \theta)$ measures how well each candidate value of $\theta$ explains what you observed. Multiply them and you get the **posterior** — your updated belief. This is precisely how a quant updates a model when new market data arrives: yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior, and the market never stops handing you new evidence.

## Where the prior comes from

A prior encodes a **state of information** — what you know before the data — and Bayes' theorem is the consistent rule for updating it as evidence arrives. That only makes sense under a **subjective / epistemic** reading of probability, where a probability is a *degree of belief* you can attach to any proposition, not just a long-run frequency. The four classical interpretations — classical, frequency, logical, and subjective — are laid out on [Probability Foundations](/docs/Probability/foundations); Bayesian inference lives in the last two. Frequentist inference is not simply Bayesian inference with a hidden flat prior. It takes a different stance: the parameter is fixed but unknown, and a procedure is judged by how it behaves over repeated samples. The two can agree numerically — with a flat prior, the posterior mean of a normal mean equals the maximum-likelihood estimate, and the credible interval matches the confidence interval — but that is a property of particular models, not a general rule.

## A concrete finance example: estimating drift

Suppose you want to estimate the mean daily return $\mu$ of a stock. You start with a prior belief — say, from history or from a sector average:

$$
\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau^2)
$$

Then you observe $n$ days of returns with sample mean $\bar{x}$, assuming a known daily volatility $\sigma$. Because a normal prior and a normal likelihood are *conjugate*, the posterior is again normal, and its mean is a precision-weighted average of what you believed and what you saw:

$$
\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\frac{\mu_0}{\tau^2} + \frac{n\bar{x}}{\sigma^2}}{\frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}},
\qquad
\sigma_{\text{post}}^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1}
$$

Two things fall out of this formula that every quant should internalize:

1. **Evidence accumulates.** As $n$ grows, the data term dominates and the posterior collapses onto $\bar{x}$. With little data, the prior does the heavy lifting.
2. **In this model, uncertainty shrinks with every observation.** Precision simply adds, so the posterior variance is smaller than both the prior variance and the data-only variance. That is a property of this conjugate normal model with known $\sigma$, not of Bayesian updating in general: with an unknown variance or heavy-tailed data, one surprising observation can *widen* the posterior. What holds generally is weaker — averaged over the datasets you might see, the posterior variance is no larger than the prior variance.

## Beyond normal–normal: conjugate priors

The drift example was friendly because a normal prior times a normal likelihood is again normal. That property — the posterior staying in the *same family* as the prior — is called **conjugacy**, and it turns Bayesian updating into arithmetic on the prior's parameters. Three pairings cover a huge share of everyday modeling:

| Data / likelihood | Conjugate prior | Posterior update (in words) |
|---|---|---|
| Binary outcomes — Binomial(n, p) | **Beta**(α, β) | add successes to α, failures to β |
| Counts — Poisson(λ) | **Gamma**(α, β) | add the total count to α, exposure to β |
| K categories — Multinomial | **Dirichlet**(α₁ … αₖ) | add each category count cₖ to its αₖ |

**Beta–Binomial.** Model a hit rate $p$ (a signal's win probability, a fill rate) with a $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ prior. Observe $s$ successes in $n$ trials and the posterior is simply

$$
p \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + s,\; \beta + n - s).
$$

The prior behaves like $\alpha+\beta$ "pseudo-observations" you had already seen — a clean dial for how much the prior should count against the real data.

**Gamma–Poisson.** For event counts (trades per hour, jumps per day) with rate $\lambda$ and a $\text{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)$ prior, observing counts $x_1,\dots,x_n$ gives

$$
\lambda \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \textstyle\sum_i x_i,\; \beta + n\right).
$$

Because a Poisson's variance equals its mean, the Gamma–Poisson also stretches into the **negative-binomial** when real counts are overdispersed.

**Dirichlet–Multinomial.** The multi-category generalization of Beta–Binomial: a Dirichlet prior over category probabilities becomes $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1 + c_1,\dots,\alpha_K + c_K)$ after observing counts $c_k$. Handy for regime classification or discrete state models.

Conjugacy is a computational luxury, not a modeling requirement — but when it is available, the update is exact and instant.

## Choosing a prior

A prior is an assumption, so state it deliberately. The usual options, from least to most opinionated:

- **Diffuse / uninformative** — deliberately vague (a very wide normal, a $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ uniform), meant to "let the data speak." Convenient, but a flat prior is not the same as *no* assumption: flatness on one scale is informative on another (a uniform prior on a variance is not uniform on the standard deviation).
- **Weakly informative** — the sensible default. Rule out the absurd (drifts of ±50% per day, correlations outside the plausible range) while staying gentle inside it. Weakly informative priors *regularize*: they pull wild estimates back toward sanity, exactly like ridge regression.
- **Maximum entropy** — when you want the *least committal* distribution consistent with what you actually know (a mean, a variance, a support), pick the one with the highest entropy. This is why the normal is the natural choice given only a mean and variance, and the exponential given only a positive mean — a MaxEnt prior bakes in your constraints and nothing more.

The lab above makes the stakes tangible: $\tau$ *is* the prior's strength. Dogmatic priors resist data; diffuse priors surrender to it. Neither is "correct" — the honest move is to choose consciously and show that your conclusions survive a reasonable change of prior (a **prior-sensitivity** check).

## Predicting new data: the posterior predictive

A posterior over $\theta$ is rarely the end goal — you want to predict the *next observation* $\tilde{y}$. Averaging the likelihood over the *full* posterior (not just a point estimate) gives the **posterior predictive distribution**:

$$
P(\tilde{y} \mid \text{data}) = \int P(\tilde{y}\mid\theta)\, P(\theta\mid\text{data})\, d\theta.
$$

It carries *two* sources of uncertainty — the noise in a fresh draw **and** your remaining uncertainty about $\theta$ — which is why plugging in a single "best" $\theta$ always understates risk. Its sibling, the **prior predictive**, runs the same integral against the prior and is the best sanity check you have *before* touching the data: simulate fake datasets from the prior and ask whether they look remotely like reality. If your prior predictive coughs up 300%-per-day returns, fix the prior, not the data.

## Computing posteriors without conjugacy

Most real models are not conjugate. Three escalating tools:

1. **Grid approximation.** Chop the parameter space into a fine grid, evaluate prior × likelihood at each point, and normalize. Exact in the limit and wonderfully intuitive — but the grid explodes with the number of parameters (the curse of dimensionality), so it is a teaching tool, not a workhorse.
2. **Quadratic (Laplace) approximation.** Find the posterior mode and fit a Gaussian using the curvature there. Cheap and often excellent when the posterior is roughly bell-shaped near its peak; the basis of the classic "MAP estimate ± standard error."
3. **MCMC** — when the posterior is high-dimensional or badly shaped, sample from it directly. That is the next section.

## When there's no formula: MCMC

The normal–normal example is the friendly special case — the posterior has a closed form. Most real models don't cooperate: a stochastic-volatility model or a hierarchical model of many assets produces a posterior you can write down only up to that proportionality sign. **Markov Chain Monte Carlo** solves this by constructing a Markov chain whose stationary distribution *is* the posterior: you take a random walk through parameter space, accepting or rejecting proposed moves (Metropolis–Hastings) so that the chain spends time in each region in proportion to its posterior probability. Run the chain long enough, discard the burn-in, and the samples you collect are draws from the posterior — histograms of those samples give you means, credible intervals, and tail probabilities no formula could. Modern samplers like NUTS (used by PyMC and Stan) automate the hard parts.

Why is a histogram of samples allowed to stand in for the posterior at all? The **[Law of Large Numbers](/docs/Probability/distributions/sampling-and-estimation/law-of-large-numbers)**: as you collect more draws, their sample average converges to the true expectation, so a posterior mean, tail probability, or credible interval computed from enough samples is as accurate as you like. That is the **Monte Carlo** principle every sampler leans on — trade an intractable integral for an average over draws.

## Summarizing the posterior: point estimates and credible intervals

Once you hold posterior draws — from a formula or from MCMC — you *summarize* them. A single number (the posterior **mean**, **median**, or **mode/MAP**) is a point estimate, but point estimates throw away exactly what makes the Bayesian answer valuable: the shape of your uncertainty. Prefer an interval.

A **credible interval** is the Bayesian cousin of the confidence interval — with the interpretation people wrongly wish confidence intervals had: a 95% credible interval genuinely carries a 95% posterior probability of containing the parameter. Two ways to draw one:

- **Equal-tailed interval (ETI)** — chop 2.5% off each tail. Simple and reported by default, but for a skewed posterior it can exclude the most probable values while keeping less probable ones.
- **Highest posterior density interval (HPDI)** — the *narrowest* interval holding 95% of the mass, so every point inside is more probable than every point outside. It is the honest summary for skewed or bounded posteriors (a variance, or a probability near 0 or 1). For a symmetric posterior the two coincide.

## Making the call: the ROPE and decision tables

For actual decisions, credible intervals pair beautifully with a **Region of Practical Equivalence (ROPE)** — a band around a null value (say $[-0.1, +0.1]$) you'd treat as "practically zero." Rather than testing whether an effect is *exactly* zero (almost never true in markets), you ask how much of the posterior lands inside that practically-zero band:

$$
P(\theta \in \text{ROPE} \mid \text{data}) \;\begin{cases} > 0.99 & \Rightarrow \text{accept "no practical effect"} \\ < 0.01 & \Rightarrow \text{the effect is real} \\ \text{else} & \Rightarrow \textbf{inconclusive} \end{cases}
$$

This is the Bayesian replacement for **null-hypothesis significance testing (NHST)**. Classical NHST forces a binary verdict — reject or fail-to-reject — and can never *accept* a null or return "not enough data yet." The ROPE instead yields a **trichotomous decision table**: *accept*, *reject*, or the honest **undecided**.

That middle verdict — impossible to state cleanly with a p-value — is often the most useful answer a dataset can give, because it tells you to gather more data rather than force a false conclusion. I leaned on exactly this in a term project regressing NVDA on SPY and META: the peer stock's coefficient carried ~76% of its posterior inside the ROPE — plenty of mass near zero, but short of the 99% needed to *declare* it negligible. You can see it applied in [Regression Analysis](/docs/Statistics/regression-analysis).

More generally, a decision needs a **loss function** — the cost of each action under each possible truth. Bayesian **decision theory** says: pick the action that minimizes *expected* loss, averaging that loss over the full posterior. The ROPE rule is the simple, symmetric case; when the payoff is lopsided — a false "trade" costs more than a missed one — you encode that asymmetry in the loss and let the posterior choose. Point estimates fall out of the same idea: minimizing expected squared error gives the posterior mean, while absolute error gives the median.

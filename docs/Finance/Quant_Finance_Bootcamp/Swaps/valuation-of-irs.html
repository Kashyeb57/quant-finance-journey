Valuation of an Interest Rate Swap

At inception a swap is worth roughly zero to both sides. After that, rates move and it acquires value. This note computes that value.

To value an IRS you typically focus on the NPV of the future cash flows from the swap's fixed and floating legs.

Step 1 — identify the swap terms​

Term
Fixed rate
Floating ratethe benchmark interest rate used for the floating leg
Notional amountthe principal upon which interest payments are calculated
Payment frequencyquarterly, semi-annual, annual
Day count convention
Duration of the swap3 years, 5 years, …

Day count conventions​

How you convert "between these two dates" into a number to multiply a rate by:

ConventionMeaning
ACT/365actual number of days, over a 365-day year
ACT/ACTactual days over the actual year length — 2024 is a leap year → 366
30/36030 days in a month, 360 days in a year

15 January to 15 March under 30/360 is 30×2=6030 \times 2 = 6030×2=60 days, so 60/36060/36060/360 of a year. Under ACT it would be the true 59 days. Small differences, but they compound across a large notional.

Discounting CF=e−rT,T=time between two consecutive dates\text{Discounting CF} = e^{-rT}, \quad T = \text{time between two consecutive dates}Discounting CF=e−rT,T=time between two consecutive dates

Step 2 — forecast the future cash flows​

LegHow
Fixed legthe rate remains the same for all the cash flows — trivial
Floating legdepends on the future value of the floating rate index, usually calculated using the forward curve
spot rate⟶forward rate⟶CF of the floating leg\text{spot rate} \longrightarrow \text{forward rate} \longrightarrow \text{CF of the floating leg}spot rate⟶forward rate⟶CF of the floating leg

This is why the forward rate derivation had to come first. You cannot forecast the floating leg without it.

Step 3 — determine the discount factors​

  DF=e−rt  \boxed{\;DF = e^{-rt}\;}DF=e−rt​

Discount factors are used to calculate the present value of the future cash flows.

Step 4 — calculate the present values​

PV=DF×CFPV = DF \times CFPV=DF×CF

done separately for the fixed leg and the floating leg, then summed across all payment dates.

Step 5 — compute the NPV​

  NPV=PV(floating leg)−PV(fixed leg)  \boxed{\;NPV = PV(\text{floating leg}) - PV(\text{fixed leg})\;}NPV=PV(floating leg)−PV(fixed leg)​
NPVMeaning
Positivethe value of the floating leg exceeds the fixed leg → beneficial for the party receiving floating
Negativethe fixed leg value exceeds the floating leg → beneficial for the party receiving fixed

The bond-pair method​

There is a second route, and the workbook's Swap Rate of IRS sheet uses it:

An interest rate swap is equivalent to being long one bond and short another.

Pay fixed, receive floating = short a fixed-rate bond, long a floating-rate bond. Both bonds have the same notional and dates, so the principals cancel — leaving exactly the swap's net interest flows.

On a 2-year, $10M swap with the SOFR curve 3%, 4%, 4%, 3.5%:

Value
Fixed-rate bond PV$9,924,545.11
Floating-rate bond PV$9,924,545.18
Value to pay fixed / receive float≈ 0
Swap rate3.144490%

The swap rate is the fixed rate that makes the two bonds equal — i.e. the fixed rate at which the swap is worth zero at inception. That is the rate a dealer would quote.

The floating bond does not reprice to par, and the swap rate is 39.5bp off

The sheet carries the note "Floating rate bond reprices itself back to par." That is a genuine theoretical result — but this sheet's numbers contradict it.

The floating leg uses typed-in rates (2.5%, 3.5%, 3.5%, 3.1%) rather than the forwards implied by the SOFR curve it is discounted with. The result:

Floating bond PV
With the sheet's assumed rates$9,924,545 — 99.245% of par
With the forwards implied by the same curve$10,000,000.00 — exactly par

The implied forwards from that curve are 3%, 5%, 4%, 2% — quite different from the ones entered. Using them, the floating bond prices to par to the cent, confirming the note's claim.

This propagates into the swap rate. Since the swap rate is the fixed rate that matches the floating bond's value:

Swap rate
Sheet's answer (floating bond below par)3.144490%
Correct (floating bond at par)3.539535%
Difference39.5 basis points

The method is right, and the bond-pair framing is standard. But the floating leg must be built from the same curve used to discount it — otherwise you are quoting a swap rate against a bond mispriced by three quarters of a percent.

Summary​

  • Valuing an IRS: identify terms → forecast cash flows → discount factors → present values → NPV.
  • NPV=PV(float)−PV(fixed)NPV = PV(\text{float}) - PV(\text{fixed})NPV=PV(float)−PV(fixed). Positive favours the floating receiver; negative favours the fixed receiver.
  • The fixed leg is constant; the floating leg comes from the forward curve, which comes from the spot curve.
  • Day count conventions (ACT/365, ACT/ACT, 30/360) decide how a date gap becomes a fraction of a year.
  • Worked: 1.25 years remaining, $100M, 3% fixed → forwards of 3.4291% and 3.7344% semi-annual → NPV +$511,732, verified independently.
  • A swap is a pair of bonds — pay fixed / receive float = short a fixed bond, long a floating one. The swap rate equalises them.
  • The workbook's floating bond prices at 99.245% of par, not par, because its floating rates were typed rather than derived from its own discount curve — putting the quoted swap rate 39.5bp below the correct 3.539535%.

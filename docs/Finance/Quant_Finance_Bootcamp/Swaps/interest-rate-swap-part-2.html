The Interest Rate Swap

The most common swap by far: one party pays a fixed rate, the other pays a floating rate, both on the same notional.

Fixed→never changesFloating→always changes\textbf{Fixed} \rightarrow \text{never changes} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Floating} \rightarrow \text{always changes}Fixed→never changesFloating→always changes

Floating rates in practice are a benchmark plus a spread — the notes list SOFR, SOFR + 0.5%, US Treasury + 1%, Fed Funds. (The example below uses LIBOR, which was the standard benchmark at the time and has since been replaced by SOFR.)

The worked example​

A hypothetical 3-year swap initiated on 8 March 2017 between Apple and Citigroup, on a $100M notional:

Apple agrees to pay3% per annum on $100M
Citigroup agrees to pay6-month LIBOR on $100M
Paymentssemi-annual — the first on 8 September 2017

So Apple is the fixed-rate payer and Citigroup is the floating-rate payer. Six exchanges over three years.

The cash flows, from Apple's side​

DateLIBORFloating CF receivedFixed CF paidNet
8 Mar 20172.2%———
8 Sep 20172.8%+$1.10M−$1.50M−$0.40M
8 Mar 20183.3%+$1.40M−$1.50M−$0.10M
8 Sep 20183.5%+$1.65M−$1.50M+$0.15M
8 Mar 20193.6%+$1.75M−$1.50M+$0.25M
8 Sep 20193.9%+$1.80M−$1.50M+$0.30M
8 Mar 2020—+$1.95M−$1.50M+$0.45M

Apple received $0.65M over the life of the swap.

Where each number comes from​

The fixed leg is trivial and identical every period:

Fixed CF=−3%2×$100M=−$1.5M\text{Fixed CF} = -\frac{3\%}{2} \times \$100\text{M} = -\$1.5\text{M}Fixed CF=−23%​×$100M=−$1.5M

Divided by 2 because payments are semi-annual — an annual rate applied to a half-year period.

The floating leg uses the previous period's LIBOR:

Floating CF=LIBORprevious date2×$100M\text{Floating CF} = \frac{\text{LIBOR}_{\text{previous date}}}{2} \times \$100\text{M}Floating CF=2LIBORprevious date​​×$100M

So the payment on 8 Sep 2017 uses the 2.2% observed on 8 Mar 2017, not the 2.8% observed on the payment date itself: 2.2% / 2 x $100M = $1.1M.

Set in advance, paid in arrears

This one-period lag is the standard convention and it is easy to misread from the table. The floating rate for a period is fixed at the start of that period and paid at the end of it.

The reason is practical: both parties need to know what they owe before the period runs, so the amount is locked in at the beginning. It also means the very first floating payment is known with certainty on day one — a fact the valuation note depends on, because that payment needs no forecasting.

It also explains why the 8 Mar 2020 row has no LIBOR in the table: the swap ends there, so no new rate is set. Its payment uses the 3.9% from 8 Sep 2019.

What the swap actually did​

Look at the net column: negative for the first two periods, positive for the last four. Apple locked in 3% while LIBOR started at 2.2% and climbed to 3.9%.

Apple paid slightly more than it received while rates were low, then received more as rates rose past 3%. The break-even is exactly where LIBOR crosses the fixed rate — below it the fixed payer loses, above it the fixed payer gains.

Which is the whole point of the instrument. Apple was not predicting rates; it was converting a floating exposure into a fixed one (or vice versa). The $0.65M is what that conversion happened to be worth given how rates actually moved — it was not the objective.

Transforming a liability or an asset​

The workbook carries four further scenario sheets — Swap_transform_liability_Eg1 and Eg2, Swap_transform_Asset_Eg1 and Eg2 — which are the two standard uses:

UseWhat the swap does
Transform a liabilityA firm with floating-rate debt pays fixed in the swap → its net obligation becomes fixed
Transform an assetA firm holding a fixed-rate asset receives floating in the swap → its net income becomes floating

In both cases the underlying debt or asset is untouched. The swap sits on top and flips the rate character of the cash flows, which is far cheaper than retiring the debt and reissuing it.

Summary​

  • An interest rate swap exchanges a fixed rate for a floating rate on the same notional. Fixed never changes; floating always does.
  • Worked: Apple pays 3% fixed, Citigroup pays 6-month LIBOR, $100M notional, semi-annual, 3 years. Apple netted +$0.65M.
  • The fixed leg is rate/2×notional\text{rate}/2 \times \text{notional}rate/2×notional every period; the floating leg uses the rate set at the start of the period, paid at the end.
  • Set in advance, paid in arrears — so the first floating payment is known from day one.
  • Apple's net turns from negative to positive exactly where LIBOR crosses 3%.
  • Swaps are used to transform a liability (floating debt → fixed) or transform an asset (fixed income → floating), without touching the underlying instrument.

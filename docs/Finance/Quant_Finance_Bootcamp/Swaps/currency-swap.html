The Currency Swap

The interest rate swap exchanged one kind of interest for another in the same currency. A currency swap crosses the currency boundary, and that one change alters the mechanics considerably.

Definition​

A currency swap is a financial derivative that commits 2 counterparties to exchange streams of interest payments and principal payments in different currencies.

Currency swaps are typically used by institutions to manage exposure to foreign exchange.

The problem it solves​

An Indian company owes $100,000 three months from now. Today the bank quotes 1 USD = ₹85, so the obligation is ₹8,500,000. But the rate moves every day — 82, 84, 85, 86 — and in three months it could be anything.

The amount should not be reduced.

The company does not want its rupee cost floating around with the exchange rate. A currency swap fixes the terms of the exchange in advance, converting an uncertain FX exposure into a known schedule.

The three-stage structure​

Unlike an IRS, a currency swap exchanges principal as well as interest — and it does so twice.

1) Initial exchange of principal.

At the inception of the swap, the principal amounts in 2 different currencies are exchanged between the counterparties at a spot rate.

2) Exchange of interest payments.

Interest payments between the 2 counterparties are exchanged — on each payment date through the life of the swap.

3) Re-exchange of principal.

At the maturity of the swap, the original principal amounts are swapped back at the same exchange rate as at the inception, regardless of the prevailing market exchange rate.

t=0⏟principal exchanged  →  t=1,2,3,4⏟interest exchanged  →  t=5⏟principal + final interest\underbrace{t=0}_{\text{principal exchanged}} \;\rightarrow\; \underbrace{t=1,2,3,4}_{\text{interest exchanged}} \;\rightarrow\; \underbrace{t=5}_{\text{principal + final interest}}principal exchangedt=0​​→interest exchangedt=1,2,3,4​​→principal + final interestt=5​​
This is why the principal is real here

In an interest rate swap both legs are in the same currency, so exchanging identical principals would be pointless — they would cancel. Hence "notional".

In a currency swap the two principals are in different currencies, so they do not cancel. Exchanging them is the entire point: it is how each party gets the currency it actually needs.

And because the re-exchange happens at the inception rate regardless of the prevailing market rate, the FX risk over the swap's life is removed — which is precisely what the Indian importer above was trying to achieve.

Types of currency swap​

TypeStructure
1Fixed for FixedBoth parties exchange fixed interest payments
2Fixed for FloatingOne party pays a fixed rate, the other pays a floating rate
3Floating for FloatingBoth parties exchange floating interest payments — in different currencies

Worked example — fixed for fixed​

A hypothetical 5-year currency swap, initiated 1 February 2017:

British Petroleum pays3% fixed on $15 million
BP receives4% fixed on £10 million
Paymentsonce a year

Cash flows to BP:

DateDollar cash flowSterling cash flow
1 Feb 2017+$15M−£10Minitial exchange
1 Feb 2018−$0.45M+£0.40M
1 Feb 2019−$0.45M+£0.40M
1 Feb 2020−$0.45M+£0.40M
1 Feb 2021−$0.45M+£0.40M
1 Feb 2022−$15.45M+£10.40Mfinal interest + re-exchange

At inception BP pays £10 million and receives $15 million — implying an exchange rate of 1.50 dollars per pound, locked in for the life of the swap.

The interest amounts are straightforward: 3%×15=0.453\% \times 15 = 0.453%×15=0.45 and 4%×10=0.404\% \times 10 = 0.404%×10=0.40, annually. The final row bundles the last interest payment with the principal going back the other way.

Notice the signs are opposite across the two columns at every date. BP is short dollars and long sterling throughout, which is the exposure it wanted.

The same two-bond framing as the IRS valuation — but now the two bonds are in different currencies, so one leg has to be converted before they can be netted.

Where the value comes from

Two forces are at work, and they pull in opposite directions.

The yen leg pays a lower coupon (3% vs 4%) — which hurts. But the yen discount rate is also lower (1.5% vs 2.5%) — which helps, because a lower discount rate raises present value.

Here the discounting effect wins. The yen bond's coupon of 3% sits above its 1.5% discount rate, so it prices above par; the dollar bond's 4% coupon sits above its 2.5% rate too, but by a smaller margin relative to its size. Converted at the current rate, the yen leg is worth more — hence the positive value.

And note what is not in this calculation: any forecast of the exchange rate. The valuation uses today's spot rate to convert, and the two interest rate curves to discount. That is the no-arbitrage approach — an FX forecast would introduce a view, and a valuation is not supposed to have one.

Summary​

  • A currency swap exchanges interest payments and principal in two different currencies, and is used to manage foreign exchange exposure.
  • Three stages: initial exchange of principal at spot → exchange of interest through the life → re-exchange of principal at maturity, at the original inception rate, whatever the market rate has become.
  • The principal really is exchanged, unlike an IRS, because the two principals are in different currencies and do not cancel.
  • Three types: fixed-for-fixed, fixed-for-floating, floating-for-floating.
  • Worked: BP pays 3% on $15M, receives 4% on £10M for 5 years — principals swapped at inception and returned at maturity.
  • Valued as two bonds in two currencies: PV each leg on its own curve, convert at the spot rate, and net. Worked to +$962,788, verified.
  • The valuation needs no exchange-rate forecast — only today's spot rate and the two interest rate curves.

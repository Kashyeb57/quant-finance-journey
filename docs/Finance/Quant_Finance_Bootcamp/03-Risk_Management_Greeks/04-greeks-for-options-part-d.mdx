Delta and gamma track the stock. Theta tracks the calendar. **Vega tracks
uncertainty itself.**

## Definition

> Vega measures the sensitivity of the option price with respect to **implied
> volatility** — how much the price of the option will change for a **1% change
> in the implied volatility** of the asset.

$$
\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}
$$

where $V$ is the option price and $\sigma$ the volatility, so $\partial \sigma$
is the change in volatility.

### Implied versus historical volatility

The distinction is simply which direction in time you are looking:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Historical volatility** | Volatility of the **past** — what the asset actually did |
| **Implied volatility** | Volatility of the **future** — what the market currently expects |

Vega is about the **implied** one. Historical volatility is measured; implied
volatility is a market expectation baked into today's option prices, and it can
sit above or below the historical figure.

Volatility itself gets modelled two broad ways:

| Family | Models |
| --- | --- |
| **Time-series models** | ARCH, GARCH, EWMA (and the wider AR, MA, ARIMA, ARIMAX, SARIMA, VAR family) |
| **Stochastic-volatility models** | Heston, SABR, the 3/2 model |

### A worked reading

A call option:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Stock price | \$100 |
| Strike | \$100 |
| Time to expiry | 30 days |
| **Vega** | **0.15** |

> For every **1% increase in implied volatility**, the option price will
> **increase by \$0.15**.

| | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **High vega** | The option price is **very sensitive** to volatility — a small increase in volatility raises the option price significantly |
| **Low vega** | Changes in volatility have only a **small impact** on the price |

## Vega is never negative

> Vega is **always non-negative**.

Rising volatility **always** raises an option's value — call or put, in or out
of the money. The reason is the payoff's asymmetry, and it is worth doing
properly because it explains the entire Greek.

### Why volatility helps the long — a call

A long call pays $\max(S_T - K,\, 0)$, with $K = 100$. Suppose higher volatility
means the stock could land at **120** or **80** instead of staying near 100:

| Outcome | Payoff |
| --- | --- |
| $S_T = 120$ | $\max(120 - 100,\, 0) = \mathbf{20}$ |
| $S_T = 80$ | $\max(80 - 100,\, 0) = \mathbf{0}$ |

Up, you exercise and profit. Down, you simply **do not exercise** — no profit,
no loss beyond the premium already paid. The upside is open-ended; **the
downside is floored at zero**. More volatility therefore adds only upside.

### And a put

A long put pays $\max(K - S_T,\, 0)$, same $K = 100$:

| Outcome | Payoff |
| --- | --- |
| $S_T = 120$ | $\max(100 - 120,\, 0) = \mathbf{0}$ |
| $S_T = 80$ | $\max(100 - 80,\, 0) = \mathbf{20}$ |

Identical logic, mirrored. Which gives the rule:

$$
\text{Long position (call or put)} \Rightarrow \text{vega is your friend}
$$
$$
\text{Short position} \Rightarrow \text{vega works against you}
$$

The short side wrote that floored payoff, so every increase in volatility hands
value to the buyer at the seller's expense.

## Vega and moneyness

| Moneyness | Vega |
| --- | --- |
| **ATM** | **Highest** |
| **ITM** | Low |
| **OTM** | Low |

**Why ATM.** At the money, $S_T = K$ — the option sits exactly on the knife
edge. Even a small change in volatility can tip it either way: to 101 and it is
ITM, to 99 and it is OTM. That balance is where extra uncertainty is worth the
most.

**Why not ITM.** A deep in-the-money option is already going to be exercised;
its value is mostly intrinsic. More volatility barely moves that, so the impact
on the option price is small.

**Why not OTM.** Far out of the money, the option is unlikely to be exercised
regardless, so extra volatility changes little.

Note this is the **same bell shape as gamma** — both peak at the money, for the
same underlying reason: that is where the option's fate is least decided.

## Vega and time to expiry

| Maturity | Vega |
| --- | --- |
| **Long-term** (e.g. 3 months) | **High** |
| **Short-term** (e.g. 5 days) | **Low** |

Short duration means the stock has less room to travel, so the option price is
**less impacted** by a change in volatility.

> Think of **bouncing a ball**. Dropped from high up it bounces high — that is
> **high vega**. Held near the floor and released, it barely bounces — **low
> vega**. Volatility needs *room*, and room is time.

## How vega compares with the others

Worth holding the four together, because their behaviour with time diverges:

| | More time to expiry | Peak at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Delta** | Higher | — (monotonic S-curve) |
| **Gamma** | **Lower** | **ATM** |
| **Theta** | Lower per day (until it accelerates near expiry) | **ATM** |
| **Vega** | **Higher** | **ATM** |

Gamma and vega share the ATM peak but move in **opposite** directions with time:
a short-dated ATM option is a gamma instrument, a long-dated ATM option is a
vega instrument.

## Summary

- $\mathcal{V} = \partial V / \partial \sigma$ — the price change per **1%**
  move in **implied** volatility. Vega 0.15 means +\$0.15 per point of IV.
- **Implied** volatility is the market's view of the future; **historical** is
  the measured past.
- **Vega is always non-negative**, because the option payoff is floored at zero:
  volatility adds upside without adding downside.
- **Long is long vega** (calls and puts alike); **short is short vega**.
- **Vega is highest ATM** — the knife edge — and low both ITM and OTM.
- **Long-dated options carry high vega; short-dated carry little** — the
  bouncing ball needs height.

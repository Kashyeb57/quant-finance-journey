[Delta hedging](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/delta-hedging)
kills direction but leaves gamma.
[Gamma hedging](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/gamma-hedging)
stabilises delta but leaves direction. **Delta-gamma hedging does both.**

## Definition

> Delta-gamma neutral hedging is a risk-management strategy which aims to
> **neutralise both delta and gamma**.

$$
\Delta_p = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Gamma_p = 0
$$

And each of the two conditions has a plain-English reading:

| Condition | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Delta neutral** ($\Delta_p = 0$) | The portfolio's total delta is zero — it is **not sensitive to small price changes** in the underlying asset |
| **Gamma neutral** ($\Gamma_p = 0$) | The portfolio's total gamma is zero — **delta does not change** with a change in the underlying asset |

Put together, the portfolio is protected against **small *and* large** moves.
Delta neutrality handles the small ones; gamma neutrality is what keeps that
protection standing when the move is big.

## Worked example

**Long 200 call options on XYZ stock**, with:

- **Delta = 0.4**
- **Gamma = 0.05**

Goal: make the portfolio **delta-gamma neutral.**

### Step 1 — the portfolio's Greeks

$$
\Delta_p = 200 \times 0.4 = \mathbf{80}
\qquad\qquad
\Gamma_p = 200 \times 0.05 = \mathbf{10}
$$

### Step 2 — make the portfolio delta neutral

Delta is +80, so **sell 80 shares** (delta −80):

$$
\text{Portfolio} = \text{long 200 calls} \;+\; \text{short 80 stock}
$$

$$
\Delta_p = 80 - 80 = \mathbf{0} \quad \Rightarrow\ \textbf{delta neutral}
$$

### Step 3 — make the portfolio gamma neutral

The portfolio gamma is still **+10**, and stock contributes nothing to it. To
cancel +10 we need **−10**, and negative gamma comes only from the short side:

| Gamma | Position |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive** | **Long call, long put** |
| **Negative** | **Short call, short put** |

From the market we find a **call option contract** which, taken **short**,
carries:

$$
\Delta = -0.3 \qquad\qquad \Gamma = -0.05
$$

Short **200** of them:

$$
200 \times (-0.05) = \mathbf{-10}
$$

The portfolio's gamma, leg by leg:

$$
\Gamma_p = \underbrace{+10}_{\text{long 200 calls}}
\;+\; \underbrace{0}_{\text{short 80 stock}}
\;+\; \underbrace{-10}_{\text{short 200 calls}}
\;=\; \mathbf{0}
$$

**Gamma neutrality achieved.**

### Step 4 — the option leg broke the delta, so fix it again

This is the step people forget. Entering that call option contract **changed the
portfolio's delta**, because the option we just shorted carries delta as well as
gamma:

$$
\Delta_p = \underbrace{200 \times (-0.3)}_{\text{the new short calls}} \;+\; \underbrace{0}_{\text{already-neutral portfolio}} = \mathbf{-60}
$$

We were at zero; we are now at **−60**. So **buy 60 shares** (+60 delta):

$$
\Delta_p = -60 + 60 = \mathbf{0}
$$

And buying stock does not disturb gamma, because stock has none. Final state:

$$
\Delta_p = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Gamma_p = 0
$$

**That is delta-gamma hedging.**

## The ordering rule

The example teaches a sequence, and the sequence is not arbitrary:

1. **Hedge gamma first, with options.** Only an option can change gamma.
2. **Hedge delta second, with stock.** Stock changes delta and *nothing else*.

The reason is that the two instruments are not symmetric:

| Instrument | Affects delta | Affects gamma |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stock** | ✅ (±1 per share) | ❌ (always 0) |
| **Option** | ✅ | ✅ |

Because the option leg moves **both** Greeks, it must be sized first — from the
gamma requirement, which stock cannot help with. The stock leg is then a pure
correction: it mops up whatever delta the option leg left behind, without
touching the gamma you just fixed.

Do it the other way around — delta first, then gamma — and the gamma trade
knocks your delta back out of neutral, which is exactly what happened between
Step 2 and Step 4 above. Step 2 was not wasted (it is how the bookkeeping is
kept legible), but the *final* delta number can only be computed once the option
leg is set.

## Summary

- **Delta-gamma hedging neutralises both Greeks at once**: $\Delta_p = 0$ and
  $\Gamma_p = 0$, protecting against **small and large** moves in the underlying.
- **Delta neutral** = insensitive to small price changes. **Gamma neutral** =
  delta itself stops changing.
- Worked: long 200 calls at $\Delta$ 0.4 / $\Gamma$ 0.05 → $\Delta_p = 80$,
  $\Gamma_p = 10$; short 80 stock; short 200 calls at $\Delta$ −0.3 /
  $\Gamma$ −0.05 to zero the gamma; then **buy 60 stock** to re-zero the delta
  the option leg introduced.
- **Gamma is hedged with options, delta with stock** — and because options carry
  delta too, the **option leg is sized first and the stock leg last**.

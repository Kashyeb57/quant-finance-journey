[Delta hedging](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/delta-hedging)
ended on a problem: it removes directional risk but **introduces gamma risk**,
because a delta-neutral portfolio is only neutral at the current price. Gamma
hedging attacks that.

## What gamma hedging is

> Gamma hedging is a risk-management strategy that aims to **neutralize or
> reduce gamma risk** in an option position.

**Gamma risk** is the sensitivity of an option's **delta** with respect to a
change in the underlying asset price:

$$
\Gamma = \frac{\text{change in delta}}{\text{change in the underlying asset}}
$$

The goal, stated plainly: *even though my asset price changes, it should not
affect my gamma* — that is, my delta should stay put.

### Position gamma

An option's own gamma is positive, but the **position's** gamma takes the sign
of the side you are on:

| Position gamma | Positions |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive** | **Long call, long put** |
| **Negative** | **Short call, short put** |

That is the lever: to cancel positive gamma you need to **sell** options.

## Worked example

You own **10 call option contracts** with:

- **Delta = 0.5**
- **Gamma = 0.02**

### Step 1 — portfolio delta and gamma

$$
\text{Total delta} = 10 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{5}
\qquad\qquad
\text{Total gamma} = 10 \times 0.02 = \mathbf{0.2}
$$

### Step 2 — neutralise delta

Delta is 5, so **sell 5 shares** (delta −5):

$$
\Delta_p = 5 - 5 = \mathbf{0}
$$

Portfolio so far: **long 10 calls + short 5 stock**.

### Step 3 — neutralise gamma

The portfolio gamma is **+0.2**, so we need **−0.2**, which means going **short
an option**. Suppose we short **put option contracts** with **gamma = −0.04**
each:

$$
5 \times (-0.04) = \mathbf{-0.2}
$$

So short **5 put option contracts**. The full portfolio is now:

$$
P = \underbrace{10 \text{ calls}}_{\Gamma = +0.2}
\;+\; \underbrace{\text{short 5 stock}}_{\Gamma = 0}
\;+\; \underbrace{\text{short 5 puts}}_{\Gamma = -0.2}
$$

$$
\Gamma_p = 0.2 + 0 + (-0.2) = \mathbf{0}
$$

**The portfolio is gamma neutral.**

Note the shape of the solution: the stock leg fixed **delta** and contributed
nothing to gamma; the option leg fixed **gamma**. Which raises the obvious
question.

## Why stock cannot hedge gamma

> **Stocks do not have gamma — but they do have delta.**

And this is provable in one line. Gamma is the rate of change of delta with
respect to the underlying:

$$
\Gamma = \frac{\partial (\Delta)}{\partial S}
$$

For a share, the delta is **always ±1** — +1 if you buy it, −1 if you sell it.
It never changes, no matter what the price does. So the delta is a **constant**,
and the derivative of a constant is zero:

$$
\Gamma_{\text{stock}} = \frac{\partial (\pm 1)}{\partial S} = \mathbf{0}
$$

The practical consequence:

- **Buying or selling stock has no impact on the portfolio's gamma.**
- **But it does affect the portfolio's delta**, by ±1 per share.

Compare with an option, whose delta of (say) 0.5 *does* move as the stock moves
— that changing delta is exactly what gamma measures. So gamma can only be
hedged with **another option**, never with the underlying.

## Delta hedging versus gamma hedging

They are not the same objective, and conflating them is the common mistake:

| | **Delta hedging** | **Gamma hedging** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aim** | Make the delta of the portfolio **zero** ($\Delta = 0$) | **Stabilise** the delta ($\Gamma = 0$) — the delta may stay at 0.4, it just stops moving |
| **Directional bias** | **Eliminates** directional risk | **Reduces the sensitivity** of delta to changes in the asset |
| **Instrument** | Stock | **Another option** |

> The primary goal of gamma hedging is to **neutralise gamma** — to neutralise
> the change in delta with respect to a change in the underlying — **rather than
> to make delta zero.**

Which means gamma hedging on its own leaves you **still carrying directional
bias**: you can have $\Gamma = 0$ while $\Delta \neq 0$. A stable delta is not
the same as no delta.

Hedging both at once is the subject of the next note.

## Summary

- **Gamma hedging neutralises gamma risk** — the risk that your delta itself
  moves as the underlying moves.
- **Position gamma is positive when long options, negative when short**, so
  positive gamma is cancelled by **selling** options.
- Worked: 10 calls at $\Delta$ 0.5 / $\Gamma$ 0.02 → portfolio $\Delta = 5$,
  $\Gamma = 0.2$; short 5 stock zeroes delta; short 5 puts at $\Gamma = -0.04$
  zeroes gamma.
- **Stock has zero gamma** because its delta is the constant ±1 and the
  derivative of a constant is zero — so **gamma must be hedged with options.**
- Delta hedging makes delta **zero**; gamma hedging makes delta **stable**. They
  are different goals, and $\Gamma = 0$ does not imply $\Delta = 0$.

Delta, gamma and delta-gamma hedging all defend against the **price** of the
underlying. Vega hedging defends against something else entirely: **the market's
expectation of how much that price will move.**

## Definition

> Vega hedging is a risk-management strategy which is used to manage the
> sensitivity of options to a **change in the implied volatility** of the
> underlying asset.

Hedging vega means the trader is trying to **eliminate the impact of volatility
fluctuations on the option price / portfolio.**

The transmission chain is worth stating, because it is why vega exists at all:

$$
\text{volatility} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{stock price} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{option price}
$$

Volatility moves the stock around, and the stock's movement is what the option
is priced on. Vega hedging cuts the link at the first arrow.

> **The goal is to neutralise the vega** — meaning the portfolio value should not
> be impacted by the change in implied volatility.

$$
\mathcal{V}_p = 0
$$

## The sign of vega

Same structure as gamma, and for the same reason — it is the *side* you are on
that sets the sign:

| Vega | Positions |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive** | **Long call, long put** |
| **Negative** | **Short call, short put** |

The payoff algebra makes it obvious. A long call pays

$$
\max(S_T - K,\, 0)
$$

and the short call is simply the negative of it:

$$
-\max(S_T - K,\, 0)
$$

The buyer's floored, open-ended payoff is exactly what makes
[vega non-negative](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/greeks-for-options-part-d)
for the long — so the seller, holding the mirror image, has the sign flipped.

### When vega is negative

> **When you sell an option (call or put), you lose money if volatility rises.**

That is the entire meaning of negative vega, and it is also what makes the hedge
possible: to cancel a positive vega you need somebody's negative vega, and the
only way to get it is to **sell an option**.

## Worked example

A portfolio holds **200 option contracts** with a **vega of 5** each.

### Step 1 — the portfolio vega

$$
\mathcal{V}_p = 200 \times 5 = \mathbf{1000}
$$

Which reads, in both directions:

| Change in implied volatility | Effect on the portfolio |
| --- | --- |
| **Increases by 1%** | The option price would **increase by \$1,000** |
| **Decreases by 1%** | The option price would **decrease by \$1,000** |

Note that this is a risk, not a bonus. A position that gains \$1,000 on a
one-point rise in IV loses \$1,000 on a one-point fall — and IV is not something
you control.

### Step 2 — find negative vega

The portfolio vega is **+1000**, so we need **−1000**. From the sign table, a
negative-vega exposure means a **short call** or a **short put**.

Suppose we go **short a put option** with **vega = −2**, and short **500** of
them:

$$
500 \times (-2) = \mathbf{-1000}
$$

### Step 3 — total

$$
\mathcal{V}_{\text{total}} = +1000 - 1000 = \mathbf{0}
$$

**The portfolio is vega neutral.** A change in implied volatility now has no
effect on its value.

## When vega hedging matters

The same conditional logic as gamma hedging, driven this time by the volatility
regime rather than the size of price moves.

**In a low-volatility market**, option prices are small, so the **gains and
losses are small** too. There is not much vega exposure to remove, and paying to
remove it is hard to justify.

**In a higher-volatility environment** — an **earnings announcement**, or some
other market event — vega neutrality earns its keep by **protecting the
portfolio from sudden volatility changes.** These are precisely the moments when
implied volatility gaps rather than drifts.

**Market stability.** When markets are stable (a low-volatility environment),
**vega hedging may not be necessary.** It is **high uncertainty** that calls for
it.

Which is a familiar shape by now: every Greek hedge is worth its cost only when
the thing it protects against is actually moving.

## Summary

- **Vega hedging neutralises exposure to implied volatility**: $\mathcal{V}_p = 0$
  means the **portfolio value will not change even if implied volatility does.**
- **Vega is positive when long** (long call, long put) and **negative when
  short** (short call, short put) — selling an option means **losing money if
  volatility rises.**
- Worked: 200 contracts at vega 5 → $\mathcal{V}_p = 1000$ (±\$1,000 per point of
  IV), neutralised by shorting **500 puts at vega −2**.
- The hedge instrument must be an **option** — and specifically a **short**
  one, since that is the only source of negative vega.
- It matters around **earnings and market events**, and is largely unnecessary
  in a **stable, low-volatility market.**

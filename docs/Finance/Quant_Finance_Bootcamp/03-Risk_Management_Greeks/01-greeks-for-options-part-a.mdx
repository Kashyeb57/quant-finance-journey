## What the Greeks are

> The Greeks are **risk measures** used in options to assess the **sensitivity
> of the option price to various factors**.

An option's price does not depend on one thing. It moves with the underlying
asset, with time, with interest rates, and with volatility. Each Greek isolates
**one** of those and asks: if this input moves, how much does the option price
move?

There are five:

| Greek | Sensitivity of the option price with respect to… |
| --- | --- |
| **Delta** ($\Delta$) | the **underlying asset** |
| **Gamma** ($\Gamma$) | *(the sensitivity of **delta** to the underlying asset)* |
| **Theta** ($\Theta$) | **time** |
| **Rho** ($\rho$) | the **risk-free rate** |
| **Vega** | **implied volatility** |

Gamma is the odd one out: it is a sensitivity *of another Greek*, not of the
price directly.

## Delta

> Delta tells the sensitivity of the option price with respect to the underlying
> asset — or equivalently, the **rate of change of the option price relative to
> the change in the underlying asset**.

$$
\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
$$

where $V$ is the price of the option and $S$ is the price of the underlying
asset. So $\partial V$ is the change in the option price and $\partial S$ the
change in the underlying asset price.

### Why a partial derivative?

Because the option price is a function of **several** variables at once — the
asset price, volatility, the rate, time. A function of one variable gives an
**ordinary** derivative (an ODE, $dV/dS$); a function of many gives a
**partial** derivative (a PDE, $\partial V / \partial S$), which holds
everything else fixed and varies only $S$. Option pricing is the second case.

## The sign of delta

Delta can be positive or negative, and which one depends on **the type of
option** and **the position** you hold.

**Positive delta** — a positive relationship with the underlying:

- the option value **rises** when the stock price rises
- the option value **falls** when the stock price falls

**Negative delta** — a negative relationship:

- the option price **falls** when the underlying **rises**
- the option price **rises** when the underlying **falls**

A put has negative delta for exactly this reason: you hold the right to sell, so
a rising stock makes that right less valuable.

### Range, by option and by position

$$
\text{Call option: } 0 \text{ to } 1
\qquad\qquad
\text{Put option: } 0 \text{ to } -1
$$

Taking the position into account as well:

| Position | Delta range |
| --- | --- |
| **Long call** | 0 to **+1** |
| **Short call** | 0 to **−1** |
| **Long put** | 0 to **−1** |
| **Short put** | 0 to **+1** |

Laid on a number line, −1 is where long put and short call live; +1 is where
long call and short put live.

## Reading a delta

Suppose the delta of a call is **0.5**. Then

$$
\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial V}{1} = 0.5
\;\Longrightarrow\; \partial V = \$0.5
$$

> For every **\$1 increase** in the underlying asset, the option price
> **increases by \$0.50**.

And for a put with delta **−0.5**: a \$1 *rise* in the asset makes the put price
*fall* by \$0.50.

These values come out of the option pricing models — **Black-Scholes**, the
**binomial tree**, or **Monte Carlo**. Delta is not assumed; it is computed.

## Delta and moneyness

For a **call**:

| Moneyness | Delta |
| --- | --- |
| **ITM** | 0.5 to 1 |
| **ATM** | ≈ 0.5 |
| **OTM** | 0 to 0.5 |

For a **put**, mirrored into the negatives:

| Moneyness | Delta |
| --- | --- |
| **ITM** | −0.5 to −1 |
| **ATM** | ≈ −0.5 |
| **OTM** | 0 to −0.5 |

This is **standard practice among traders and risk managers for defining
moneyness** — rather than comparing $S$ and $K$ directly, they read it off the
delta.

## Delta as a probability

This is the interpretation worth carrying around:

> **Delta is the probability of the option contract being in the money at
> expiry.**

- $\Delta = 0.2$ → roughly a **20% chance** of finishing in the money — clearly
  OTM territory.
- $\Delta = 0.5$ → a coin flip — at the money.
- $\Delta$ between 0.5 and 1 → a **50% to 100%** chance — in the money.
- $\Delta = 1$ → effectively certain.

And recall what each outcome means at expiry: **ITM** you receive money,
**ATM** no profit and no loss, **OTM** you have paid the money (the premium) for
nothing.

## What moves delta

### Time to maturity

Take an **OTM call**. With a **long** time to maturity, the stock has more time
to move up to the strike, so there is a **higher chance of expiring ITM** — and
therefore a **higher delta**. Two days from expiry, the stock has almost no room
to travel, the probability collapses, and delta with it.

$$
\text{Long-term maturity} \Rightarrow \text{higher delta}
\qquad
\text{Short-term maturity} \Rightarrow \text{lower delta}
$$

### Volatility

The same logic through a different lever.

- **High volatility** — the stock moves around more, so an OTM option has a
  **higher probability of expiring ITM** → **higher delta**.
- **Low volatility** — fluctuation is small, the probability of reaching the
  strike is low → **lower delta**.

Both effects are really one effect: anything that raises the chance of finishing
in the money raises delta, because *delta is that chance*.

## Summary

- The Greeks are risk measures: the sensitivity of the option price to each
  input. Five of them — delta, gamma, theta, rho, vega.
- $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ — a **partial** derivative, because the option
  price depends on several variables at once.
- **Call delta runs 0 to +1, put delta 0 to −1**; the short of each flips the
  sign.
- A delta of 0.5 means a \$1 move in the underlying moves the option \$0.50.
- **Delta is the probability of finishing in the money** — which is why traders
  use it to define moneyness (ITM 0.5–1, ATM ≈0.5, OTM 0–0.5 for a call).
- **More time → higher delta. More volatility → higher delta.** Both raise the
  chance of expiring ITM.

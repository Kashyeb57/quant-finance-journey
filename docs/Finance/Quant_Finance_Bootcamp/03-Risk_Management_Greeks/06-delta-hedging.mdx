Knowing the Greeks is one thing; **doing something about them** is another. That
is hedging, and there are six Greek-based varieties:

| | Hedge |
| --- | --- |
| a) | **Delta hedging** |
| b) | **Gamma hedging** |
| c) | **Delta-Gamma hedging** |
| d) | **Vega hedging** |
| e) | **Theta hedging** |
| f) | **Rho hedging** |

This note is the first — and the foundation for the rest.

## What delta hedging is

> Delta hedging is an option trading strategy that aims to make the portfolio
> **"delta neutral"** — so that a **change in the underlying asset will not
> impact the option position's value**.

Recall the definition it is built on:

$$
\Delta = \frac{\text{change in option price}}{\$1 \text{ change in the underlying asset}}
$$

An option derives its price from the stock, so the stock moving up or down drags
the option with it. Delta hedging severs that link: **the stock price can
change and it will not affect our position.**

For reference: $\Delta_{\text{call}}$ runs 0 to 1, and $\Delta_{\text{put}}$
runs −1 to 0.

## The key fact: a share has a delta of 1

This is what makes the hedge possible.

| Position | Delta |
| --- | --- |
| **Buy** a stock | **+1** |
| **Sell (short)** a stock | **−1** |

A share moves dollar-for-dollar with itself. So shares are the instrument you
use to add or subtract delta from a portfolio at will.

## Worked example

Assume:

- You own **100 call options**
- The underlying asset is the **stock**
- **Delta of the call option = 0.5**

### Step 1 — calculate the portfolio delta

$$
1 \text{ call} = 0.5
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
100 \text{ calls} = 0.5 \times 100 = \mathbf{50}
$$

So $\Delta_p = 50$, which reads: **if the stock price changes by \$1, the
position's value changes by \$50.**

### Step 2 — create a delta-neutral position

You need to add −50 of delta. Since a short share carries −1:

$$
\underbrace{+50}_{100 \text{ calls}} \;+\; \underbrace{(-50)}_{\text{short 50 shares}} \;=\; \mathbf{0}
$$

**Short 50 shares** and the portfolio is delta neutral.

### Checking it — impact of a price movement

Suppose the stock price **rises by \$1**:

| Leg | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| 100 call options | $0.5 \times 100 \times 1 =$ **gain \$50** |
| Short 50 shares | **loss \$50** |
| **Net** | $+50 - 50 =$ **0 — no impact** |

The position is immunised against the move. That is the whole idea.

## Which way to hedge

The hedge always takes the **opposite sign** of the position's delta:

| You hold | Its delta | Hedge with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Call option** | positive | **Sell** stock |
| **Put option** | negative (−1 to 0) | **Buy** stock |

A put already carries negative delta, so you add **positive** delta by buying
shares to bring the total to zero.

## Benefit

> Delta hedging **protects the option portfolio from price changes in the
> underlying asset.**

It removes *directional* risk — you stop caring which way the stock goes.

## Challenges

Four, and they are the reason delta hedging is a job rather than a one-off
trade.

**a) Continuous rebalancing.** Delta is not constant — it **changes with every
fluctuation in the underlying asset price**. The moment the stock moves, your
carefully constructed 50-share hedge is no longer the right size, so you must
buy or sell stock again to restore neutrality. That is why the strategy needs
**frequent delta-hedging adjustments**.

**b) Transaction cost.** Regular rebalancing **incurs cost**, because it means
frequently buying and selling the underlying asset. A hedge that must be
adjusted constantly is a hedge that is constantly paying fees and spread.

**c) Market condition.** Delta hedging is **less effective in a highly volatile
environment** — precisely when the stock is jumping around, delta moves fastest
and the hedge is stalest.

**d) Gamma risk.** The deepest one:

> Delta hedging **reduces directional risk but introduces gamma risk.**

Because gamma measures **the rate of change of delta with respect to the
underlying price**, a delta-neutral portfolio is only neutral *at this instant
and at this stock price*. Move the stock, and gamma tells you how fast your
neutrality decays. You have not eliminated risk — you have **traded delta risk
for gamma risk.**

Which is exactly why the next hedge exists.

## How often is delta hedging actually performed?

In a financial institution the frequency depends on several factors:

- **market condition**
- **volatility of the asset**
- the option's **time to expiry**
- the firm's own **risk-management policies**

And note the link back to gamma: **high gamma means delta moves rapidly, which
requires more frequent adjustment.** In practice five regimes are used.

**1. End-of-day hedging.** Many institutions rebalance once, at the end of each
trading day. It is the **cost-effective** approach — it manages the risk without
incurring excessive transaction costs.

**2. Intra-day hedging.** Hedging **multiple times within a day**. Used during
periods of significant volatility — a market event, an announcement, the release
of economic data — when **the delta of the option can shift quickly**. The cost
is obvious: hedging repeatedly means paying transaction costs repeatedly, and in
a volatile stock the option price is moving a lot while you do it.

**3. Threshold-based hedging.** Some institutions use a **threshold or bands**
approach: they rebalance **only when the delta exposure exceeds a certain
threshold**. This is the compromise between the two above — you tolerate a small
amount of drift in exchange for far fewer trades.

**4. Real-time hedging (automated).** The **HFT** end of the spectrum:
systems execute millions of transactions within a second. Appropriate where
**sensitivity to fluctuations in the stock price is high**.

**5. Less frequently — weekly or monthly.** Suitable for a **less volatile
market** and **longer-dated options**, where delta simply does not move fast
enough to justify more.

The through-line: rebalancing frequency is a trade against transaction cost.
Hedge too rarely and you carry unwanted delta; hedge too often and the fees eat
the protection.

## Summary

- **Delta hedging** makes a portfolio **delta neutral**, so the underlying's
  moves stop affecting it.
- **A share has delta 1** (+1 long, −1 short) — that is the lever you use.
- 100 calls at delta 0.5 gives a portfolio delta of **50**, neutralised by
  **shorting 50 shares**; a \$1 move then nets to exactly zero.
- **Calls are hedged by selling stock; puts by buying it.**
- The costs are real: **continuous rebalancing, transaction costs, weakness in
  volatile markets**, and above all **gamma risk** — delta neutrality is
  instantaneous, not permanent.

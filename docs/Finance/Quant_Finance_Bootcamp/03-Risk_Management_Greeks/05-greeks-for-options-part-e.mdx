The last of the five, and the one traders think about least — until rates start
moving.

## Definition

> Rho measures the sensitivity of the option price with respect to the
> **risk-free interest rate** — the change in price for a **1% change** in that
> rate.

$$
\rho = \frac{\partial V}{\partial r}
$$

where $r$ is the risk-free rate.

### What "risk-free rate" means

> The **theoretical return on an investment with no risk.**

In practice it is the **US Treasury rate** (or **SOFR**). The mechanism is
simple: the US government issues bonds to acquire money, people lend to it
because it is considered as close to riskless as exists, and the return on that
lending is the risk-free interest rate.

### Two worked readings

| Option | Rho | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Call** | **+0.05** | For every **1% increase** in the interest rate, the option price **increases by \$0.05** |
| **Put** | **−0.04** | For every 1% increase in the risk-free rate, the put price **decreases by \$0.04** |

So immediately:

$$
\text{Call} \Rightarrow \rho > 0
\qquad\qquad
\text{Put} \Rightarrow \rho < 0
$$

## Why — it is all discounting

This is the one Greek whose sign is best understood through present value.

When the interest rate rises, **the present value of the strike price falls**.
Discounting means the interest rate and the discounted amount are **inversely
related** — a \$110 strike payable in three months might be worth only \$100 in
today's money once rates rise.

Now split by option type, asking *who pays the strike*:

**Call — you PAY the strike.** A call holder will hand over $K$ to buy the
stock. If the present value of that obligation falls, you are effectively paying
**less**. Rising rates worked **in your favour** → **rho is positive**.

**Put — you RECEIVE the strike.** A put holder sells the stock and collects $K$.
If the present value of what you will receive falls, you are collecting a
**lesser price** in today's terms. Rising rates work **against** you → **rho is
negative**.

## Rho and time to expiry

| Maturity | Rho | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Short-term** (5 days) | **Low** | The interest rate has little chance to fluctuate in five days, so the option price will not fluctuate either |
| **Long-term** (60 days+) | **High** | Rates can move a lot over that horizon, so the impact on the option price is significant |

Rho therefore grows with maturity — the same direction as vega and delta, the
opposite of gamma.

## Rho and moneyness

Here rho breaks the pattern of the other Greeks. Gamma, vega and theta all peak
**at the money**. Rho does not — it is **monotonic, and highest in the money**:

| | **Call option** | **Put option** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ITM** | **Highest** +ve rho | **Highest** −ve rho |
| **ATM** | Moderate +ve rho | Moderate −ve rho |
| **OTM** | **Lowest** +ve rho | **Lowest** −ve rho |

**Why ITM is highest.** An in-the-money option is dominated by its **intrinsic
value** ($S_T - K$), with the extrinsic part small. That intrinsic value contains
$K$ — and $K$ is exactly the term discounting acts on. So the option is
**impacted a lot by the interest rate** → higher rho.

**Why OTM is lowest.** An out-of-the-money option has **no intrinsic value** at
all; it is pure extrinsic value. There is no strike payment in the money to
discount, so the rate barely touches it → lower rho.

## When does rho actually matter?

> Rho becomes **extremely important when interest rates are expected to rise or
> fall.**

Conversely, in a **stable interest-rate environment rho becomes less
significant**, and delta, gamma, theta and vega dominate the risk picture. That
is why rho is the Greek most often ignored — and why ignoring it stops being
safe the moment a central bank starts moving.

## All five Greeks, side by side

With rho done, the full set:

| Greek | Measures sensitivity to | Sign | Peaks at | With more time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Delta** $\Delta$ | Underlying price | Call +, put − | — (S-curve) | **Higher** |
| **Gamma** $\Gamma$ | Delta (2nd derivative) | Always + | **ATM** | **Lower** |
| **Theta** $\Theta$ | Time | Long −, short + | **ATM** | Lower per day |
| **Vega** $\mathcal{V}$ | Implied volatility | Always ≥ 0 | **ATM** | **Higher** |
| **Rho** $\rho$ | Risk-free rate | Call +, put − | **ITM** | **Higher** |

Two patterns worth carrying away:

- **Gamma, theta and vega all peak at the money** — that is where an option's
  fate is least decided, so that is where uncertainty is worth most. **Rho is
  the exception**, peaking in the money, because it acts on the strike rather
  than on uncertainty.
- **Long positions are long gamma and long vega but short theta.** You pay decay
  every day in exchange for convexity and volatility exposure. The short side
  takes the opposite trade. That single sentence is most of option trading.

## Summary

- $\rho = \partial V / \partial r$ — the price change per **1%** move in the
  risk-free rate (US Treasury / SOFR).
- **Calls have positive rho, puts negative** — because rising rates cut the
  present value of the strike, which helps the buyer who *pays* it and hurts the
  buyer who *receives* it.
- **Longer-dated options have more rho**; short-dated have almost none.
- **Rho is highest ITM**, not ATM — it tracks the strike, and the strike matters
  most where intrinsic value dominates.
- It matters when **rates are expected to move**, and is largely ignorable when
  they are not.

The last hedge, for the Greek traders think about least — and the one that
becomes urgent the moment a central bank starts moving.

## Definition

> Rho hedging is a risk-management strategy which is used to manage an option's
> **exposure to changes in the interest rate.**

Rho itself **measures the sensitivity of the option price with respect to a 1%
change in the risk-free rate.** The mechanism is direct: **when the interest
rate changes, the option price also changes** — because the rate is what
discounts the strike.

### The goal

> The goal is to **neutralise the portfolio's rho exposure** to prevent
> fluctuations coming from changes in the interest rate.

$$
\rho_p = 0
$$

So that **even if the interest rate changes, there is no change in the option
price.**

## The sign of rho

Rho's sign table is the one that does **not** split cleanly along long/short.
Gamma, vega and theta all key off which side of the trade you are on. Rho keys
off **the option type as well**:

| Rho | Positions |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive** | **Long call, short put** |
| **Negative** | **Short call, long put** |

The reason is in
[the rho note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/greeks-for-options-part-e):
a **call holder pays** the strike, so a falling present value of that strike
helps them; a **put holder receives** it, so the same fall hurts. Take either
side short and the sign flips again. Two flips, two dimensions — hence the
crossed table.

## Worked example

Suppose you are a trader and you hold a portfolio with a **long call option**:

$$
\rho_p = 200
$$

Reading it in both directions:

| Change in the risk-free rate | Effect on the portfolio |
| --- | --- |
| **Rises by 1%** | The portfolio value will **rise by \$200** |
| **Falls by 1%** | The portfolio value will **decrease by \$200** |

Now suppose you are **anticipating a drop in the interest rate**. That would
**reduce your portfolio value** — \$200 for every point of cut, with nothing you
can do about the decision itself.

### Solution — rho hedging

Rho is **+200**, so you need **−200**. From the sign table, negative rho comes
from a **short call** or a **long put**. Enter a **long put contract**, whose
rho is negative:

$$
1 \text{ put} \Rightarrow \rho = -2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
100 \text{ puts} \Rightarrow 100 \times (-2) = \mathbf{-200}
$$

### Combine

$$
\rho_p = 200 - 200 = \mathbf{0}
$$

After adding the **100 put option contracts**, the portfolio is **rho neutral**
— **even if you see fluctuations in the interest rate, it will have no effect on
your portfolio.**

Note what makes this hedge different from the others: the offsetting leg is a
**long** position, not a short one. Because rho's sign depends on option type,
you can buy your way to neutrality rather than having to sell.

## When is rho hedging done?

**a) Interest-rate volatility.** If interest rates **are about to fluctuate
significantly**, rho hedging is useful — it means **no effect of those
fluctuations on your portfolio.** This is the rate-regime analogue of vega
hedging around earnings.

**b) High rho exposure.** A portfolio which **has a high rho measure** will be
**impacted a lot** by a rate move. The difference between a **0.5%** and a
**1%** move is small in the abstract and large on a book carrying size, so **rho
hedging can be useful** here even in a calm rate environment.

**c) Long-term options.** **Long-dated option contracts are more sensitive to
the interest rate** than short-dated ones — compare **1 week** against **6
months**. Over a week the rate barely has time to move; over six months it can
move a great deal, and the discounting acts on the strike for the whole period.
So **rho hedging is useful** on the long end of the curve.

## The six hedges, side by side

That completes the set. What the module has been building toward:

| Hedge | Neutralises | Instrument | Matters when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Delta** | Directional risk, $\Delta_p = 0$ | **Stock** | Always — but only for *small* moves |
| **Gamma** | Delta's own movement, $\Gamma_p = 0$ | **Another option** | Volatile markets, large moves |
| **Delta-gamma** | Both at once | **Option, then stock** | Small *and* large moves |
| **Vega** | Implied-volatility risk | **Short option** | Earnings, market events |
| **Theta** | Time decay | **Opposite side** | Very long or very short dated |
| **Rho** | Interest-rate risk | **Long put / short call** | Rates moving, long-dated books |

Two patterns run through all six:

- **Only stock has a Greek it cannot touch.** Stock moves delta and nothing
  else, which is why every other hedge needs an option — and why the option leg
  is always sized first.
- **Every hedge is a trade against cost.** None of them is free, and each is
  worth doing only when the risk it removes is actually live. That is why every
  one of these notes ends with a "when it matters" section rather than a rule to
  always apply it.

## Summary

- **Rho hedging neutralises interest-rate exposure**: $\rho_p = 0$ means a rate
  change has **no effect on the portfolio.**
- **Rho is positive for a long call or a short put**, negative for a **short
  call or a long put** — the only Greek whose sign depends on option type as
  well as side.
- Worked: a long call portfolio at $\rho = 200$ (±\$200 per point of rate),
  neutralised by **buying 100 puts at $\rho = -2$** → $200 - 200 = 0$.
- Do it when **rates are about to move**, when the book carries **high rho**, or
  when the options are **long-dated** — long maturities give the rate room to
  work on the strike.

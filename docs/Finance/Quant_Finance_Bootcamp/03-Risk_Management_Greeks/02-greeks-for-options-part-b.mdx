Delta answers "how much does the option move when the stock moves?" But delta
itself is not constant — it changes as the stock moves. **Gamma measures that.**

## Definition

> Gamma tells how sensitive your **delta** is with respect to the change in the
> underlying asset.

$$
\Gamma = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}
$$

Since delta is already the first derivative of the option price, gamma is the
**second** derivative — the curvature of the option's price curve.

| | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| **Gamma is higher** | Delta is **more** sensitive to the underlying — when the stock fluctuates, **delta changes rapidly** |
| **Gamma is lower** | Delta is **less** sensitive — **delta adjusts slowly**, even if the stock price moves a lot |

Read geometrically, $\partial \Delta / \partial S$ is the **slope of the delta
curve**. Where the delta curve is steep, gamma is high; where it is flat, gamma
is near zero.

## Gamma and moneyness

This is the shape to remember. Delta traces an S-curve against the stock price;
its slope — gamma — is therefore a **bell**, peaking exactly where the delta
curve is steepest:

- **Gamma is highest at ATM.**
- **Gamma is lowest at ITM and OTM** — at both extremes the delta curve
  flattens out (delta is pinned near 0 or near ±1), so it has almost no slope
  left to give.

And note: **the gamma curve is the same for a call and a put.** The delta curves
are different (0 to 1 versus 0 to −1) but they have the same *slope* at every
stock price, and gamma is that slope.

## Gamma is always positive

> **Gamma is always positive** — for both call options and put options.

This trips people up, because delta's sign depends on call versus put. Gamma's
does not: buying either a call or a put buys curvature.

What *does* differ is who benefits.

## Who gamma favours

**Long call.** As the underlying price rises, the delta rises (say 0.5 → 0.7),
so the option becomes **more sensitive to price changes** in the direction you
want. A \$1 rise now earns \$0.70 instead of \$0.50. Positive gamma
**benefits the long position**.

**Short call.** Gamma is still positive, but your **exposure is negative**. As
the underlying rises, the delta of the short call moves further negative
(−0.5 → −0.7) — so each additional \$1 rise costs you \$0.70 instead of \$0.50.
The position loses money at an *accelerating* rate.

$$
\text{Long position} \Rightarrow \text{gamma favours you}
\qquad
\text{Short position} \Rightarrow \text{gamma works against you}
$$

That asymmetry is the reason short option positions are dangerous in a fast
market: the loss rate itself increases as the move continues.

## Gamma and time to expiry

Here is the part that surprises people, because it runs **opposite to delta**:

| Maturity | Gamma | Effect on delta |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Short-term** | **Higher** gamma | Delta changes **pretty fast** |
| **Long-term** | **Lower** gamma | Delta changes **slowly** over time |

Intuitively: near expiry, an at-the-money option is on a knife edge — a small
move in the stock flips it from "probably worthless" to "probably exercised", so
its delta swings violently from near 0 to near 1. That violent swing *is* high
gamma. A long-dated option has time to absorb the same move, so its delta barely
reacts.

Compare with Part A: **more time raises delta but lowers gamma.**

## Summary

- $\Gamma = \dfrac{\text{change in delta}}{\text{change in the asset}} = \dfrac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}$ — the second derivative, the curvature.
- **Gamma is always positive**, for calls and puts alike, and the gamma curve is
  the same for both.
- **Highest at ATM; lowest at ITM and OTM** — a bell, because it is the slope of
  delta's S-curve.
- **Gamma favours the long position and works against the short**, because the
  short's losses accelerate.
- **Short-dated options have high gamma; long-dated have low gamma** — the
  reverse of delta's relationship with time.

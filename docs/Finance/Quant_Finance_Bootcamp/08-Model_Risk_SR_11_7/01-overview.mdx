Every previous module in this course built a model. This one asks the question
regulators started asking after 2008: **how do you know the model is any good,
and who is accountable when it is not?**

## What SR 11-7 is

> **SR 11-7 is the Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management, issued jointly
> by the Federal Reserve Board (FRB) and the Office of the Comptroller of the
> Currency (OCC) in 2011.**

Dated **4 April 2011**, it is the standard reference for model risk management
in US banking, and it is what a model validation team is measured against.

### Why it was issued

> **Lessons learned from the 2008 financial crisis.**
>
> **Failures in model reliability during the financial crisis highlighted the
> need for robust model risk management practices.**

## Introduction

> **Banks rely heavily on quantitative analysis and models for most of their
> decision making.**

The guidance lists the range: **underwriting credits, valuing exposures,
measuring risk, managing client assets, determining capital and reserve
adequacy**, and many other activities.

> **The expanding use of models in all aspects of banking reflects the extent to
> which models can improve business decisions — but models also come with a
> cost.**

| Cost | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct cost** | the **resources** spent building all the models |
| **Indirect cost** | the consequences of **relying on the outcomes of the model** |

And the indirect cost is the one that matters here:

$$
\text{Decision} \longrightarrow \text{model gives an incorrect result} \longrightarrow \textbf{banks have faced large financial losses}
$$

which is why it is **important to actively manage model risk.**

## Section 2 — Purpose and scope

> **The purpose of this document is to provide comprehensive guidance for banks
> on model risk management.**

> **Rigorous model validation plays a critical role in MRM. However, sound
> development, implementation and use of the model are also vital elements.**

That balance is the guidance's central posture, and it is worth stating plainly:
**validation is not a substitute for building the model properly.** The chain
runs:

$$
\underbrace{\text{Model development}}_{\text{build the model}}
\longrightarrow
\underbrace{\text{Model validation}}_{\text{validate the same model}}
\longrightarrow
\underbrace{\text{Approve the model}}_{}
$$

with two further requirements on the far side: **the user must know the model's
usage**, and the model must be **implemented the right way.**

## Section 3 — What counts as a model

The definition is precise, and it is broader than most people expect:

> The term **model** refers to a **quantitative method, system, or approach that
> applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories, techniques
> and assumptions to process input data into quantitative estimates.**

### The three components

> A model consists of **three components.**

| # | Component | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Information input** | delivers **assumptions and data** to the model |
| 2 | **Processing** | **transforms inputs into estimates** |
| 3 | **Reporting** | **translates the estimates into useful business information** |

$$
\text{Input} \longrightarrow \text{Processing} \longrightarrow \text{Output / Reporting}
$$

:::note The definition is deliberately wide

Two consequences worth noticing.

**A spreadsheet is a model.** Nothing in the definition requires code, a
database, or sophistication — the Black-Scholes sheet from
[Module 4](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/black-scholes-excel)
meets it in full.

**Expert judgement can be an input.** The guidance explicitly covers
"quantitative approaches whose inputs are partially or wholly qualitative or
based on expert judgment, **provided that the output is quantitative in
nature**." So a scorecard driven by analyst opinion is still a model if it
produces a number.

:::

### Models are simplifications

> Models are **simplified representations of real-world relationships.**
> Simplification is **inevitable**, due to the complexity of those
> relationships, **but also intentional** — to focus attention on the aspects
> considered most important for a given application.

> **Model quality can be measured in many ways: precision, accuracy,
> discriminatory power, robustness, stability and reliability**, to name a few.

Which metric applies depends on the model's job:

| Model type | Relevant metrics |
| --- | --- |
| **Regression model** | **RMSE**, **MSE**, **MAE**, **MAPE** |
| **VaR model** | [**Traffic Light Approach**](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/backtest-traffic-light), [**Kupiec Test**](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/backtest-kupiec) |

That second row is the direct link back to
[Module 6](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-introduction):
backtesting a VaR model *is* model validation, done by the standards this
document sets.

> **Models are never perfect, and the appropriate metric of quality depends on
> the situation.**

## Model risk, and the two ways it arises

> The use of models invariably presents **model risk**, which is the **potential
> for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model
> outputs and reports.**

It can lead to **financial loss, poor business and strategic decision making, or
damage to a bank's reputation.**

**Reason 1 — the model may have fundamental errors.**

> The model **may have fundamental errors and may produce inaccurate output when
> viewed against the design objective and intended use.**

Errors can enter anywhere along the chain:

- the **mathematical calculation and quantification**
- the **choice of sample design**
- the **selection of inputs and estimation**
- the **implementation**

> **Errors can occur at any point from design through implementation.**

The lecturer's example is an implementation error you can see: the
[Vasicek model](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/vasicek-theory)
is $dr_t = a(b - r_t)dt + \sigma\,dW_t$, but deployed as
$dr_t = a(b - r_t)\sigma\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$ — the theory is right and the code
is not. *Is the model deployed correctly?* is a separate question from *is the
model correct?*

**Reason 2 — the model may be used incorrectly or inappropriately.**

The example here is one this course has already built: **Black-Scholes prices a
European option.** Point it at an **American option** and it will return a
number — a wrong one, because
[the model assumes exercise only at maturity](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/black-scholes-theory).

$$
\text{Black-Scholes} \rightarrow \text{European} \;\checkmark
\qquad
\text{Black-Scholes} \rightarrow \text{American} \;✗ \rightarrow \textbf{model risk}
$$

> It is **very important to understand the limitations of a model, to avoid
> using it in ways that are not consistent with the original intent.**

That is the sharpest practical lesson in the whole document: **a model does not
fail loudly when misused.** It returns a plausible number.

## How model risk behaves

- **Model risk should be managed like any other kind of risk** — identified,
  measured, controlled.
- **Banks should identify the source of risk and assess the magnitude.**
- **Model risk increases with greater model complexity.**
- **Aggregate model risk is affected by interaction and dependencies among
  models** — where one model's output is another's input, errors propagate:

$$
M_1 \xrightarrow{\;\text{output}\;} M_2 \xrightarrow{\;\text{output}\;} \text{decision}
$$

## It cannot be eliminated

> **Even with skilled modelling and robust validation, model risk cannot be
> eliminated.**

So the guidance turns to containment instead:

- **establish limits on model use**
- **monitor model performance**
- **adjust or revise the model over time**
- **ad-hoc analysis** — extra checks to make sure everything is correct

Monitoring runs on a defined cycle — **monthly, quarterly, semi-annual, annual,
or two-yearly** — and the outcome is either to **adjust the model or build a new
one.**

This is the same posture the hedging module took: you do not remove the risk,
you decide how much of it to carry and check on it regularly.

## Summary

- **SR 11-7** is the **Fed/OCC Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management
  (2011)**, issued after the **2008 crisis** exposed failures in model
  reliability.
- A **model** is any **quantitative method processing input data into
  quantitative estimates**, with **three components: input, processing,
  reporting.** The definition covers spreadsheets and expert-judgement inputs.
- **Models are deliberate simplifications** and **never perfect**; quality
  metrics depend on the job — RMSE for a regression, traffic light and Kupiec
  for a VaR model.
- **Model risk** is the **potential for adverse consequences from decisions
  based on incorrect or misused model outputs.**
- It arises **two ways**: **fundamental errors** (anywhere from design through
  implementation) and **incorrect or inappropriate use** (Black-Scholes on an
  American option).
- It **increases with complexity** and **compounds across linked models.**
- **It cannot be eliminated** — so it is contained with **limits on use,
  monitoring, revision and ad-hoc analysis.**

At inception a swap is worth roughly zero to both sides. After that, rates move
and it acquires value. This note computes that value.

> To value an IRS you typically focus on the **NPV of the future cash flows from
> the swap's fixed and floating legs.**

## Step 1 — identify the swap terms

| Term | |
| --- | --- |
| **Fixed rate** | |
| **Floating rate** | the **benchmark interest rate used for the floating leg** |
| **Notional amount** | the **principal upon which interest payments are calculated** |
| **Payment frequency** | quarterly, semi-annual, annual |
| **Day count convention** | |
| **Duration of the swap** | 3 years, 5 years, … |

### Day count conventions

How you convert "between these two dates" into a number to multiply a rate by:

| Convention | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **ACT/365** | **actual** number of days, over a 365-day year |
| **ACT/ACT** | actual days over the actual year length — **2024 is a leap year → 366** |
| **30/360** | **30 days in a month, 360 days in a year** |

15 January to 15 March under **30/360** is $30 \times 2 = 60$ days, so
$60/360$ of a year. Under **ACT** it would be the true 59 days. Small
differences, but they compound across a large notional.

$$
\text{Discounting CF} = e^{-rT}, \quad T = \text{time between two consecutive dates}
$$

## Step 2 — forecast the future cash flows

| Leg | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Fixed leg** | the **rate remains the same for all the cash flows** — trivial |
| **Floating leg** | **depends on the future value of the floating rate index, usually calculated using the forward curve** |

$$
\text{spot rate} \longrightarrow \text{forward rate} \longrightarrow \text{CF of the floating leg}
$$

This is why the
[forward rate derivation](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Swaps/spot-rate-forward-rate)
had to come first. You cannot forecast the floating leg without it.

## Step 3 — determine the discount factors

$$
\boxed{\;DF = e^{-rt}\;}
$$

> **Discount factors are used to calculate the present value of the future cash
> flows.**

## Step 4 — calculate the present values

$$
PV = DF \times CF
$$

done **separately for the fixed leg and the floating leg**, then summed across
all payment dates.

## Step 5 — compute the NPV

$$
\boxed{\;NPV = PV(\text{floating leg}) - PV(\text{fixed leg})\;}
$$

| NPV | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive** | the **value of the floating leg exceeds the fixed leg** → **beneficial for the party receiving floating** |
| **Negative** | the **fixed leg value exceeds the floating leg** → **beneficial for the party receiving fixed** |

## The worked spreadsheet

A swap with a **remaining life of 1.25 years**, **\$100M notional**, **3%
fixed**, semi-annual, where the institution **pays fixed and receives LIBOR.**

The LIBOR term structure (continuously compounded):

| Time | −0.25 | 0.25 | 0.75 | 1.25 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **LIBOR** | **2.9%** | 2.8% | 3.2% | 3.4% |

The **−0.25** column is the rate observed **three months ago**, which — by the
[set-in-advance convention](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Swaps/interest-rate-swap-part-2)
— determines the payment due at **+0.25**. It is already known, so it needs no
forecasting.

**Step 1 — discount factors**, `=EXP(-rate*time)`:

| Time | 0.25 | 0.75 | 1.25 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DF** | 0.993024 | 0.976286 | 0.958390 |

**Step 2 — implied forward rates**, using the formula from the previous note:

```excel
D20 = (D19*D18 - C19*C18)/(D18 - C18)
```

$$
f(0.25,\,0.75) = \frac{0.032 \times 0.75 - 0.028 \times 0.25}{0.50} = 3.4\%
$$

$$
f(0.75,\,1.25) = \frac{0.034 \times 1.25 - 0.032 \times 0.75}{0.50} = 3.7\%
$$

then converted from continuous to semi-annual compounding:

```excel
D21 = 2*(EXP(D20/2)-1)
```

| | f(0.25,0.75) | f(0.75,1.25) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Continuous** | 3.4000% | 3.7000% |
| **Semi-annual** | **3.4291%** | **3.7344%** |

That conversion matters: the cash flow is paid semi-annually, so the rate must
be expressed in the same convention before dividing by 2.

**Steps 3-5 — the cash flows:**

| Time | 0.25 | 0.75 | 1.25 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Floating CF | 1.4500 | 1.714532 | 1.867219 |
| Fixed CF | 1.5 | 1.5 | 1.5 |
| **Net CF (FV)** | −0.0500 | +0.214532 | +0.367219 |
| **Net CF (PV)** | −0.049651 | +0.209445 | +0.351939 |
| | | **Total** | **+\$0.511732M** |

**The swap is worth +\$511,732** to the party paying fixed and receiving
floating. Every figure above reproduces exactly when recomputed independently.

The first floating payment, **1.45**, is $2.9\%/2 \times 100$ — the known rate
from three months ago. The other two come from the forwards.

## The bond-pair method

There is a second route, and the workbook's `Swap Rate of IRS` sheet uses it:

> **An interest rate swap is equivalent to being long one bond and short
> another.**

Pay fixed, receive floating = **short a fixed-rate bond, long a floating-rate
bond.** Both bonds have the same notional and dates, so the principals cancel —
leaving exactly the swap's net interest flows.

On a **2-year, \$10M** swap with the SOFR curve 3%, 4%, 4%, 3.5%:

| | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed-rate bond PV | \$9,924,545.11 |
| Floating-rate bond PV | \$9,924,545.18 |
| **Value to pay fixed / receive float** | ≈ **0** |
| **Swap rate** | **3.144490%** |

The **swap rate** is the fixed rate that makes the two bonds equal — i.e. the
fixed rate at which the swap is worth zero at inception. That is the rate a
dealer would quote.

:::danger The floating bond does not reprice to par, and the swap rate is 39.5bp off

The sheet carries the note *"Floating rate bond reprices itself back to par."*
That is a genuine theoretical result — but **this sheet's numbers contradict
it.**

The floating leg uses **typed-in** rates (2.5%, 3.5%, 3.5%, 3.1%) rather than
the forwards implied by the SOFR curve it is discounted with. The result:

| | Floating bond PV |
| --- | --- |
| **With the sheet's assumed rates** | **\$9,924,545** — **99.245% of par** |
| **With the forwards implied by the same curve** | **\$10,000,000.00 — exactly par** |

The implied forwards from that curve are **3%, 5%, 4%, 2%** — quite different
from the ones entered. Using them, the floating bond prices to par to the cent,
confirming the note's claim.

**This propagates into the swap rate.** Since the swap rate is the fixed rate
that matches the floating bond's value:

| | Swap rate |
| --- | --- |
| Sheet's answer (floating bond below par) | **3.144490%** |
| Correct (floating bond at par) | **3.539535%** |
| **Difference** | **39.5 basis points** |

The *method* is right, and the bond-pair framing is standard. But the floating
leg must be built from the same curve used to discount it — otherwise you are
quoting a swap rate against a bond mispriced by three quarters of a percent.

:::

## Summary

- Valuing an IRS: **identify terms → forecast cash flows → discount factors →
  present values → NPV.**
- $NPV = PV(\text{float}) - PV(\text{fixed})$. **Positive favours the floating
  receiver; negative favours the fixed receiver.**
- The **fixed leg is constant**; the **floating leg comes from the forward
  curve**, which comes from the spot curve.
- **Day count conventions** (ACT/365, ACT/ACT, 30/360) decide how a date gap
  becomes a fraction of a year.
- Worked: 1.25 years remaining, \$100M, 3% fixed → forwards of **3.4291%** and
  **3.7344%** semi-annual → **NPV +\$511,732**, verified independently.
- **A swap is a pair of bonds** — pay fixed / receive float = short a fixed
  bond, long a floating one. The **swap rate** equalises them.
- **The workbook's floating bond prices at 99.245% of par, not par**, because
  its floating rates were typed rather than derived from its own discount curve
  — putting the quoted swap rate **39.5bp** below the correct 3.539535%.

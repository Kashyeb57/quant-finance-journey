The [interest rate swap](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Swaps/interest-rate-swap-part-2)
exchanged one kind of interest for another in the same currency. A **currency
swap** crosses the currency boundary, and that one change alters the mechanics
considerably.

## Definition

> A currency swap is a **financial derivative that commits 2 counterparties to
> exchange streams of interest payments and principal payments in different
> currencies.**

> Currency swaps are typically used by institutions to **manage exposure to
> foreign exchange.**

### The problem it solves

An Indian company owes **\$100,000 three months from now**. Today the bank quotes
**1 USD = ₹85**, so the obligation is ₹8,500,000. But the rate moves every day —
82, 84, 85, 86 — and in three months it could be anything.

> The **amount should not be reduced.**

The company does not want its rupee cost floating around with the exchange rate.
A currency swap fixes the terms of the exchange in advance, converting an
uncertain FX exposure into a known schedule.

## The three-stage structure

Unlike an IRS, a currency swap exchanges **principal as well as interest** — and
it does so twice.

**1) Initial exchange of principal.**

> At the **inception of the swap, the principal amounts in 2 different
> currencies are exchanged between the counterparties at a spot rate.**

**2) Exchange of interest payments.**

> **Interest payments between the 2 counterparties are exchanged** — on each
> payment date through the life of the swap.

**3) Re-exchange of principal.**

> At the **maturity of the swap, the original principal amounts are swapped back
> at the same exchange rate as at the inception, regardless of the prevailing
> market exchange rate.**

$$
\underbrace{t=0}_{\text{principal exchanged}} \;\rightarrow\;
\underbrace{t=1,2,3,4}_{\text{interest exchanged}} \;\rightarrow\;
\underbrace{t=5}_{\text{principal + final interest}}
$$

:::note This is why the principal is real here

In an interest rate swap both legs are in the same currency, so exchanging
identical principals would be pointless — they would cancel. Hence "notional".

In a currency swap the two principals are in **different currencies**, so they
do **not** cancel. Exchanging them is the entire point: it is how each party
gets the currency it actually needs.

And because the re-exchange happens at the **inception rate regardless of the
prevailing market rate**, the FX risk over the swap's life is removed — which is
precisely what the Indian importer above was trying to achieve.

:::

## Types of currency swap

| | Type | Structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Fixed for Fixed** | **Both parties exchange fixed interest payments** |
| 2 | **Fixed for Floating** | **One party pays a fixed rate, the other pays a floating rate** |
| 3 | **Floating for Floating** | **Both parties exchange floating interest payments** — in different currencies |

## Worked example — fixed for fixed

A hypothetical **5-year currency swap**, initiated **1 February 2017**:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **British Petroleum pays** | **3% fixed on \$15 million** |
| **BP receives** | **4% fixed on £10 million** |
| **Payments** | **once a year** |

Cash flows to BP:

| Date | Dollar cash flow | Sterling cash flow | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Feb 2017 | **+\$15M** | **−£10M** | *initial exchange* |
| 1 Feb 2018 | −\$0.45M | +£0.40M | |
| 1 Feb 2019 | −\$0.45M | +£0.40M | |
| 1 Feb 2020 | −\$0.45M | +£0.40M | |
| 1 Feb 2021 | −\$0.45M | +£0.40M | |
| 1 Feb 2022 | **−\$15.45M** | **+£10.40M** | *final interest + re-exchange* |

At inception **BP pays £10 million and receives \$15 million** — implying an
exchange rate of 1.50 dollars per pound, locked in for the life of the swap.

The interest amounts are straightforward: $3\% \times 15 = 0.45$ and
$4\% \times 10 = 0.40$, annually. The final row bundles the last interest
payment with the principal going back the other way.

Notice the **signs are opposite across the two columns** at every date. BP is
short dollars and long sterling throughout, which is the exposure it wanted.

## Valuing a currency swap

The same **two-bond** framing as the
[IRS valuation](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Swaps/valuation-of-irs)
— but now the two bonds are in different currencies, so one leg has to be
converted before they can be netted.

**The setup:**

| | US | Japan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Principal** | **\$10M** | **¥1,200M** |
| **Fixed rate paid/received** | 4% | 3% |
| **Risk-free rate** (continuous) | **2.5%** | **1.5%** |

with **3 years remaining** and a current exchange rate of **110 yen per dollar**.
The institution **pays dollars and receives yen.**

**The dollar leg** — cash flows $0.4$, $0.4$, $10.4$ (the last including
principal), discounted at 2.5%:

| Time | 1 | 2 | 3 | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CF | 0.4 | 0.4 | 10.4 | |
| DF `=EXP(-0.025*t)` | 0.975310 | 0.951229 | 0.927743 | |
| **PV** | 0.390124 | 0.380492 | 9.648532 | **Σ = \$10.419148M** |

**The yen leg** — cash flows $36$, $36$, $1236$, discounted at 1.5%:

| Time | 1 | 2 | 3 | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CF | 36 | 36 | 1236 | |
| DF `=EXP(-0.015*t)` | 0.985112 | 0.970446 | 0.955997 | |
| **PV** | 35.46403 | 34.936039 | 1181.612888 | **Σ = ¥1,252.012957M** |

**Convert and net:**

$$
\frac{¥1{,}252.012957\text{M}}{110} = \$11.381936\text{M}
$$

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Net value} = \$11.381936 - \$10.419148 = \mathbf{+\$0.962788\text{M}}\;}
$$

The swap is worth **+\$962,788** to the institution. All six present values and
the final net reproduce exactly when recomputed independently.

:::note Where the value comes from

Two forces are at work, and they pull in opposite directions.

**The yen leg pays a lower coupon** (3% vs 4%) — which hurts. But **the yen
discount rate is also lower** (1.5% vs 2.5%) — which helps, because a lower
discount rate raises present value.

Here the discounting effect wins. The yen bond's coupon of 3% sits **above** its
1.5% discount rate, so it prices above par; the dollar bond's 4% coupon sits
above its 2.5% rate too, but by a smaller margin relative to its size. Converted
at the current rate, the yen leg is worth more — hence the positive value.

**And note what is *not* in this calculation: any forecast of the exchange
rate.** The valuation uses today's spot rate to convert, and the two interest
rate curves to discount. That is the no-arbitrage approach — an FX forecast
would introduce a view, and a valuation is not supposed to have one.

:::

## Summary

- A **currency swap** exchanges **interest payments and principal in two
  different currencies**, and is used to **manage foreign exchange exposure.**
- **Three stages**: **initial exchange of principal** at spot → **exchange of
  interest** through the life → **re-exchange of principal at maturity, at the
  original inception rate**, whatever the market rate has become.
- **The principal really is exchanged**, unlike an IRS, because the two
  principals are in different currencies and do not cancel.
- **Three types**: fixed-for-fixed, fixed-for-floating, floating-for-floating.
- Worked: BP pays **3% on \$15M**, receives **4% on £10M** for 5 years —
  principals swapped at inception and returned at maturity.
- Valued as **two bonds in two currencies**: PV each leg on its own curve,
  **convert at the spot rate**, and net. Worked to **+\$962,788**, verified.
- The valuation needs **no exchange-rate forecast** — only today's spot rate and
  the two interest rate curves.

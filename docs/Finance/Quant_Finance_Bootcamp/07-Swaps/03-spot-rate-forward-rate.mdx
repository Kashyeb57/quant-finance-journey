---
title: Spot Rates and Forward Rates
description: 'The rate you can transact at today versus the rate implied for a future period — and the no-arbitrage derivation that pins the second to the first.'
---

Valuing a swap means discounting future cash flows, and the floating leg's
future cash flows are not yet known. Both problems need the **term structure of
interest rates** — which is what this note builds.

## Spot rate

> The spot rate is the **current interest rate at which a financial instrument
> can be bought or sold for immediate delivery.**

**Usage: it is used for immediate transactions.**

The parallel the notes draw: stock ABC trading at **\$100** right now is the
**spot value of the stock**. An interest rate of **4%** available right now — a
**SOFR** quote, say — is the **spot rate**. Both are prices for doing something
*today*.

## Forward rate

> The forward rate is an **agreed-upon rate between 2 parties for a transaction
> that will occur at a specified future date.**

$$
\underbrace{0 \longrightarrow 3}_{\text{spot rate}} \qquad \underbrace{3 \longrightarrow 6}_{\text{forward rate}}
$$

> **Note: the forward rate is not a market rate today, but rather a rate
> expected in the future.**
>
> ⤷ **Derived from the spot rate.**

That last line is the important one, and the rest of this note is why it is
true.

| | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| **Spot rates** | **immediate transactions** |
| **Forward rates** | **future transactions** |

## Deriving the forward rate from spot rates

The tool is **continuous compounding**:

$$
\text{Compounding} = e^{rt}
\qquad\qquad
\text{Discounting} = e^{-rt}
$$

where $r$ is the rate and $t$ the time.

### The setup

Two ways to invest an amount $P$ from time 0 to time $T_2$:

**Case 1 — invest straight through.** Put $P$ in at the $T_2$ spot rate $R_2$
and hold to $T_2$:

$$
FV_{T_2} = P \cdot e^{R_2 T_2} \qquad\qquad \text{— (1)}
$$

**Case 2 — invest in two steps.** Put $P$ in at the $T_1$ spot rate $R_1$ until
$T_1$:

$$
FV_{T_1} = P \cdot e^{R_1 T_1}
$$

then roll that amount forward from $T_1$ to $T_2$ at the forward rate $F$, for a
duration of $(T_2 - T_1)$:

$$
FV_{T_2} = FV_{T_1} \times e^{F(T_2 - T_1)} = P \cdot e^{R_1 T_1} \cdot e^{F(T_2-T_1)} \qquad \text{— (2)}
$$

### Why they must be equal

If the two routes gave different amounts, you could borrow via the cheaper one
and lend via the richer one and pocket the difference with no capital and no
risk. The notes name it directly: **arbitrage opportunities.**

$$
\$100 \xrightarrow{\;\;1\text{yr, then 1yr}\;\;} X
\qquad
\$100 \xrightarrow{\;\;\;\;2\text{yr straight}\;\;\;\;} X
$$

Both must land on the same $X$. So set **(1) = (2)**:

$$
\cancel{P}\,e^{R_2 T_2} = \cancel{P}\,e^{R_1 T_1} \cdot e^{F(T_2-T_1)}
$$

$$
e^{R_2 T_2} = e^{R_1 T_1} \cdot e^{F(T_2-T_1)}
$$

Take logs of both sides, using $\log(A \times B) = \log(A) + \log(B)$, and since
log and exp are inverses:

$$
R_2 T_2 = R_1 T_1 + F(T_2 - T_1)
$$

$$
F(T_2 - T_1) = R_2 T_2 - R_1 T_1
$$

$$
\boxed{\;F = \frac{R_2 T_2 - R_1 T_1}{T_2 - T_1}\;}
$$

**the forward rate between $T_1$ and $T_2$**, where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the
**zero rates / spot rates** for those maturities.

:::note A slip in the middle of the derivation

Immediately after stating the log rule correctly, the source writes

$$
\log\left[e^{R_2T_2}\right] = \log\left[e^{R_1T_1} + e^{F(T_2-T_1)}\right]
$$

with a **plus** inside the bracket where the previous line correctly had a
**product**. The very next line then gives the right answer,
$R_2T_2 = R_1T_1 + F(T_2-T_1)$ — which only follows from the product form.

It is a transcription slip mid-derivation; the result is correct. But the
distinction matters: **the sum appears *after* taking the log, not before.**

:::

## Reading the formula

Rearranged, the formula says something intuitive:

$$
R_2 T_2 = R_1 T_1 + F(T_2 - T_1)
$$

**Total growth over the long period = growth over the short period + growth over
the stub.** The forward rate is whatever rate makes the second leg fill exactly
the gap the two spot rates leave.

Which also explains why forward rates can look extreme. If $R_2$ is barely above
$R_1$ but $T_2$ is much larger than $T_1$, the implied $F$ has to be low — and
if the curve is steep, $F$ can exceed both spot rates.

## Summary

- **Spot rate**: the rate available **now**, for **immediate** transactions.
- **Forward rate**: an agreed rate for a transaction at a **specified future
  date** — **not a market rate today**, but one **derived from spot rates.**
- Continuous compounding is $e^{rt}$; discounting is $e^{-rt}$.
- The derivation equates **investing straight through to $T_2$** against
  **investing to $T_1$ then rolling forward** — they must agree or there is an
  **arbitrage**.
- $\boxed{F = \dfrac{R_2T_2 - R_1T_1}{T_2 - T_1}}$
- Equivalently $R_2T_2 = R_1T_1 + F(T_2-T_1)$: the long period's growth is the
  short period's growth plus the forward stub.

Greeks for Options – Part A (Delta)

What the Greeks are​

The Greeks are risk measures used in options to assess the sensitivity of the option price to various factors.

An option's price does not depend on one thing. It moves with the underlying asset, with time, with interest rates, and with volatility. Each Greek isolates one of those and asks: if this input moves, how much does the option price move?

There are five:

GreekSensitivity of the option price with respect to…
Delta (Δ\DeltaΔ)the underlying asset
Gamma (Γ\GammaΓ)(the sensitivity of delta to the underlying asset)
Theta (Θ\ThetaΘ)time
Rho (ρ\rhoρ)the risk-free rate
Vegaimplied volatility

Gamma is the odd one out: it is a sensitivity of another Greek, not of the price directly.

Delta​

Delta tells the sensitivity of the option price with respect to the underlying asset — or equivalently, the rate of change of the option price relative to the change in the underlying asset.

Δ=∂V∂S\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}Δ=∂S∂V​

where VVV is the price of the option and SSS is the price of the underlying asset. So ∂V\partial V∂V is the change in the option price and ∂S\partial S∂S the change in the underlying asset price.

Why a partial derivative?​

Because the option price is a function of several variables at once — the asset price, volatility, the rate, time. A function of one variable gives an ordinary derivative (an ODE, dV/dSdV/dSdV/dS); a function of many gives a partial derivative (a PDE, ∂V/∂S\partial V / \partial S∂V/∂S), which holds everything else fixed and varies only SSS. Option pricing is the second case.

The sign of delta​

Delta can be positive or negative, and which one depends on the type of option and the position you hold.

Positive delta — a positive relationship with the underlying:

  • the option value rises when the stock price rises
  • the option value falls when the stock price falls

Negative delta — a negative relationship:

  • the option price falls when the underlying rises
  • the option price rises when the underlying falls

A put has negative delta for exactly this reason: you hold the right to sell, so a rising stock makes that right less valuable.

Range, by option and by position​

Call option: 0 to 1Put option: 0 to −1\text{Call option: } 0 \text{ to } 1 \qquad\qquad \text{Put option: } 0 \text{ to } -1Call option: 0 to 1Put option: 0 to −1

Taking the position into account as well:

PositionDelta range
Long call0 to +1
Short call0 to −1
Long put0 to −1
Short put0 to +1

Laid on a number line, −1 is where long put and short call live; +1 is where long call and short put live.

Reading a delta​

Suppose the delta of a call is 0.5. Then

Δ=∂V∂S=∂V1=0.5  ⟹  ∂V=$0.5\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial V}{1} = 0.5 \;\Longrightarrow\; \partial V = \$0.5Δ=∂S∂V​=1∂V​=0.5⟹∂V=$0.5

For every $1 increase in the underlying asset, the option price increases by $0.50.

And for a put with delta −0.5: a $1 rise in the asset makes the put price fall by $0.50.

These values come out of the option pricing models — Black-Scholes, the binomial tree, or Monte Carlo. Delta is not assumed; it is computed.

Delta and moneyness​

For a call:

MoneynessDelta
ITM0.5 to 1
ATM≈ 0.5
OTM0 to 0.5

For a put, mirrored into the negatives:

MoneynessDelta
ITM−0.5 to −1
ATM≈ −0.5
OTM0 to −0.5

This is standard practice among traders and risk managers for defining moneyness — rather than comparing SSS and KKK directly, they read it off the delta.

Delta as a probability​

This is the interpretation worth carrying around:

Delta is the probability of the option contract being in the money at expiry.

  • Δ=0.2\Delta = 0.2Δ=0.2 → roughly a 20% chance of finishing in the money — clearly OTM territory.
  • Δ=0.5\Delta = 0.5Δ=0.5 → a coin flip — at the money.
  • Δ\DeltaΔ between 0.5 and 1 → a 50% to 100% chance — in the money.
  • Δ=1\Delta = 1Δ=1 → effectively certain.

And recall what each outcome means at expiry: ITM you receive money, ATM no profit and no loss, OTM you have paid the money (the premium) for nothing.

What moves delta​

Time to maturity​

Take an OTM call. With a long time to maturity, the stock has more time to move up to the strike, so there is a higher chance of expiring ITM — and therefore a higher delta. Two days from expiry, the stock has almost no room to travel, the probability collapses, and delta with it.

Long-term maturity⇒higher deltaShort-term maturity⇒lower delta\text{Long-term maturity} \Rightarrow \text{higher delta} \qquad \text{Short-term maturity} \Rightarrow \text{lower delta}Long-term maturity⇒higher deltaShort-term maturity⇒lower delta

Volatility​

The same logic through a different lever.

  • High volatility — the stock moves around more, so an OTM option has a higher probability of expiring ITM → higher delta.
  • Low volatility — fluctuation is small, the probability of reaching the strike is low → lower delta.

Both effects are really one effect: anything that raises the chance of finishing in the money raises delta, because delta is that chance.

Summary​

  • The Greeks are risk measures: the sensitivity of the option price to each input. Five of them — delta, gamma, theta, rho, vega.
  • Δ=∂V/∂S\Delta = \partial V/\partial SΔ=∂V/∂S — a partial derivative, because the option price depends on several variables at once.
  • Call delta runs 0 to +1, put delta 0 to −1; the short of each flips the sign.
  • A delta of 0.5 means a $1 move in the underlying moves the option $0.50.
  • Delta is the probability of finishing in the money — which is why traders use it to define moneyness (ITM 0.5–1, ATM ≈0.5, OTM 0–0.5 for a call).
  • More time → higher delta. More volatility → higher delta. Both raise the chance of expiring ITM.

Gamma Hedging

Delta hedging ended on a problem: it removes directional risk but introduces gamma risk, because a delta-neutral portfolio is only neutral at the current price. Gamma hedging attacks that.

What gamma hedging is​

Gamma hedging is a risk-management strategy that aims to neutralize or reduce gamma risk in an option position.

Gamma risk is the sensitivity of an option's delta with respect to a change in the underlying asset price:

Γ=change in deltachange in the underlying asset\Gamma = \frac{\text{change in delta}}{\text{change in the underlying asset}}Γ=change in the underlying assetchange in delta​

The goal, stated plainly: even though my asset price changes, it should not affect my gamma — that is, my delta should stay put.

Position gamma​

An option's own gamma is positive, but the position's gamma takes the sign of the side you are on:

Position gammaPositions
PositiveLong call, long put
NegativeShort call, short put

That is the lever: to cancel positive gamma you need to sell options.

Worked example​

You own 10 call option contracts with:

  • Delta = 0.5
  • Gamma = 0.02

Step 1 — portfolio delta and gamma​

Total delta=10×0.5=5Total gamma=10×0.02=0.2\text{Total delta} = 10 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{5} \qquad\qquad \text{Total gamma} = 10 \times 0.02 = \mathbf{0.2}Total delta=10×0.5=5Total gamma=10×0.02=0.2

Step 2 — neutralise delta​

Delta is 5, so sell 5 shares (delta −5):

Δp=5−5=0\Delta_p = 5 - 5 = \mathbf{0}Δp​=5−5=0

Portfolio so far: long 10 calls + short 5 stock.

Step 3 — neutralise gamma​

The portfolio gamma is +0.2, so we need −0.2, which means going short an option. Suppose we short put option contracts with gamma = −0.04 each:

5×(−0.04)=−0.25 \times (-0.04) = \mathbf{-0.2}5×(−0.04)=−0.2

So short 5 put option contracts. The full portfolio is now:

P=10 calls⏟Γ=+0.2  +  short 5 stock⏟Γ=0  +  short 5 puts⏟Γ=−0.2P = \underbrace{10 \text{ calls}}_{\Gamma = +0.2} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{short 5 stock}}_{\Gamma = 0} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{short 5 puts}}_{\Gamma = -0.2}P=Γ=+0.210 calls​​+Γ=0short 5 stock​​+Γ=−0.2short 5 puts​​ Γp=0.2+0+(−0.2)=0\Gamma_p = 0.2 + 0 + (-0.2) = \mathbf{0}Γp​=0.2+0+(−0.2)=0

The portfolio is gamma neutral.

Note the shape of the solution: the stock leg fixed delta and contributed nothing to gamma; the option leg fixed gamma. Which raises the obvious question.

Why stock cannot hedge gamma​

Stocks do not have gamma — but they do have delta.

And this is provable in one line. Gamma is the rate of change of delta with respect to the underlying:

Γ=∂(Δ)∂S\Gamma = \frac{\partial (\Delta)}{\partial S}Γ=∂S∂(Δ)​

For a share, the delta is always ±1 — +1 if you buy it, −1 if you sell it. It never changes, no matter what the price does. So the delta is a constant, and the derivative of a constant is zero:

Γstock=∂(±1)∂S=0\Gamma_{\text{stock}} = \frac{\partial (\pm 1)}{\partial S} = \mathbf{0}Γstock​=∂S∂(±1)​=0

The practical consequence:

  • Buying or selling stock has no impact on the portfolio's gamma.
  • But it does affect the portfolio's delta, by ±1 per share.

Compare with an option, whose delta of (say) 0.5 does move as the stock moves — that changing delta is exactly what gamma measures. So gamma can only be hedged with another option, never with the underlying.

Delta hedging versus gamma hedging​

They are not the same objective, and conflating them is the common mistake:

Delta hedgingGamma hedging
AimMake the delta of the portfolio zero (Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0)Stabilise the delta (Γ=0\Gamma = 0Γ=0) — the delta may stay at 0.4, it just stops moving
Directional biasEliminates directional riskReduces the sensitivity of delta to changes in the asset
InstrumentStockAnother option

The primary goal of gamma hedging is to neutralise gamma — to neutralise the change in delta with respect to a change in the underlying — rather than to make delta zero.

Which means gamma hedging on its own leaves you still carrying directional bias: you can have Γ=0\Gamma = 0Γ=0 while Δ≠0\Delta \neq 0Δ=0. A stable delta is not the same as no delta.

Why gamma hedging is important​

While delta hedging neutralises the exposure to small price movements in the underlying asset, it does not account for gamma risk — delta itself can change significantly as the underlying asset moves.

That is the whole case, and it splits into three benefits.

a) Stabilise delta. A gamma hedge reduces the fluctuation in delta, making the portfolio less sensitive to large price changes in the underlying asset.

The division of labour between the two hedges is a question of size:

Move in the asset priceHandled by
Small fluctuationDelta hedging
Large fluctuationGamma hedging — delta hedging fails here

Delta hedging is a local approximation. It is correct for the next penny and increasingly wrong after that; the bigger the move, the more of the error is gamma.

b) Protects against larger price movements. By addressing gamma risk directly, the hedge protects the portfolio from large fluctuations rather than only from small ones.

c) Reduces rebalancing cost. This is the one that pays for itself. Gamma hedging makes delta more stable, which means traders do not need to rebalance delta as frequently → lower transaction costs.

Recall delta hedging's second challenge: continuous rebalancing is expensive precisely because delta keeps moving. Gamma hedging attacks the cause instead of paying the symptom.

Practical considerations​

a) Most effective in volatile markets. Gamma hedging is generally more effective during volatile market conditions, or when you see large price fluctuations. In calm markets it may not be necessary at all — if the asset is barely moving, delta is barely moving, and there is little gamma risk to pay for removing.

b) Cost. Gamma hedging means buying or selling options, and that comes with a cost. It can be costly, since it requires both the frequent adjustment and the cost of purchasing additional options. Options carry spread and premium in a way that shares do not — hedging gamma is structurally more expensive than hedging delta.

So the decision is a trade: pay the option premium to stop paying the rebalancing bill, and only when the market is moving enough for that to be worth it.

Hedging both delta and gamma at once is the subject of the next note.

Summary​

  • Gamma hedging neutralises gamma risk — the risk that your delta itself moves as the underlying moves.
  • Position gamma is positive when long options, negative when short, so positive gamma is cancelled by selling options.
  • Worked: 10 calls at Δ\DeltaΔ 0.5 / Γ\GammaΓ 0.02 → portfolio Δ=5\Delta = 5Δ=5, Γ=0.2\Gamma = 0.2Γ=0.2; short 5 stock zeroes delta; short 5 puts at Γ=−0.04\Gamma = -0.04Γ=−0.04 zeroes gamma.
  • Stock has zero gamma because its delta is the constant ±1 and the derivative of a constant is zero — so gamma must be hedged with options.
  • Delta hedging makes delta zero; gamma hedging makes delta stable. They are different goals, and Γ=0\Gamma = 0Γ=0 does not imply Δ=0\Delta = 0Δ=0.
  • It matters because it stabilises delta, protects against large moves, and cuts rebalancing costs — delta hedging alone only covers small moves.
  • It is most effective in volatile markets and often unnecessary in calm ones, and it costs more than delta hedging because the instrument is an option, not a share.

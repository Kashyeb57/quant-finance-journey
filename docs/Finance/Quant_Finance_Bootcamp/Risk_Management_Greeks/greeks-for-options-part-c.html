Greeks for Options – Part C (Theta)

Delta and gamma are about the stock moving. Theta is about nothing happening at all — the option losing value simply because a day went by.

Definition​

Theta measures the sensitivity of the option price with respect to time. It represents how much the price of an option falls as time passes (each day).

Θ=∂V∂t=change in option pricechange in time\Theta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = \frac{\text{change in option price}}{\text{change in time}}Θ=∂t∂V​=change in timechange in option price​

Theta is also called time decay, because an option loses its value as we approach maturity. An option contract bought at t=0t=0t=0 and expiring at t=3t=3t=3 months is, all else equal, worth a little less every single day of that journey.

Formally: it tells how much the price of the option will decrease as each day passes, holding all other factors constant.

A worked reading​

You own a call option:

Current stock price$100
Strike price$100
Time to expiry30 days
Theta−$0.10 per day

As every day passes, the value of your option decreases by $0.10.

  • If theta is negative, the option price declines by that much.
  • If theta is positive, the option price increases by that much.

Who time works for​

This is the part that matters in practice.

Theta is negative for a LONG position — long call and long put. When you are the buyer, theta works against you.

Think of an ice cube. As time passes, does the ice cube melt? Yes. Time is working against the ice cube — and against you.

Theta is positive for a SHORT position — short call and short put. As time passes, the short position benefits from time decay.

Think of renting out a house. You collect rent from the person staying there. The more days they stay, the more rent they pay. Time is working in support of you.

Long⇒Θ<0 (against you)Short⇒Θ>0 (in your favour)\text{Long} \Rightarrow \Theta < 0 \text{ (against you)} \qquad \text{Short} \Rightarrow \Theta > 0 \text{ (in your favour)}Long⇒Θ<0 (against you)Short⇒Θ>0 (in your favour)

This is the mirror image of gamma, which favours the long. The option seller collects decay but carries accelerating directional risk; the buyer pays decay for that convexity. That trade-off is the heart of option trading.

The shape of decay​

Time decay is not linear. It is slow while the option is young and accelerates sharply into the final days:

Why: intrinsic and extrinsic value​

Theta only makes sense once the option price is split in two.

Intrinsic value — the real value of the option if exercised right now:

Call: ST−KPut: K−ST\text{Call: } S_T - K \qquad\qquad \text{Put: } K - S_TCall: ST​−KPut: K−ST​

If that is negative the option would not be exercised, so OTM intrinsic value is 0. (With K=100K=100K=100 and the stock at 110, a call's intrinsic value is 10.)

Extrinsic value — also called time value:

The additional value beyond the intrinsic value, which accounts for factors such as time to expiration and volatility.

Putting them together:

Option price=Intrinsic value+Extrinsic value\text{Option price} = \text{Intrinsic value} + \text{Extrinsic value}Option price=Intrinsic value+Extrinsic value

Theta eats the extrinsic value only. Intrinsic value is untouched by the calendar — it is arithmetic on today's prices. So an option's exposure to time decay is exactly its exposure to extrinsic value.

Theta by moneyness​

Everything follows from that last sentence.

In the money​

ITM options have a significant amount of intrinsic value, so the price is less dependent on time decay. As expiration approaches, the intrinsic value dominates and the extrinsic value becomes smaller and smaller — so theta is smaller.

At the money​

Here ST=KS_T = KST​=K, so the intrinsic value is exactly zero and the option price is extrinsic value only. It is pure time value — and there is more of it here than anywhere else.

Theta is highest for ATM options. They have the greatest potential to lose value as time passes.

Out of the money​

Intrinsic value is zero as well, but the extrinsic value is small. Worked through — an OTM call with K=110K = 110K=110 while the stock sits at S=100S = 100S=100:

Case 1 — 30 days to expiryThere is still a real chance the stock climbs to 110, so the option still has some time value. Decay is relatively slow.
Case 2 — 5 days to expiryIn a normal market it is highly unlikely the stock travels from 100 to 110. Intrinsic value is zero and the remaining extrinsic value evaporates → theta is high, and decay accelerates in the final days.

So the conclusion is two-sided, and both halves matter:

  • Theta decay is relatively slower for an OTM option at longer maturity…
  • …but as expiration approaches, the chance of becoming ITM diminishes and theta accelerates — the option loses its value much faster.

Summary​

  • Θ=∂V/∂t\Theta = \partial V / \partial tΘ=∂V/∂t — the option's daily bleed, all else equal. A theta of −$0.10 costs you ten cents a day.
  • Long → theta negative, time against you (the melting ice cube). Short → theta positive, time for you (collecting rent).
  • Option price = intrinsic + extrinsic, and theta only consumes the extrinsic part.
  • Theta is highest ATM (pure time value), lower ITM and OTM.
  • Decay is convex — slow early, accelerating hard into the last days.

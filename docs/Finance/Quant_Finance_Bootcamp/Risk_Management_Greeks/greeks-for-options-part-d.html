Greeks for Options – Part D (Vega)

Delta and gamma track the stock. Theta tracks the calendar. Vega tracks uncertainty itself.

Definition​

Vega measures the sensitivity of the option price with respect to implied volatility — how much the price of the option will change for a 1% change in the implied volatility of the asset.

V=∂V∂σ\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}V=∂σ∂V​

where VVV is the option price and σ\sigmaσ the volatility, so ∂σ\partial \sigma∂σ is the change in volatility.

Implied versus historical volatility​

The distinction is simply which direction in time you are looking:

Historical volatilityVolatility of the past — what the asset actually did
Implied volatilityVolatility of the future — what the market currently expects

Vega is about the implied one. Historical volatility is measured; implied volatility is a market expectation baked into today's option prices, and it can sit above or below the historical figure.

Volatility itself gets modelled two broad ways:

FamilyModels
Time-series modelsARCH, GARCH, EWMA (and the wider AR, MA, ARIMA, ARIMAX, SARIMA, VAR family)
Stochastic-volatility modelsHeston, SABR, the 3/2 model

A worked reading​

A call option:

Stock price$100
Strike$100
Time to expiry30 days
Vega0.15

For every 1% increase in implied volatility, the option price will increase by $0.15.

Meaning
High vegaThe option price is very sensitive to volatility — a small increase in volatility raises the option price significantly
Low vegaChanges in volatility have only a small impact on the price

Vega is never negative​

Vega is always non-negative.

Rising volatility always raises an option's value — call or put, in or out of the money. The reason is the payoff's asymmetry, and it is worth doing properly because it explains the entire Greek.

Why volatility helps the long — a call​

A long call pays max⁡(ST−K, 0)\max(S_T - K,\, 0)max(ST​−K,0), with K=100K = 100K=100. Suppose higher volatility means the stock could land at 120 or 80 instead of staying near 100:

OutcomePayoff
ST=120S_T = 120ST​=120max⁡(120−100, 0)=20\max(120 - 100,\, 0) = \mathbf{20}max(120−100,0)=20
ST=80S_T = 80ST​=80max⁡(80−100, 0)=0\max(80 - 100,\, 0) = \mathbf{0}max(80−100,0)=0

Up, you exercise and profit. Down, you simply do not exercise — no profit, no loss beyond the premium already paid. The upside is open-ended; the downside is floored at zero. More volatility therefore adds only upside.

And a put​

A long put pays max⁡(K−ST, 0)\max(K - S_T,\, 0)max(K−ST​,0), same K=100K = 100K=100:

OutcomePayoff
ST=120S_T = 120ST​=120max⁡(100−120, 0)=0\max(100 - 120,\, 0) = \mathbf{0}max(100−120,0)=0
ST=80S_T = 80ST​=80max⁡(100−80, 0)=20\max(100 - 80,\, 0) = \mathbf{20}max(100−80,0)=20

Identical logic, mirrored. Which gives the rule:

Long position (call or put)⇒vega is your friend\text{Long position (call or put)} \Rightarrow \text{vega is your friend}Long position (call or put)⇒vega is your friend Short position⇒vega works against you\text{Short position} \Rightarrow \text{vega works against you}Short position⇒vega works against you

The short side wrote that floored payoff, so every increase in volatility hands value to the buyer at the seller's expense.

Vega and moneyness​

MoneynessVega
ATMHighest
ITMLow
OTMLow

Why ATM. At the money, ST=KS_T = KST​=K — the option sits exactly on the knife edge. Even a small change in volatility can tip it either way: to 101 and it is ITM, to 99 and it is OTM. That balance is where extra uncertainty is worth the most.

Why not ITM. A deep in-the-money option is already going to be exercised; its value is mostly intrinsic. More volatility barely moves that, so the impact on the option price is small.

Why not OTM. Far out of the money, the option is unlikely to be exercised regardless, so extra volatility changes little.

Note this is the same bell shape as gamma — both peak at the money, for the same underlying reason: that is where the option's fate is least decided.

Vega and time to expiry​

MaturityVega
Long-term (e.g. 3 months)High
Short-term (e.g. 5 days)Low

Short duration means the stock has less room to travel, so the option price is less impacted by a change in volatility.

Think of bouncing a ball. Dropped from high up it bounces high — that is high vega. Held near the floor and released, it barely bounces — low vega. Volatility needs room, and room is time.

How vega compares with the others​

Worth holding the four together, because their behaviour with time diverges:

More time to expiryPeak at
DeltaHigher— (monotonic S-curve)
GammaLowerATM
ThetaLower per day (until it accelerates near expiry)ATM
VegaHigherATM

Gamma and vega share the ATM peak but move in opposite directions with time: a short-dated ATM option is a gamma instrument, a long-dated ATM option is a vega instrument.

Summary​

  • V=∂V/∂σ\mathcal{V} = \partial V / \partial \sigmaV=∂V/∂σ — the price change per 1% move in implied volatility. Vega 0.15 means +$0.15 per point of IV.
  • Implied volatility is the market's view of the future; historical is the measured past.
  • Vega is always non-negative, because the option payoff is floored at zero: volatility adds upside without adding downside.
  • Long is long vega (calls and puts alike); short is short vega.
  • Vega is highest ATM — the knife edge — and low both ITM and OTM.
  • Long-dated options carry high vega; short-dated carry little — the bouncing ball needs height.

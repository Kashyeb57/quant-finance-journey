Introduction to Derivative (Call & Put Option) – Part A

A forward and a future are an obligation. An option is a right, but not the obligation. Everything in this note follows from that one difference.

The difference, in one example​

You hold an option contract to buy XYZ stock at $100, three months from now. At t=3t = 3t=3 the market can be anywhere. Two cases:

Case 1 — the stock rises to $110. Your contract lets you buy at $100 while the market charges $110. You exercise: buy at 100, and you are $10 better off.

Case 2 — the stock falls to $90. Your contract says $100, the market says $90. Exercising would mean paying $100 for something worth $90 — a $10 loss. So you do not execute the deal. You walk away.

With a forward or a future, Case 2 is not a choice: you are obligated, and you take the $10 loss. The option gives you the flexibility to buy or not.

Buyer and seller are not symmetric​

Buyer (holder) of the optionSeller (writer / underwriter)
HasThe right to buy or sell—
Obliged?Not obligatedAlways obligated to buy or sell the underlying

The buyer picks whichever outcome suits them; the seller has to live with that choice. Which raises the obvious question.

Why does the buyer pay a premium?​

Because the seller is at a structural disadvantage. They carry the obligation and have no say in whether the contract is exercised. The premium is what compensates them for taking on that obligation. It flows once, at the start, from buyer to seller:

   Buyer (long)  ──── premium ────→  Seller (short)
   holds the right                   carries the obligation

The two types​

Call option​

A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price KKK, on a predetermined date.

You would only want a fixed price to buy at if you think the price is going up.

PositionWhat you didExpectation
Long callBought the right to buyPrice will rise → bullish
Short callSold the right to buyPrice will decline → bearish

Put option​

A put option gives the holder the right to sell the underlying asset at KKK, on a predetermined date.

PositionWhat you didExpectation
Long putBought the right to sellPrice will fall → bearish
Short putSold the right to sellPrice will rise → bullish

A memory aid: a bull attacks by throwing its horns up; a bear swipes down.

Note the pairing is not "call = bullish". It is the combination: long call and short put are bullish; short call and long put are bearish.

The five characteristics of an option​

  1. Underlying asset — stocks, commodities, currencies, bonds.
  2. Strike price KKK — the price the holder can buy or sell at.
  3. Expiration date — after which the option expires worthless.
  4. Exercise style — American (exercisable at any point) or European (exercisable only at maturity). These names are conventions, not geographical locations — a European-style option is not a European product.
  5. Premium — the price paid by the holder to the seller.

Payoff​

Four positions, four formulas. The max(·, 0) is the option's whole character: you never exercise into a loss, so the intrinsic part can never go below zero.

Long call  =  max⁡(ST−K, 0)−premiumShort call  =  premium−max⁡(ST−K, 0)Long put  =  max⁡(K−ST, 0)−premiumShort put  =  premium−max⁡(K−ST, 0)\begin{aligned} \text{Long call} \;&=\; \max(S_T - K,\, 0) - \text{premium}\\[2pt] \text{Short call} \;&=\; \text{premium} - \max(S_T - K,\, 0)\\[2pt] \text{Long put} \;&=\; \max(K - S_T,\, 0) - \text{premium}\\[2pt] \text{Short put} \;&=\; \text{premium} - \max(K - S_T,\, 0) \end{aligned}Long callShort callLong putShort put​=max(ST​−K,0)−premium=premium−max(ST​−K,0)=max(K−ST​,0)−premium=premium−max(K−ST​,0)​

The short is always the negative of the long — the seller's payoff is −(long payoff)-(\text{long payoff})−(long payoff). Derivatives are a zero-sum game: what one side gains, the other loses, exactly.

Note also who pays and who receives:

  • Long call and long put → pay the premium.
  • Short call and short put → receive the premium.

The short put can also be written premium+min⁡(ST−K, 0)\text{premium} + \min(S_T - K,\, 0)premium+min(ST​−K,0), which is the same thing.

Breakeven for a put is K−premium=94K - \text{premium} = 94K−premium=94.

Worked from the contract's side: you bought the right to sell XYZ at $100. If the market goes to $110 you do not execute — why sell at 100 what fetches 110? If it drops to $90 you do execute, gaining K - S_T = 100 - 90 = \10$.

The four diagrams​

The asymmetry is the point:

PositionLossProfit
Long callLimited (the premium)Unlimited — the price can keep rising
Short callUnlimitedLimited (the premium)
Long putLimited (the premium)Limited — the price can only fall to zero
Short putLimitedLimited (the premium)

Selling a naked call is the one position here with genuinely unbounded loss.

Summary​

  • An option is a right, not an obligation — the holder chooses, the writer must comply.
  • The premium exists to compensate the writer for carrying that obligation.
  • Call = right to buy, put = right to sell; long = holds the right, short = carries the obligation and receives the premium.
  • Bullish: long call, short put. Bearish: short call, long put.
  • All four payoffs are built from max(·, 0), and long and short are exact negatives — a zero-sum game.
  • Breakeven is K+premiumK + \text{premium}K+premium for a call and K−premiumK - \text{premium}K−premium for a put.

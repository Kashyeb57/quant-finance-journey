Introduction to Derivative (Operation of Margin Account)

A futures contract is marked to market daily: gains and losses are settled every single day rather than accumulating quietly until expiry. That is what stops a losing party from walking away at maturity owing more than they can pay — but it only works if there is money in the account to settle against.

That money is the margin.

The four terms​

a) Margin account — a type of account that traders must maintain with their broker in order to engage in trading.

b) Initial margin (IM) — the minimum amount of capital a trader must deposit in the margin account to open a futures position.

  • It is usually a small percentage of the contract's total value, typically ranging from about 2% to 12%.
  • Where in that range depends on the volatility of the asset — a more volatile underlying demands more margin.
  • It acts as collateral, ensuring the trader can cover potential losses.

c) Maintenance margin (MM) — a threshold level the margin account must maintain after the trade has been initiated. It sits below the initial margin.

d) Margin call — if the account balance falls below the maintenance margin, the broker issues a margin call, and the trader must deposit additional funds to bring the account back up to the initial margin level.

Variation margin is the name for that top-up: the amount of money required to restore the account to the initial margin level.

Note the asymmetry that trips people up: the call is triggered by falling below MM, but the amount you must pay restores you all the way to IM — not merely back to MM.

A minimal example​

Initial margin $12,000, maintenance margin $9,000:

BalanceBelow MM?Margin call
Day 1$11,000No—
Day 2$10,000No—
Day 3$8,000YesDeposit $4,000 to restore $12,000

That $4,000 is the variation margin.

Worked example — two gold futures contracts​

This is the full mechanic, day by day.

A trader contacts their broker to buy two December gold futures contracts. The current futures price is $1,250 per ounce, and the contract size is 100 ounces, so the trader has contracted to buy 200 ounces at that price.

Reading it:

  • Day 6 ends at $9,240. Uncomfortable, but still above the $9,000 maintenance margin — no call.
  • Day 7 the price drops to 1,229.90, a $1,260 loss, taking the balance to $7,980. That is below the maintenance margin, so the broker calls for $4,020 — the amount that restores the account to the $12,000 initial margin, not to the $9,000 threshold.
  • Day 11 is the big one: gold falls 17.10 in a day, which on 200 ounces is a $3,420 loss. Balance $8,220 → a second call for $3,780.
  • The position then recovers. By day 16 the balance is $15,180.

Sanity check on the whole thing: the trader deposited $12,000 + $4,020 + $3,780 = $19,800 and finished with $15,180 — a net loss of $4,620, which is exactly the cumulative column, and exactly (1,226.90 − 1,250.00) × 200. The margin account never changes the economics; it only guarantees the money is there to settle them.

Position limits and exercise limits​

Two related restrictions, both established by regulatory bodies (and so differing by country).

Position limit​

The maximum number of option contracts an investor or entity can hold on the same side of the market.

"Same side" means positions expressing the same directional view:

SidePositions
Bullishlong call and short put
Bearishshort call and long put

So a long call and a short put count together against the limit — you cannot evade it by splitting a bullish bet across two instruments.

With a limit of, say, 250,000 contracts, you cannot hold more than 250,000 option contracts on one side. Since one option contract is 100 shares, that is

250,000×100=25,000,000 shares250{,}000 \times 100 = 25{,}000{,}000 \text{ shares}250,000×100=25,000,000 shares

Why the limit exists: to stop large entities from controlling too much of the market in a particular stock's options, and so to prevent market manipulation or liquidity problems.

Exercise limit​

Restricts the number of option contracts an investor or entity can exercise over a specific period — typically five consecutive business days.

The position limit caps what you can hold; the exercise limit caps what you can act on in a window. Both close off the same abuse from different sides.

Summary​

  • Futures are marked to market daily, and the margin account is what makes that settlement possible.
  • Initial margin opens the position (~2–12% of contract value, driven by volatility). Maintenance margin is the lower threshold you must stay above.
  • Fall below the MM and you get a margin call — and the variation margin you must post restores the account to the initial margin, not the threshold.
  • On the gold example, two calls ($4,020 and $3,780) kept a position alive whose total loss was $4,620.
  • Position limits cap contracts held on the same side of the market; exercise limits cap contracts exercised over ~5 business days. Both exist to prevent manipulation and liquidity problems.

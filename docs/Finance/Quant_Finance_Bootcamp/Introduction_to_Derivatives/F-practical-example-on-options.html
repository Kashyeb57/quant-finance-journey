Introduction to Derivative (Practical Example on Options)

Two worked examples, both with the numbers carried all the way through. The first uses options to reduce risk; the second uses them to magnify it. Same instrument, opposite purposes.

1. Hedging — the protective put​

The situation. An investor owns 1,000 shares of Company A, currently trading at $28. They are concerned about a possible share price decline over the next two months and want protection.

The choice. Buy ten July put option contracts on the company's stock with a strike price of $27.50.

ParameterValue
Shares held1,000 @ $28
Strike KKK$27.50
Premium$1 per share
Contract size100 shares
Contracts bought10 → covers 1,000 shares
Total cost1×100×10=1 \times 100 \times 10 =1×100×10= $1,000

The strategy costs $1,000, but guarantees the shares can be sold for at least $27.50 per share during the life of the option.

That sentence is the whole idea of a hedge: you have bought a floor.

What it does, price by price​

Two separate P/Ls, then the combination:

Option P/L  =  max⁡(27.5−S, 0)×1000  −  1000Stock P/L  =  (S−28)×1000\begin{aligned} \text{Option P/L} \;&=\; \max(27.5 - S,\, 0) \times 1000 \;-\; 1000\\[2pt] \text{Stock P/L} \;&=\; (S - 28) \times 1000 \end{aligned}Option P/LStock P/L​=max(27.5−S,0)×1000−1000=(S−28)×1000​

Read the last column. Below $27.50 the combined result is frozen at −$1,500, no matter how far the stock falls. At $20 the unhedged shareholder has lost $8,000; the hedged one has lost $1,500. Above the strike the put expires worthless and the investor keeps the upside, minus the $1,000 premium.

Where does −$1,500 come from? The floor is $27.50 per share, so the shares are worth at least 27.50 × 1000 = $27,500; subtract the $1,000 premium and the position is worth $26,500 against the $28,000 it started at.

maximum loss  =  28,000−(27.50×1000)+1,000  =  $1,500\text{maximum loss} \;=\; 28{,}000 - (27.50 \times 1000) + 1{,}000 \;=\; \$1{,}500maximum loss=28,000−(27.50×1000)+1,000=$1,500

The trade-off: the investor gave up $1,000 of certain money to convert an open-ended downside into a known, capped one. On the option leg alone, breakeven is at S=26.50S = 26.50S=26.50 (where the $1 premium is exactly recovered).

2. Speculation — 100 shares versus 20 call contracts​

The situation. A speculator believes a stock will increase in value over the next two months. The stock currently trades at $20. A two-month call option with a $22.50 strike is selling for $1. The speculator is willing to invest $2,000.

Two ways to spend the same $2,000:

AlternativeWhat it buys
A100 shares at $20 = $2,000
B2,000 call options (20 contracts) at $1 = $2,000

The two outcomes​

December stock priceA: 100 sharesB: 20 call contracts
$15 (down)−$500−$2,000
$27 (up)+$700+$7,000

The same $2,000, the same view, and wildly different consequences. On the way up the option returns ten times what the shares do. On the way down it loses everything, while the shareholder is down only a quarter of their stake.

Across the range​

Option P/L  =  max⁡(S−22.5, 0)×2000  −  2000Stock P/L  =  (S−20)×100\begin{aligned} \text{Option P/L} \;&=\; \max(S - 22.5,\, 0) \times 2000 \;-\; 2000\\[2pt] \text{Stock P/L} \;&=\; (S - 20) \times 100 \end{aligned}Option P/LStock P/L​=max(S−22.5,0)×2000−2000=(S−20)×100​

Note the shape. The option line is flat at −$2,000 for every price below the $22.50 strike — the speculator is wiped out identically whether the stock ends at 15.5 or 22.5. Then it turns and climbs at 2,000 per dollar, twenty times the slope of the share position. Breakeven is at

S=K+premium=22.50+1=$23.50S = K + \text{premium} = 22.50 + 1 = \$23.50S=K+premium=22.50+1=$23.50

which is where the option column crosses zero.

The lesson​

Leverage the position with options.

The option gives exposure to 2,000 shares for the price of 100. That is the leverage: a large exposure for a small outlay. It amplifies the return — and amplifies the loss in exactly the same proportion. The shareholder needs the stock to fall to zero to lose everything; the option holder only needs it to finish a cent below $22.50.

Summary​

  • The same instrument hedges or speculates depending on how it is used against the position you already hold.
  • A protective put buys a floor: the 1,000-share holder converted an $8,000 loss at $20 into a capped $1,500, at a cost of $1,000, while keeping the upside.
  • A long call used for speculation gives leverage: $2,000 controlled 2,000 shares instead of 100, turning a $700 gain into $7,000 — and a $500 loss into a total $2,000 loss.
  • Breakeven in both cases is the strike adjusted by the premium: K−PK - PK−P for the put leg, K+PK + PK+P for the call.

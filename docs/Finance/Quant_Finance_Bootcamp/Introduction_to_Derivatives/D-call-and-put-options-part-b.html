Introduction to Derivative (Call & Put Option) – Part B

Part A covered what calls and puts are and what they pay. This note covers the two things that decide whether you would exercise at all — moneyness — and the corporate actions that quietly rewrite the contract underneath you.

Moneyness​

The moneyness of an option refers to the relationship between the strike price of the option and the current price of the underlying asset.

So it is a statement about the pair (ST, K)(S_T,\, K)(ST​,K), and there are only three possible relationships:

ST>KST=KST<KS_T > K \qquad S_T = K \qquad S_T < KST​>KST​=KST​<K

Three names follow:

TermMeaning
In the money (ITM)Money coming in — exercising is profitable
At the money (ATM)No profit, no loss
Out of the money (OTM)Money going out — exercising would lose

Which is which depends on the option​

For a call (right to buy), you want the market above your strike:

Call
ITMST>KS_T > KST​>K → profit
ATMST=KS_T = KST​=K → zero
OTMST<KS_T < KST​<K → loss

For a put (right to sell), it is exactly reversed — you want the market below your strike:

Put
ITMST<KS_T < KST​<K
ATMST=KS_T = KST​=K
OTMST>KS_T > KST​>K

Side by side:

CallPut
ITMST>KS_T > KST​>KST<KS_T < KST​<K
ATMST=KS_T = KST​=KST=KS_T = KST​=K
OTMST<KS_T < KST​<KST>KS_T > KST​>K

One thing to be careful about​

Moneyness does not consider the premium. It is purely the intrinsic relationship between STS_TST​ and KKK.

That matters, because an option can be in the money and still lose you money overall. From Part A: a long call with K=95K = 95K=95 and a premium of 1, at ST=95.5S_T = 95.5ST​=95.5, is ITM — but the payoff is 0.5−1=−0.50.5 - 1 = -0.50.5−1=−0.5. Moneyness tells you whether you would exercise; it does not tell you whether the trade was profitable.

So moneyness decides one thing at expiry: whether the option is exercised or left to expire.

Exercise style, in time​

The other half of "when can I act" is the exercise style:

  • American — exercisable at any point during the life of the contract. Its moneyness can be checked, and acted on, all the way along.
  • European — exercisable only at maturity. Moneyness only matters on the final date.

Corporate actions: splits and dividends​

An option contract is written on a number of shares at a strike price. A corporate action can change what a "share" even means — so the contract has to be adjusted, or the holder would be arbitrarily enriched or wiped out.

Stock split​

In a 3-for-1 stock split, three new shares are issued for each existing share. One XYZ share becomes three XYZ shares.

The company is not worth more afterwards; the same value is just divided into more, cheaper pieces. So the option contract is adjusted to keep the holder exactly where they were.

The rule:

QuantityAdjustment for an nnn-for-1 split
Strike priceDivided by nnn → K/nK/nK/n
Number of sharesMultiplied by nnn

Worked example — 3-for-1​

Suppose you hold a call option:

Pre-splitPost-split (3-for-1)
Strike KKK$9090/3=90/3 =90/3= $30
Shares per contract100100×3=100 \times 3 =100×3= 300
Total strike value90×100=90 \times 100 = 90×100= $9,00030×300=30 \times 300 = 30×300= $9,000

Identical. The split changed the units, not your position.

Worked example — 4-for-1​

The same rule with n=4n = 4n=4: strike becomes K/4K/4K/4, share count becomes ×4\times 4×4.

Pre-splitPost-split
Strike$100$25
Shares100400
Total$10,000$10,000

Again unchanged — which is the entire point of the adjustment.

Stock dividend​

A stock dividend involves a company issuing more shares to its existing stakeholders.

A stock dividend is quoted as a percentage, and the trick is to convert that percentage into an equivalent split:

100%dividend %  =  shares you must already hold to receive one new share\frac{100\%}{\text{dividend }\%} \;=\; \text{shares you must already hold to receive one new share}dividend %100%​=shares you must already hold to receive one new share
Stock dividend100/%100/\%100/%MeaningEquivalent split
10%101 new share for every 10 held11-for-10
20%51 new share for every 5 held6-for-5
25%41 new share for every 4 held5-for-4

Once you have the equivalent split, the adjustment is the same rule as before. For a 25% stock dividend (a 5-for-4 split):

shares  →  ×54K  →  ×45\text{shares} \;\to\; \times \tfrac{5}{4} \qquad\qquad K \;\to\; \times \tfrac{4}{5}shares→×45​K→×54​

Worked example — a put, and a 25% stock dividend​

Consider a put option contract to sell 100 shares of the company at $15 per share. The company then announces a 25% stock dividend.

100%/25%=4100\% / 25\% = 4100%/25%=4, so one new share for every four held — a 5-for-4 split.

Pre-dividendPost-dividend
Number of shares100100×54=100 \times \tfrac{5}{4} =100×45​= 125
Strike KKK$1515×45=15 \times \tfrac{4}{5} =15×54​= $12
Total value100×15=100 \times 15 =100×15= $1,500125×12=125 \times 12 =125×12= $1,500

Unchanged, exactly as with a split.

Cash dividend — the exception​

This one catches people out:

A cash dividend does NOT adjust the option contract.

If XYZ pays $3 per share in cash, the option contract is left alone. Only stock dividends and stock splits change the strike and the share count.

(Cash dividends do still matter for pricing — they are an input to the Black-Scholes-Merton model — but they do not rewrite the contract terms.)

Summary​

  • Moneyness is the relationship between STS_TST​ and KKK — ITM, ATM or OTM.
  • For a call ITM means ST>KS_T > KST​>K; for a put it is reversed, ST<KS_T < KST​<K.
  • Moneyness ignores the premium. It decides whether you exercise, not whether you profited.
  • American options can be exercised any time, European only at maturity.
  • A stock split divides the strike and multiplies the share count, leaving the contract's total value untouched.
  • A stock dividend does the same thing, once you convert the percentage into an equivalent split via 100%/dividend %100\%/\text{dividend }\%100%/dividend % — a 25% dividend is a 5-for-4 split.
  • A cash dividend does not adjust the contract at all.

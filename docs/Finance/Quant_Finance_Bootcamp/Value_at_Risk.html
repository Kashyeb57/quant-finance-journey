Value at Risk Modeling

How much could we lose, and how often? Three ways to compute VaR, two ways to backtest it, and the Expected Shortfall measure that answers what VaR refuses to.

📄️ What is Value at Risk?

The question VaR answers, its two components, what it is used for — and the one thing it deliberately does not tell you, which is what the rest of the module is about.

📄️ VaR — the Historical Method

The non-parametric approach: sort the actual returns, count to the right rank, multiply by portfolio value. Plus the portfolio-weighting step and the rounding choice the notes leave open.

📄️ Historical VaR in Excel

Building the historical method on real JPM/MS/BAC data — the SUMPRODUCT that collapses three stocks into one return, and two routes to the answer that disagree by $527.

📄️ VaR — the Variance-Covariance Method

The parametric approach: assume normality and the whole distribution collapses to a mean and a standard deviation. Includes the wrong way to combine volatilities and why the covariance matrix fixes it.

📄️ Variance-Covariance VaR in Excel

Building the parametric method on JPM/MS/BAC — and because the sheet computes the wrong portfolio volatility alongside the right one, the diversification benefit comes out as a number.

📄️ VaR — the Monte Carlo Method

Simulating the portfolio forward with GBM — why the random draws must be correlated, how Cholesky supplies that correlation, and the two things this method buys you that the other two cannot.

📄️ Monte Carlo VaR in Excel

Simulating 1,000 correlated paths with a verified 3x3 Cholesky — and a live formula chain that no longer reproduces the VaR the sheet reports.

📄️ Backtesting — the Traffic Light Approach

Basel's test for whether a VaR model is any good: count the days the loss exceeded VaR, and read off green, yellow or red. All three methods tested on the real portfolio.

📄️ Backtesting — the Kupiec Test

The statistical version of the traffic light: a likelihood-ratio test on the breach count, with a chi-square p-value. All three VaR models tested and verified against the workbook.

📄️ Expected Shortfall

The measure that answers what VaR refuses to: how bad is the tail? Covers the two shortcomings of VaR that motivated it — tail-risk ignorance and the failure of subadditivity.

📄️ Expected Shortfall in Excel

The workbook has no ES sheet — so this builds one on the same portfolio, with a single AVERAGEIF, and shows how much of the tail VaR was hiding.

Every model so far has been about a **stock**. This module changes the
underlying: the thing being modelled is the **interest rate**.

## Why a stochastic model at all

Look at daily data for, say, the **US 3-year Treasury rate** and you see the
same thing you see in a stock chart — a jagged, unpredictable line.

> **The pattern that we see in the interest rate is random in nature.**

So it needs the same treatment: **for modelling the randomness of interest
rates, we use a stochastic interest rate model.**

This module covers two of them — **Vasicek** and **CIR** — in theory, and then
in Excel, where there are two separate jobs: **calibrate** the model (with MLE)
and then **implement** it.

## The model

> The Vasicek model was introduced by **Oldrich Vasicek in 1977.** It is a
> stochastic interest rate model used to model **how interest rates will move in
> the future.**

$$
\boxed{\;dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\;}
$$

Term by term:

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $r_t$ | The **interest rate at time $t$** |
| $dr_t$ | The **change** in the interest rate |
| $a$ | **Speed of mean reversion** — how fast the rate returns to the mean |
| $b$ | **Long-term average rate** |
| $dt$ | Small change in time — **1/252** |
| $\sigma$ | **Volatility** of the interest rate |
| $dW_t$ | **Wiener process / Brownian motion** |

**Where $b$ comes from**: take the historical data — the notes use 3-year
Treasury data — and **average it.** That average is $b$.

**Why 1/252**: a year has 365 days, minus Saturdays, Sundays and holidays,
leaving **252 trading days.**

$$
dt = \frac{1}{252}
$$

**What $dW_t$ is doing:** it **represents the random, unpredictable fluctuation
that you see in the interest rate over time**, and in practice it is generated
exactly as in the previous module:

$$
dW_t = Z_t \times \sqrt{\Delta t}
$$

where $Z_t$ is a **random number generated from a standard normal
distribution.**

### Why rates fluctuate in the first place

The notes list the real-world causes that $dW_t$ is standing in for:

- **economic news**
- **policy change**
- **market sentiment**

None of them are predictable, which is why they are modelled as Brownian motion
rather than forecast.

## Two parts, doing two different jobs

The equation splits cleanly, and this split is the whole model:

| Term | Name | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $a(b - r_t)\,dt$ | **Deterministic part** / **mean reversion** | **Drives the interest rate toward the long-term $b$** |
| $\sigma\,dW_t$ | **Stochastic part** | **Captures the random and unpredictable deviation from the deterministic trajectory** |

The first term knows where the rate *should* go. The second term is everything
that stops it getting there in a straight line.

## Mean reversion, in two cases

The mean-reverting component is $a(b - r_t)\,dt$, and its **sign** is what
matters. Note that $a$ and $dt$ are both always positive — $dt = 1/252$, and $a$
is a speed. So the sign of the whole term is decided by $(b - r_t)$ alone.

**Case 1 — the rate is above the mean ($r_t > b$).**

$$
r_t > b \;\Longrightarrow\; (b - r_t) < 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; dr_t < 0
$$

The change is negative, so the model **adjusts the short rate downward.**

**Case 2 — the rate is below the mean ($r_t < b$).**

$$
r_t < b \;\Longrightarrow\; (b - r_t) > 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; dr_t > 0
$$

The change is positive, so the model **adjusts the short rate upward.**

> The direction of the short rate depends on the values of **$b$ and $r_t$**.

| | | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $r_t > b$ | $dr_t < 0$ | **Downwards** |
| $r_t < b$ | $dr_t > 0$ | **Upwards** |

That is mean reversion in one line: **the further the rate is from $b$, and the
larger $a$ is, the harder it is pulled back.**

## Strengths

**a) It is intuitive.** The concept of mean reversion **aligns with how many
people think about interest rates** — returning to a normal level after a period
of being high or low. The model's central assumption is one most practitioners
already hold.

**b) It has an analytical solution.** It is possible to **derive a closed-form
solution for certain use cases** — notably **bond pricing** and **risk
management.**

The notes make "closed form" concrete with the simplest possible example: $x + 2
= 5$ solves directly to $x = 3$. No iteration, no simulation. Vasicek admits
that kind of solution, which is why it survived — you can price a bond with a
formula rather than a
[Monte Carlo run](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-method).

## Limitations

**a) It can produce negative interest rates.**

> It can produce a **negative interest rate**, which can be **unrealistic** in
> the case where interest rates are high.

The culprit is identifiable: the **stochastic part $\sigma\,dW_t$** is
**responsible for negative interest rates.** Since $dW_t$ is normally
distributed, it is unbounded below — a large enough negative draw can push $r_t$
through zero, and nothing in the equation stops it.

Whether that is a flaw depends on where you are:

| | Rates |
| --- | --- |
| **USA, India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, China** | **Positive** |
| **Germany, Japan** | Have seen **negative** rates |

So negative rates are not impossible in reality — but a model that produces them
in a high-rate economy is producing nonsense.

**This is exactly what the CIR model fixes**, and it is why the next note
exists.

**b) It is a single-factor model.**

> The model is driven by **market risk, which is the only factor affecting the
> change in the interest rate.**

One source of randomness, one $dW_t$. Everything that moves rates in reality —
policy, inflation, credit, liquidity — is compressed into that single term.

## Summary

- Interest rates are **random in nature**, so they are modelled with a
  **stochastic interest rate model.**
- **Vasicek (Oldrich Vasicek, 1977):**
  $dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$.
- $a$ is the **speed of mean reversion**, $b$ the **long-term average rate**
  (the historical average), $\sigma$ the volatility, $dt = 1/252$, and
  $dW_t = Z_t\sqrt{\Delta t}$ the Brownian shock.
- The equation is **deterministic mean reversion + a stochastic deviation**: one
  term pulls toward $b$, the other knocks it off course.
- **Above the mean the drift is negative, below it positive** — the rate is
  always being pulled back toward $b$.
- **Strengths:** intuitive, and it has a **closed-form solution** usable for
  bond pricing and risk management.
- **Limitations:** it can generate **negative rates** (the $\sigma\,dW_t$ term is
  unbounded below), and it is a **single-factor model.**

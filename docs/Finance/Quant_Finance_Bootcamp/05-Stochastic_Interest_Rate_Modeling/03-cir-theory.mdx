The [Vasicek model](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/vasicek-theory)
ended on a specific flaw: **it can produce negative interest rates.** CIR is the
answer to that, and the fix is one square root.

## Introduction

> The **CIR model** — **Cox-Ingersoll-Ross** — is a mathematical model which is
> used to **model interest rate movement.**

It was **developed in 1985 by John C. Cox, Jonathan E. Ingersoll and Stephen A.
Ross.**

> The CIR interest rate model is also called a **"one factor model"**, because it
> describes interest rate movement as driven by a **sole source of market risk.**

Both models share that property:

$$
\text{CIR} \rightarrow f(\text{market risk})
\qquad\qquad
\text{Vasicek} \rightarrow f(\text{market risk})
$$

One component, one factor. Which is a simplification, because in reality the
interest rate responds to **monetary policy, market sentiment, inflation** and
**economic policy** — four forces compressed into one term.

## The mathematical framework

$$
\boxed{\;dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t\;}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $dr_t$ | **Change in $r_t$** (the interest rate) |
| $a$ | **Speed of mean reversion** |
| $b$ | **Mean of the interest rate** |
| $dt$ | Change in time |
| $\sigma$ | **Volatility** of the interest rate |
| $r_t$ | Interest rate |
| $dW_t$ | **Wiener process / Brownian motion** — the random fluctuation that happens in the interest rate |

Set it beside Vasicek and the difference is a single factor:

$$
\text{Vasicek:}\quad dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t
$$

$$
\text{CIR:}\quad dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,\underbrace{\sqrt{r_t}}_{\text{the addition}}\,dW_t
$$

> $\sqrt{r_t}$ is the **additional component that is added to the Vasicek
> equation** to give CIR.

**The drift is untouched.** Mean reversion works exactly as before — the two
cases are identical to Vasicek's:

| | | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $b > r_t$ | $b - r_t > 0 \Rightarrow dr_t > 0$ | **Adjust the interest rate upwards** |
| $b < r_t$ | $b - r_t < 0 \Rightarrow dr_t < 0$ | **Adjust the interest rate downward** |

with $a$, $dt$ and $\sqrt{r_t}$ all positive, so the sign of $(b - r_t)$ decides
everything.

## Why the square root fixes negative rates

> **The square root of any number is always positive.**

That is the whole argument, and it works through the *size* of the shock rather
than its sign:

In **Vasicek**, the shock is $\sigma\,dW_t$ — the **same size at every rate
level.** So when $r_t$ is already near zero, a normal draw of the usual
magnitude can push it straight through into negative territory.

In **CIR**, the shock is $\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t$. As $r_t$ falls toward zero,
$\sqrt{r_t}$ falls toward zero too, so **the randomness switches itself off
exactly where it would do damage.** At $r_t = 0$ the diffusion term vanishes
entirely and only the drift $ab\,dt$ remains — which is positive, pushing the
rate back up.

> **CIR $\rightarrow \sqrt{r_t} \rightarrow$ positive value $\rightarrow$
> interest rates can't go negative.**

:::note The guarantee has a condition

The notes state non-negativity as absolute. Strictly, CIR keeps the rate
**strictly positive** only when the **Feller condition** holds:

$$
2ab \ge \sigma^2
$$

If volatility is large relative to the mean-reversion pull, the rate can still
touch zero (though it cannot pass through it). Worth knowing, and it is checked
against the calibrated parameters in the
[CIR Excel note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/cir-excel).

:::

## Assumptions

**1) It assumes mean reversion.** This means the **interest rate tends to move
towards the long-term equilibrium level over time** — rising when it sits below
$b$, falling when above.

**2) It assumes $a$, $b$ and $\sigma$ are constant.**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| $a$ | speed of mean reversion |
| $b$ | long-term mean |
| $\sigma$ | volatility / standard deviation |

The notes flag this immediately with a question: for a stock ABC, **will
volatility always remain constant?** Obviously not — and the note beside it is
blunt about what that means:

> **Assumption → limitation.**

The long-term mean $b$ in particular has to be obtained by **calibrating the
model to market data**, and it is economy-specific — the US economy has one
level, India and China another.

## Vasicek versus CIR

| | Vasicek | CIR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Equation** | $dr_t = a(b - r_t)dt + \sigma\,dW_t$ | $dr_t = a(b - r_t)dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t$ |
| **Rates it can produce** | **Positive and negative** | **Positive only** |
| **Diffusion** | Constant | Proportional to $\sqrt{r_t}$ |

Which model to use is therefore **an empirical question about the economy you
are modelling**, not a matter of taste:

| Economy | Rates observed | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **USA, India, China** | Positive | **CIR** |
| **Germany, Japan** | Have gone to **0 or negative** | **Vasicek** |

The notes are direct about the mismatch: for economies whose rates are firmly
positive, a model that generates negative rates **"makes no sense"**. And
conversely — if you need to represent a genuinely negative rate, CIR
structurally cannot do it.

So the fix is not free. **CIR trades away the ability to model negative rates in
exchange for never producing spurious ones.**

## Limitations

**a) It assumes constant $a$, $b$ and $\sigma$.** In reality **these parameters
might change due to changes in market and economic conditions.** The assumption
that made the model tractable is the same one that makes it wrong over long
horizons.

**b) It is not suitable for modelling negative interest rates.** The
$\sqrt{r_t}$ term **makes the interest rate non-negative** — which is a feature
in the US and a defect in Germany.

**c) It is a single-factor model.**

$$
dr_t = \underbrace{a(b - r_t)\,dt}_{} + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,\underbrace{dW_t}_{\text{random fluctuation}}
$$

with $r_t$ as the sole **market risk factor**. But **interest rates can rely on
multiple factors** — **market sentiment** and **economic policies** among them —
and a one-factor model cannot separate them.

**d) No jumps or discontinuities.**

> The CIR model assumes a **continuous path** for the interest rate. This may
> **not reflect real-world market behaviour**, which can include **jumps or
> sudden changes due to unexpected events.**

A central bank announcement moves rates in a step, not a wiggle. Brownian motion
has continuous paths by construction, so no amount of parameter tuning will
reproduce that.

## Other interest rate models

The two in this module are the start of a family:

| | Model |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **Vasicek** |
| 2 | **CIR** |
| 3 | **Hull-White** (John C. Hull) |
| 4 | **Black-Karasinski** |
| 5 | **Libor Market Model** |
| 6 | **Heath-Jarrow-Morton** |

Each relaxes one of the assumptions above — time-varying parameters, log-normal
rates, or modelling the whole forward curve rather than a single short rate.

## Summary

- **CIR (Cox, Ingersoll and Ross, 1985)** is a **one-factor** stochastic
  interest rate model:
  $dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t$.
- It is **Vasicek plus $\sqrt{r_t}$** in the diffusion term. The drift, and
  therefore the mean-reversion behaviour, is unchanged.
- **That square root is what prevents negative rates**: as $r_t$ approaches
  zero the shock shrinks to nothing, leaving only the upward drift. (Strictly,
  under the **Feller condition** $2ab \ge \sigma^2$.)
- **Use CIR where rates are positive** (US, India, China); **use Vasicek where
  they can go negative** (Germany, Japan). Neither dominates.
- **Limitations:** constant parameters, cannot model negative rates, only one
  risk factor, and **no jumps** — real rates move in steps that a continuous
  path cannot reproduce.
- The wider family includes **Hull-White, Black-Karasinski, the Libor Market
  Model and Heath-Jarrow-Morton.**

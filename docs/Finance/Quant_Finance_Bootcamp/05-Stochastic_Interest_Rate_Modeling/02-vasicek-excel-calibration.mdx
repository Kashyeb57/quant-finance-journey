The [theory note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/vasicek-theory)
gives the model. It does not give the numbers. The three parameters — $a$, $b$
and $\sigma$ — have to come from **observed data**, and the method for
extracting them is **maximum likelihood estimation.**

## What MLE is

> MLE is a method which is used to find the **best-fitting parameters for a
> mathematical model, based on observed data.**

Applied here, that means finding $a$, $b$ and $\sigma$ in

$$
dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t
$$

The principle in the notes' own words:

> **Adjust the parameters in such a way that the values generated from the
> Vasicek model closely align with the actual data.**

So there are two columns of numbers — what the rate **actually did**, and what
the **model says** it should have done — and calibration is the search for the
parameters that make the gap between them as small (as *likely*) as possible.

## The five steps

**1. Calculate $dr_t$** — for both the **actual data** and the **Vasicek model.**

| | Actual rate | Vasicek | $\Delta r$ actual | $\Delta r$ Vasicek |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $T$ | 5.0% | 4.5% | | |
| $T-1$ | 5.1% | 4.75% | 5.0% − 5.1% | 4.5% − 4.75% |
| $T-2$ | 5.05% | 4.6% | 5.1% − 5.05% | 4.75% − 4.6% |
| $T-3$ | 5.15% | ⋮ | ⋮ | ⋮ |

**2. Calculate the residual** — the **error term:**

$$
\text{Residual} = \Delta r_{\text{Actual}} - \Delta r_{\text{Vasicek}}
$$

**3. Take a normal distribution of the residual**, with

$$
\text{Mean} = 0 \qquad\qquad \text{Std deviation} = \sigma
$$

The residual is assumed **normally distributed around a mean of 0.** This is not
an arbitrary assumption — it falls straight out of the model. The deterministic
part is what the model predicted; whatever is left over *is* the realisation of
$\sigma\,dW_t$, and $dW_t$ is normal with mean zero by construction. So the
residuals should look like normal draws, and $\sigma$ is exactly the parameter
that says how wide they should be.

**4. Take the log likelihood.** Each residual gets a probability density from
that normal distribution; take the **log** of each.

**5. Sum all the logs**, and **maximise** the sum — **by changing $a$, $b$ and
$\sigma$.**

> We are trying to **maximise the likelihood of the function by changing the
> parameters** $a$, $b$, $\sigma$.

## Why take the logarithm?

> Since we are working with **multiple time steps**, the **total likelihood of
> the entire dataset is the product of the likelihood of each individual time
> step.**

$$
L = A \times B \times C \times \cdots
$$

And a product of many probability densities is awkward — multiply 251 small
numbers together and you underflow to zero. The logarithm turns it into
something tractable:

$$
\log(A \times B \times C \times \cdots) = \log(A) + \log(B) + \log(C) + \cdots
$$

A **product becomes a sum.** Since the logarithm is monotonically increasing,
whatever maximises the log-likelihood also maximises the likelihood — so nothing
is lost by optimising the easier quantity.

## The Excel build

**The data** is the US Treasury yield curve — the workbook carries full
**2024** and **2023** sheets with the 1 Mo through 10 Yr columns. The
calibration uses the **3-month** rate, 252 observations.

The `Vasicek Parameter Est (MLE)` sheet is the five steps as seven columns:

| Col | Label | Formula | Step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | 3 Mo | the raw quote, e.g. 5.52 | |
| D | Actual | `=C2/100` → 0.0552 | convert percent to decimal |
| E | Return | `=(D2/D3-1)` | |
| F | r(t)−r(t−1) | `=D2-D3` | **1 — actual $dr$** |
| G | dr (V) | `=$O$2*($O$3-D3)*$O$7` | **1 — model $dr$** |
| H | residual | `=F2-G2` | **2 — the error** |
| I | pdf | `=NORMDIST(H2,0,$O$4,FALSE)` | **3 — normal density** |
| J | ln(pdf) | `=LN(I2)` | **4 — log** |
| L2 | Sum | `=SUM(J2:J252)` | **5 — the objective** |

Two details worth noticing.

**Column G has no random term.** It is only $a(b - r_{t-1})\,dt$ — the
deterministic part. That is correct and it is the point: the model's *prediction*
is the drift, and the residual in column H is what the stochastic term must have
supplied. Calibration then asks which $\sigma$ makes those residuals most
plausible.

**`NORMDIST(..., FALSE)`** returns the **density**, not the cumulative
probability. The `FALSE` is what selects the PDF — exactly what a likelihood
needs.

### The parameters

| Cell | Parameter | Initial guess | **Calibrated** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| O2 | mean reverting speed $a$ | — | **0.10232419** |
| O3 | long-term mean $b$ | 0.05 | **0.05740609** |
| O4 | volatility $\sigma$ | `=STDEV.S(E2:E252)` | **0.00018280** |
| O5 | initial rate $r_0$ | `=D2` → 0.0552 | |
| O6 | n_days | 252 | |
| O7 | $dt$ | `=1/O6` → 0.00396825 | |

Maximised log-likelihood: **1804.0966**. Excel's Solver does the maximising,
changing O2:O4 to push L2 as high as it will go.

## Did the calibration work?

Worth checking rather than assuming, and there is a clean test. If MLE is doing
its job, the fitted $\sigma$ should come out close to the **actual standard
deviation of the daily rate changes** — because that is what the residuals are.

| | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Std deviation of daily changes in the 3-month rate | **0.00018294** |
| **Calibrated $\sigma$** | **0.00018280** |

They agree to four significant figures. **The calibration recovered the
volatility correctly** — a small but real confirmation that the likelihood was
set up right.

:::note Where the data is thinner than it looks

Two things about the input are worth knowing.

**The 3-month rate is quoted to two decimal places**, so **86 of the 251 daily
changes (34%) are exactly zero** — the rate simply printed the same number two
days running. That rounding does not break the fit above, but it means the
"daily volatility" being measured is partly a quantisation artifact.

**$a$ and $b$ are weakly identified over one year.** The fitted
$a = 0.1023$ implies a mean-reversion half-life of

$$
\frac{\ln 2}{a} = \frac{0.693}{0.1023} \approx 6.8 \text{ years}
$$

Over a **single year** of data, a process reverting on a 6.8-year timescale
barely reverts at all — so the data contains very little information about where
$b$ actually is. That is why the fitted $b$ (5.74%) sits noticeably above the
sample average rate (5.49%) without the fit objecting. $\sigma$ is pinned down
by 251 observations; $a$ and $b$ are not.

This is a general feature of short-rate calibration, not a mistake in the sheet
— mean reversion is a slow phenomenon and short samples struggle to see it.

:::

## Simulating the calibrated model

The `Vasicek Simulation (MLE)` sheet takes the three fitted parameters and runs
the model forward — **10 simulations across columns E:N, 252 days down the
rows.**

| Cell | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| B1, B2, B3 | the calibrated $a$, $b$, $\sigma$ |
| B4 | $r_0 = 0.0552$ |
| B5 / B6 | n_days = 252, $dt$ = `=1/B5` |
| E2:N2 | `=$B$4` — every path starts at $r_0$ |

and the step itself:

```excel
E3 = $B$1*($B$2-E2)*$B$6 + $B$3*SQRT($B$6)*NORM.S.INV(RAND()) + E2
```

which reads directly as the Euler discretisation of the SDE:

$$
r_{t+\Delta t} = \underbrace{a(b - r_t)\,\Delta t}_{\text{drift}} + \underbrace{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z}_{\text{shock}} + \underbrace{r_t}_{\text{where we were}}
$$

The **shape** of that step is right — drift scaled by $\Delta t$, shock by
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$ — unlike the
[Monte Carlo workbooks in Module 4](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-european-option),
which scaled the drift by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ too. But the **units of $\sigma$**
do not line up between the two sheets.

:::danger The simulation is 15.9× too smooth — a units mismatch

The calibration sheet fits $\sigma$ with

```excel
=NORMDIST(H2, 0, $O$4, FALSE)
```

where `H2` is a **one-day** residual. So the fitted $\sigma$ is already a
**per-day standard deviation** — and indeed it came out at 0.00018280 against a
measured daily-change standard deviation of 0.00018294.

The simulation sheet then multiplies it by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ *again*:

```excel
$B$3*SQRT($B$6)*NORM.S.INV(RAND())
```

which treats that same number as an **annualised** volatility. The result:

| | Daily shock std |
| --- | --- |
| Actual 3-month rate | **0.00018294** |
| Simulated by the sheet | **0.00001152** |
| Ratio | **0.0629** — that is $1/\sqrt{252}$ |

The simulated paths are **15.9× less volatile than the data they were
calibrated on.** You can see it in the saved values: the first path moves
0.0552 → 0.05518 → 0.05520 in steps of about 2×10⁻⁵, where the real rate moves
in steps of about 1.8×10⁻⁴.

**Either** fix works, not both:

- calibrate against the scaled quantity — `NORMDIST(H2, 0, $O$4*SQRT($O$7), FALSE)` — so $\sigma$ is annualised, **or**
- drop the $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ from the simulation — `$B$3*NORM.S.INV(RAND())` — keeping $\sigma$ per-day.

:::

Because the rate is added back rather than compounded, the process is
**arithmetic** — which is precisely why it can cross zero into negative
territory, the limitation the
[CIR model](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/cir-theory)
sets out to fix.

## Summary

- **MLE finds the best-fitting parameters for a model given observed data** —
  here $a$, $b$ and $\sigma$ for Vasicek.
- The five steps: **compute $dr$ for actual and model → take the residual →
  assume it is normal with mean 0 and std $\sigma$ → take logs → sum and
  maximise** by changing the parameters.
- **The log turns a product of 251 densities into a sum**, which is both
  numerically stable and easier to maximise; being monotonic, it does not change
  where the maximum is.
- The model column carries **only the drift**; the residual is what
  $\sigma\,dW_t$ must have contributed.
- Calibrated on the 3-month Treasury: **$a$ = 0.1023, $b$ = 0.0574,
  $\sigma$ = 0.00018280**, log-likelihood 1804.10.
- **The fit checks out** — $\sigma$ matches the sample standard deviation of
  daily changes (0.00018294) to four significant figures. But **$a$ and $b$ are
  weakly identified**, because a 6.8-year half-life barely shows up in one year
  of data.
- The simulation sheet has the **right Euler shape** but a **units mismatch**:
  the calibration returns a per-day $\sigma$ and the simulation multiplies it by
  $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ again, making the paths **15.9× too smooth.** Fix it at one
  end or the other, not both.

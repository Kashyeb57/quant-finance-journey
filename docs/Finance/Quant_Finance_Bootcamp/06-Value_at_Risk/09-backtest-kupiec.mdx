The [traffic light](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/backtest-traffic-light)
sorts a breach count into three buckets. **Kupiec asks a sharper question: is
this breach count statistically consistent with the confidence level you
claimed?**

## What it is

> The **Kupiec Test**, referred to as the **Proportion of Failure (POF) test**,
> is a **statistical method used to evaluate the accuracy of a VaR model.**

> The model was developed by **Paul Kupiec in 1995.**

> This test specifically checks **whether the number of times losses exceeded
> the VaR number is consistent with the confidence interval set by the model.**

### The idea in one example

**1,000 days**, 1-day VaR at **95% CI**. How many breaches *should* there be?

$$
\text{Alpha}(5\%) \times n(1000) = \frac{5}{100} \times 1000 = \mathbf{50}
$$

> **The model specified that 50 breaches are allowed.**

Observe 50 and the model is behaving exactly as advertised. Observe 5, or 200,
and something is wrong. Kupiec turns "how far from 50 is too far?" into a formal
test.

## How the test works

**a) Define the parameters.**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **VaR CI** | e.g. 95% — *losses should exceed the VaR estimate only 5% of the time* |
| **Time horizon** | how many **trading days** are we considering |

**b) Count the number of breaches** — days where **loss > VaR number.**

**c) Apply the formula.**

$$
\boxed{\;LR_{POF} = -2\ln\left[\frac{p^{x}\,(1-p)^{\,n-x}}{\hat{p}^{\,x}\,(1-\hat{p})^{\,n-x}}\right]\;}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $p$ | **Expected probability of exceedance (breach)** |
| $\hat{p}$ | **Observed probability of exceedance** |
| $x$ | **Number of violations (failures)** |
| $n$ | **Total number of observations** |

with

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}
$$

and $p$ set by the confidence level:

| CI | $p$ |
| --- | --- |
| **99% VaR** | **1%** |
| **95% VaR** | **5%** |
| **90% VaR** | **10%** |

**What the ratio is doing.** The numerator is the likelihood of seeing $x$
breaches *if the model's claimed rate $p$ is true*. The denominator is the
likelihood under the rate you *actually observed*. If the model is right, the
two are close and the ratio is near 1, so the log is near 0 and **$LR$ is
small.** A large $LR$ means the observed breach rate is hard to reconcile with
the claimed one.

## Reading the result

> The **$LR$ follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom.**

| Result | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| **p-value > 5%** | **Model is performing adequately** |
| **p-value < 5%** | **Model is not performing adequately** |

:::note Why 1 degree of freedom, and why chi-square

**Why 1 degree of freedom?** Because **we are testing a single parameter.** In
the Kupiec test the key parameter being tested is **the rate at which actual
losses exceed the VaR number** — one number, one degree of freedom.

**Why chi-square?** The **likelihood ratio test statistic involves comparing two
models by looking at how much more likely the data is under one model compared
to the other.** That comparison **results in a test statistic that is not
normally distributed, because it involves a ratio — which naturally leads to the
chi-square distribution.**

:::

## The Excel test

The `Backtesting (Kupiec)` sheet runs on the same 251 days and the same three
VaR numbers as the traffic-light sheet, with breach counts from the identical
`COUNTIF(...,TRUE)`.

```excel
X26 = -2*LN(((X22^X23)*(1-X22)^(X24-X23))/((X25^X23)*(1-X25)^(X24-X23)))
X27 = CHISQ.DIST.RT(X26, 1)
```

| | Variance-Covariance | Historical | Monte Carlo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p$ (expected) | 0.01 | 0.01 | 0.01 |
| **$x$ (breaches)** | **3** | **2** | **3** |
| $n$ | 251 | 251 | 251 |
| **$\hat{p} = x/n$** | 0.01195219 | 0.00796813 | 0.01195219 |
| **$LR$** | **0.09094408** | **0.11250372** | **0.09094408** |
| **p-value** | **0.76298036** | **0.73731150** | **0.76298036** |
| **p-value > 5%?** | ✅ **Reliable** | ✅ **Reliable** | ✅ **Reliable** |

**Every one of these values reproduces exactly** when the likelihood ratio and
the chi-square survival function are computed independently — the sheet is
correct.

### What the numbers mean

All three p-values are around **0.74–0.76**, nowhere near the 5% threshold. In
plain terms: **if the models were perfectly calibrated, you would see breach
counts at least this far from 2.51 about three-quarters of the time.** Two or
three breaches is entirely unremarkable.

For reference, the chi-square critical value at 1 degree of freedom and 5% is
**3.8415** — the observed $LR$ values of 0.09 and 0.11 are far below it.

Note the shape of the test: **Monte Carlo and variance-covariance give identical
results** because they had identical breach counts (3). Kupiec sees only the
count, not the VaR level that produced it — a model with a much larger VaR that
happened to be breached 3 times would score the same.

:::note What "reliable" does and does not mean

The sheet's own footnote makes the point, and it is worth stating plainly: **a
high p-value is a failure to reject, not a proof of adequacy.**

With $n = 251$ and an expected 2.51 breaches, this test has very little power.
It would take a substantial number of breaches before the p-value dropped below
5%. Passing Kupiec means *"the data gives no evidence the model is broken"* —
which is not the same as *"the model is right."*

This is also the test's known weakness: it looks only at **how many** breaches
occurred, not **when**. Three breaches spread across the year and three
breaches on three consecutive days score identically, though the second is far
more alarming. Tests of *independence* (such as Christoffersen's) exist to cover
that gap.

:::

## Traffic light versus Kupiec

| | Traffic Light | Kupiec |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Type** | Rule of thumb, bucketed | **Statistical hypothesis test** |
| **Output** | Green / yellow / red | **p-value** |
| **Basis** | Fixed breach bands | **Likelihood ratio, chi-square** |
| **Adapts to $n$ and CI?** | No — bands assume 250 days at 99% | **Yes** — $p$ and $n$ are inputs |

The traffic light is designed to be applied uniformly by a regulator across many
institutions without argument. Kupiec is the more flexible instrument: change
the confidence level or the sample length and the test adjusts, where the
traffic light's bands would have to be recalculated.

Both agreed here — **all three models pass.**

## Summary

- The **Kupiec (POF) test**, from **Paul Kupiec, 1995**, checks whether the
  **observed breach count is statistically consistent with the claimed
  confidence level.**
- $LR_{POF} = -2\ln\left[\frac{p^x(1-p)^{n-x}}{\hat{p}^x(1-\hat{p})^{n-x}}\right]$,
  with $\hat{p} = x/n$ and $p$ = 1%, 5% or 10% for 99%, 95%, 90% CI.
- $LR$ follows a **chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom** — one,
  because a **single parameter** (the exceedance rate) is being tested.
- **p-value > 5% → adequate; < 5% → not adequate.**
- On the real portfolio all three models pass comfortably: **LR ≈ 0.09–0.11,
  p-values ≈ 0.74–0.76**, against a 5% critical value of **3.8415**. All values
  independently verified.
- **Passing is a failure to reject, not proof of correctness** — and Kupiec sees
  only the breach *count*, never whether the breaches clustered.

The third and most general method. Where variance-covariance assumes a shape and
the historical method reuses the past, Monte Carlo **generates the future** —
thousands of times.

## Why reach for it

The deciding factor is the **kind of instrument** in the portfolio:

| Instrument type | Examples | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Linear** | Forwards, futures | Simpler models suffice |
| **Non-linear** | **Options** | **Monte Carlo** |

> The Monte Carlo method for calculating VaR involves **simulation techniques to
> model the stock price (GBM)**, providing a **probabilistic assessment of the
> prices that the stock can take.**

> The Monte Carlo method is useful when dealing with **complex portfolios (e.g.
> options)** for which the market risks are **not captured by simpler models.**

## The engine — geometric Brownian motion

$$
S_t = S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]
$$

the same equation built in
[Module 4](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/model-stock-gbm-excel):

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $\mu$ **(drift)** | **Average return of the asset over time** |
| $\sigma$ **(volatility)** | **Measures the variation or dispersion of the asset's return** |
| $W_t$ **(Wiener process / Brownian motion)** | **Random variable generated from a standard normal distribution** |

### Why generate random variables at all?

> The random variable **represents the random fluctuation in the stock price.**

And those fluctuations are **driven by market sentiment, news and other
unpredictable factors** — the same list the
[Vasicek note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/vasicek-theory)
gave for interest rates. Unpredictable by definition, so modelled as noise
rather than forecast.

## The part that is specific to portfolios: correlation

Simulating one stock is straightforward. Simulating **three at once** is where
this method earns its complexity.

> It is **very important to model the correlation between different assets
> (stocks).**
>
> **Ignore correlation between assets → inaccurate result.**

A standard normal generator gives **uncorrelated** draws. If you feed three
independent random numbers into three GBMs, you get three stocks that wander
independently — which is not the portfolio you own. JPM, MS and BAC move
together, and a simulation that pretends otherwise will understate the chance
that they all fall on the same day.

### How: Cholesky decomposition

> **Using Cholesky decomposition.**

$$
\Sigma = L \cdot L^{T}
$$

where $\Sigma$ is the **correlation matrix** and $L$ the **Cholesky
decomposition matrix** — a **lower triangular matrix.**

For two stocks:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad\qquad
L = \begin{bmatrix} L_{11} & 0 \\ L_{21} & L_{22} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying out $L L^T$ and matching entries gives three equations:

$$
L_{11}^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; L_{11} = 1
$$

$$
L_{11} \cdot L_{21} = \rho \;\Longrightarrow\; L_{21} = \rho
$$

$$
L_{21}^2 + L_{22}^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho^2 + L_{22}^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; L_{22} = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{\;L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1-\rho^2} \end{bmatrix}\;}
$$

This is the identical derivation done in
[Module 4's Cholesky note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/cholesky-decomposition-gbm),
now put to a different use — there it correlated two simulated stocks, here it
correlates the risk factors inside a VaR calculation.

### The pipeline

$$
\text{stock price} \rightarrow \text{GBM} \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{uncorrelated random numbers}}_{\text{standard normal}} \rightarrow \times\, L \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{correlated random variables}}_{\text{feed into GBM}}
$$

Generate independent noise, multiply by $L$, and the output carries the
correlation structure measured from the historical data.

## Advantages

**a) Flexibility.** The method **can model complex portfolios with multiple risk
factors and capture the interaction between them.** Nothing in the procedure
cares whether the payoff is linear — you simulate the path and read the value
off it.

**b) Tail risk.** It is **effective in estimating the risk of extreme losses,
especially when the portfolio includes non-linear instruments.** Unlike the
historical method, it is not limited to losses that have already happened; it
can generate a worse day than any in the sample.

## Limitations

**a) Computationally intensive.** It **requires significant computational
resources, especially as the number of simulations and portfolio complexity
increase.** Going from 1,000 to 100,000 simulations, or from 3 stocks to 8, is
a cost and a **time factor** — and unlike the other two methods, the answer is
not instant.

**b) Model risk.**

> **Assuming something in a model = a chance of failing = model risk.**

> The Monte Carlo method is **heavily dependent on the assumption of the
> probability distribution and the correlation between the risk factors.
> Incorrect assumptions can lead to misleading results.**

This is the honest cost of the flexibility. The historical method assumes only
that the past repeats. Monte Carlo assumes a *distribution*, a *correlation
structure*, and a *process* — three places to be wrong instead of one.

## The full procedure

| Step | |
| --- | --- |
| **a** | **Download the data** (JPM, MS, BOA from Yahoo Finance) |
| **b** | **Calculate the returns** → gives the **mean** and **standard deviation** |
| **c** | **Generate random variables from the standard normal distribution** — these are **uncorrelated** |
| **d** | **Convert uncorrelated into correlated** by **multiplying the Cholesky matrix with the uncorrelated random variables** |
| **e** | **Simulate the stock price** with GBM |
| **f** | **Calculate the returns** of each simulated path, then the **portfolio return** $w_1 r_1 + w_2 r_2$ |
| **g** | **Arrange the returns in ascending order** → take the **VaR return** by the percentile approach (99%, 95%, 90%) |
| **h** | **VaR return × portfolio value = VaR** |

Note where this lands: steps **g** and **h** are **exactly the historical
method.** Once the simulation has produced its cloud of returns, they are sorted
and read off in the same way. The difference is only in **where the returns came
from** — generated rather than observed.

Which is the neat summary of all three methods:

| Method | Source of the return distribution |
| --- | --- |
| **Historical** | The returns that **actually happened** |
| **Variance-covariance** | A **normal curve** fitted to mean and variance |
| **Monte Carlo** | Returns **generated** from a simulated process |

Three different answers to the same question, and the
[Excel note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-monte-carlo-excel)
shows how far apart they land.

## Summary

- Monte Carlo VaR **simulates the stock price with GBM** to get a
  **probabilistic assessment of the prices the stock can take**, then reads VaR
  off the simulated distribution.
- It is the method for **non-linear instruments (options)**, where simpler
  models do not capture the risk.
- **The random draws must be correlated.** Independent noise would produce a
  portfolio whose stocks never fall together — **ignoring correlation gives an
  inaccurate result.**
- **Cholesky supplies the correlation**: $\Sigma = LL^T$, giving
  $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\end{bmatrix}$ for two
  assets; multiply the uncorrelated draws by $L$.
- **Advantages:** flexibility with multiple interacting risk factors, and real
  **tail-risk** estimation — it can generate losses worse than any observed.
- **Limitations:** **computationally intensive**, and **model risk** — it
  assumes a distribution *and* a correlation structure *and* a process.
- The **last two steps are identical to the historical method** — sort and
  multiply. Only the origin of the returns differs.

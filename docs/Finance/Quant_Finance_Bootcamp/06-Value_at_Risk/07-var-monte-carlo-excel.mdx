The most elaborate sheet in the workbook, and the one where the
[theory](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-monte-carlo-theory)
actually needs all its machinery: 1,000 simulations of three correlated stocks.

## Inputs

Estimated from the same 251 daily returns:

| | JPM | MS | BAC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Drift** $\mu$ `=AVERAGE(...)` | 0.00152743 | 0.00059988 | 0.00001693 |
| **Volatility** $\sigma$ `=STDEV.S(...)` | 0.01625597 | 0.01716490 | 0.01821889 |
| **$S_0$** (as of 8/1/2023) | 157.179993 | 90.940002 | 31.358679 |

The volatilities match the
[variance-covariance sheet](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-variance-covariance-excel)
exactly, as they should — same data, same estimator. Weights are again
**0.3 / 0.4 / 0.3** on **\$1M**.

## The correlation matrix and its Cholesky factor

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0.66461117 & 0.81987838 \\
0.66461117 & 1 & 0.75292400 \\
0.81987838 & 0.75292400 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The sheet builds $L$ with the standard recursion, one cell per entry:

```excel
AS15 = SQRT(AS10)                          ' L11
AS16 = AS11/AS15                           ' L21
AT16 = SQRT(AT11 - AS16^2)                 ' L22
AS17 = AS12/AS15                           ' L31
AT17 = (AT12 - AS17*AS16)/AT16             ' L32
AU17 = SQRT(AU12 - AS17^2 - AT17^2)        ' L33
```

$$
L =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0.66461117 & 0.74718940 & 0 \\
0.81987838 & 0.27840822 & 0.50028822
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Verified independently: $LL^T$ reproduces $\Sigma$ to eight decimal places.**
This is the 2×2 case from the
[theory note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-monte-carlo-theory)
generalised to three assets, and it is built correctly.

## The simulation chain

| Range | Holds | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Q6:S1005** | **3,000 uncorrelated draws** (1,000 sims × 3 stocks) | `=NORM.S.INV(RAND())*SQRT(1/252)` |
| **AC6** | **Correlated draws**, all at once | `=TRANSPOSE(MMULT(AS15:AU17, TRANSPOSE(Q6:S1005)))` |
| **AX:AZ** | Simulated prices | `=$AS$7*EXP(($AS$5-(($AS$6^2)/2))*(1/252)+$AS$6*AC6)` |
| **BH:BJ** | Per-stock returns | `=AX6/$AX$2-1` |
| **BK** | Portfolio return | `=SUMPRODUCT($BC$6:$BE$6, BH6:BJ6)` |
| **BM:BN** | Rank and sorted return | — |

The single array formula in `AC6` is elegant: `MMULT` multiplies the whole
1000×3 block of independent draws by $L$ in one operation, turning the entire
simulation correlated at a stroke.

## The two VaR blocks

**Rank method** — and here the position lands exactly, no rounding needed:

| | 99% | 95% | 90% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total returns | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Alpha | 0.01 | 0.05 | 0.10 |
| **Position** `=1000*alpha` | **10** | **50** | **100** |
| Return at that position | −0.03544727 | −0.02720183 | −0.02011471 |
| **1-day VaR** | **−\$35,447.27** | **−\$27,201.83** | **−\$20,114.71** |

**Percentile method** — `PERCENTILE.EXC(BN7:BN1006, alpha)`:

| CI | Percentile | vs rank method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | −\$35,441.69 | **\$5.58 apart** |
| 95% | −\$27,185.40 | \$16.43 |
| 90% | −\$20,095.05 | \$19.66 |

:::tip Why the two methods nearly agree here, but not in the historical sheet

In the [historical sheet](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-historical-excel)
the same two approaches differed by **\$527**. Here they differ by **\$5.58** —
nearly a hundred times less.

The reason is **sample size**. `PERCENTILE.EXC` interpolates at rank
$p(n+1)$:

| | $n$ | $p(n+1)$ at 99% | Interpolates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Historical | 251 | 2.52 | **52% of the way** between order stats 2 and 3 |
| Monte Carlo | 1,000 | 10.01 | **1% of the way** between order stats 10 and 11 |

With 1,000 observations the tail is densely packed and the interpolation barely
moves. **More simulations make the choice of estimator stop mattering** — which
is one more argument for running a lot of them.

:::

:::danger The live formulas no longer reproduce the reported VaR

The sorted column `BN` — the one both VaR blocks read from — contains
**hardcoded numbers, not formulas.** The live calculation chain
(`Q → AC → AX → BH → BK`) still runs, and it produces something very different:

| | Standard deviation of portfolio returns |
| --- | --- |
| **Frozen values in `BN`** (used for VaR) | **0.016325** |
| **Live chain in `BK`** (recalculates) | **0.001003** |
| Historical portfolio $\sigma$ (cov matrix) | 0.015624 |

The frozen values are right — 0.0163 against a historical 0.0156 is ordinary
sampling variation on 1,000 draws, and their mean (0.000723) matches the
portfolio mean (0.000737).

**The live chain is wrong by a factor of about $\sqrt{252}$.** Sorting what it
currently produces would give:

| CI | Reported (frozen) | Live chain would give |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **99%** | −\$35,447.27 | **−\$2,430.01** |
| 95% | −\$27,201.83 | −\$1,720.68 |
| 90% | −\$20,114.71 | −\$1,280.47 |

**The cause.** $\mu$ and $\sigma$ come from `AVERAGE` and `STDEV.S` of **daily**
returns — they are already per-day quantities. But the GBM formula then applies
a time step of $1/252$:

$$
S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu_{\text{daily}} - \frac{\sigma_{\text{daily}}^2}{2}\right)\times\frac{1}{252} + \sigma_{\text{daily}}\, Z \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{252}}\right]
$$

In daily units, one day is $dt = 1$, not $1/252$. Either **drop the $1/252$ and
the $\sqrt{1/252}$**, or **annualise first** ($\mu_{ann} = 252\mu_d$,
$\sigma_{ann} = \sqrt{252}\,\sigma_d$) and keep them. Doing neither shrinks the
shock by $\sqrt{252} \approx 15.9$.

This is **the same units error** found in the
[Module 4 Monte Carlo sheets](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-european-option)
and the
[Module 5 simulation sheets](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Stochastic_Interest_Rate_Modeling/vasicek-excel-calibration)
— a per-period parameter re-scaled as though it were annual. It is the single
most common defect in this course's workbooks.

**What to take from the sheet:** the structure — the Cholesky construction, the
`MMULT` correlation step, the sort-and-rank blocks — is correct and worth
copying. The frozen results are correct. The live GBM line is not.

:::

## The three methods, finally compared

| 1-day VaR @ | 99% CI | 95% CI | 90% CI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Variance-Covariance** | −\$35,609.28 | −\$24,961.76 | −\$19,285.60 |
| **Historical Simulation** | −\$36,253.51 | −\$24,160.85 | −\$19,101.28 |
| **Monte Carlo Simulation** | −\$35,447.27 | −\$27,201.83 | −\$20,114.71 |
| **Spread** | **\$806 (2.2%)** | **\$3,041 (12.6%)** | **\$1,013 (5.3%)** |

At **99%** the three agree within about 2% — three quite different assumptions
converging on the same answer, which is the reassuring case.

At **95%** they do not: Monte Carlo says \$27,202 where historical says
\$24,161. The reason is instructive. Monte Carlo draws from a **normal**
distribution, so its 95% quantile sits where normality says it should. The
historical method uses the **actual** returns, whose shape near the 5% mark
apparently differs from normal. Variance-covariance, which *also* assumes
normality, lands close to Monte Carlo — as you would expect from two methods
sharing an assumption.

So the disagreement is not noise: **it is the normality assumption showing up**,
and it shows up more at 95% than at 99% on this data.

## Summary

- 1,000 simulations of three correlated stocks, driven by GBM, on the same
  JPM/MS/BAC portfolio.
- The **3×3 Cholesky factor is built correctly** and **verified** — $LL^T$
  reproduces the correlation matrix exactly.
- One array formula, `TRANSPOSE(MMULT(L, TRANSPOSE(draws)))`, correlates all
  3,000 draws at once.
- Rank and percentile methods differ by only **\$5.58** here versus **\$527** in
  the historical sheet — because **1,000 observations make the interpolation
  irrelevant.**
- **The sorted returns are frozen values; the live formula chain is
  $\sqrt{252}$ too small** because daily $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are scaled by
  $1/252$. Re-sorting the live output would report \$2,430 instead of \$35,447.
- Across all three methods the **99% figures agree within 2.2%**, but the **95%
  figures differ by 12.6%** — the normality assumption, made visible.

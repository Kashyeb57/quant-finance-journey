Where the
[historical method](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-historical-theory)
assumed nothing about the shape of returns, this one assumes everything.

## Definition

> The variance-covariance method, also known as the **parametric method**, is
> one of the **most commonly used approaches** to calculate VaR.

**Parametric** means **assuming a certain shape**:

> We assume that the **returns are normally distributed** — the bell-shaped
> curve.

### Why assume normality?

> The variance-covariance method assumes the asset returns are normally
> distributed because **it simplifies the calculation of VaR**, as the
> distribution is **characterised by mean and variance.**

That is the entire trade. A normal distribution is **completely described by two
numbers** — its mean and its standard deviation. Once you accept that shape, you
do not need 251 sorted observations; you need $\mu$ and $\sigma$, and every
quantile follows from them.

## Step 1 — gather the data

Three stocks — **JPM, MS, BOA** — pulled from Yahoo Finance, using **adjusted
close.**

## Step 2 — calculate the returns

$$
\text{Return} = \frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}} - 1
$$

:::note Why returns and not prices

The notes make the case with two examples:

| Move | Stock price | Return |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **\$5 increase** | \$10 stock | **50%** |
| **\$5 increase** | \$100 stock | **5%** |

The same dollar move is a very different event depending on the price level.
Returns normalise that away, which is what makes stocks comparable and
combinable in a portfolio.

:::

## Step 3 — compute the mean of the portfolio

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Portfolio mean} = w_1 r_1 + w_2 r_2 + w_3 r_3\;}
$$

where $w_1, w_2, w_3$ represent **the weights of each stock in the portfolio**
and $r_1, r_2, r_3$ the **returns of the individual stocks.**

$$
w = \frac{\text{invested in individual stock}}{\text{total portfolio value}}
$$

**Worked.** A **\$100** portfolio: **A = \$50, B = \$30, C = \$20.**

| | | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $w_1$ | \$50 / \$100 | **50%** |
| $w_2$ | \$30 / \$100 | **30%** |
| $w_3$ | \$20 / \$100 | **20%** |

## Step 4 — compute the standard deviation of the portfolio

This is the step that gives the method its name, and the notes teach it by
showing the wrong answer first.

### The wrong method

$$
\sigma_p = w_1 sd_1 + w_2 sd_2 + w_3 sd_3 \qquad \boldsymbol{\times}
$$

> **What is wrong with this method?**
>
> This method **doesn't account for the interdependence (correlation or
> covariance) between the stocks in the portfolio.**

Weighting the individual standard deviations and adding them treats each stock
as if it moved in complete lockstep with the others. It does not — and the
whole value of a portfolio comes from the fact that it does not.

:::tip What the wrong formula secretly assumes

The naive sum is not arbitrary — it is the **correlation = 1** case of the
correct formula. The general expression is

$$
\sigma_p^2 = \sum_i \sum_j w_i w_j \rho_{ij}\, \sigma_i \sigma_j
$$

Set every $\rho_{ij} = 1$ and it factorises into
$\left(\sum_i w_i \sigma_i\right)^2$, so $\sigma_p = \sum_i w_i \sigma_i$ —
exactly the wrong formula.

So using it means assuming **every stock moves perfectly together.** Since real
correlations are below 1, the naive formula **overstates portfolio risk.** The
gap between the two is precisely the **diversification benefit.**

:::

### The right method

**1) Define the portfolio weights.**

$$
w = \begin{bmatrix} w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 30\% & 40\% & 30\% \end{bmatrix}
$$

**2) Build the covariance matrix $\Sigma$.**

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{Cov}(A,A) & \text{Cov}(A,B) & \text{Cov}(A,C) \\
\text{Cov}(B,A) & \text{Cov}(B,B) & \text{Cov}(B,C) \\
\text{Cov}(C,A) & \text{Cov}(C,B) & \text{Cov}(C,C)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every entry comes from one relation:

$$
\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho_{X,Y}\,\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y
$$

**The diagonal collapses.** A stock is perfectly correlated with itself, so
$\rho_{A,A} = 1$ and:

$$
\text{Cov}(A,A) = 1 \times \sigma_A \times \sigma_A = \sigma_A^2 = \text{Var}(A)
$$

and likewise $\text{Cov}(B,B) = \sigma_B^2$, $\text{Cov}(C,C) = \sigma_C^2$.
**The diagonal is the variances; the off-diagonal is the correlations.** Written
out:

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_A^2 & \rho_{A,B}\sigma_A\sigma_B & \rho_{A,C}\sigma_A\sigma_C \\
\rho_{A,B}\sigma_A\sigma_B & \sigma_B^2 & \rho_{B,C}\sigma_B\sigma_C \\
\rho_{A,C}\sigma_A\sigma_C & \rho_{B,C}\sigma_B\sigma_C & \sigma_C^2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The matrix is **symmetric** — $\text{Cov}(A,B) = \text{Cov}(B,A)$, because
correlation does not have a direction.

**3) Portfolio variance.**

$$
\text{Variance}_p = \sigma_p^2 = w^{T}\,\Sigma\,w
$$

**4) Portfolio standard deviation.**

$$
\boxed{\;\sigma_p = \sqrt{\text{Variance}} = \sqrt{w^{T}\,\Sigma\,w}\;}
$$

:::note A missing square root on page 29

The step-by-step recap at the end of the deck writes
"$\text{std dev}_p = w^T \Sigma w$" — without the square root. Page 28 has it
correctly as $\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}$.

$w^T \Sigma w$ is the **variance**; the standard deviation is its square root.
The earlier page is right.

:::

## Step 5 — compute VaR

$$
\boxed{\;\text{VaR} = \mu_p + Z \times \sigma_p\;}
$$

where **$Z$ corresponds to the confidence interval** — the number of standard
deviations out into the tail that the chosen CI sits.

This is where the normality assumption pays off. Because the distribution is
fully described by $\mu$ and $\sigma$, the quantile is just a fixed multiple of
$\sigma$ away from the mean. No sorting, no ranking, no counting — one lookup
and one multiplication.

## Conclusion — the method in five lines

> **Assume: asset returns are normally distributed** → characterised by **mean
> and variance.**

| Step | |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **Gather the data** |
| 2 | **Calculate the returns** — $\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}} - 1$ |
| 3 | **Calculate the portfolio return** — $w_1 r_1 + w_2 r_2 + w_3 r_3$ |
| 4 | **Calculate the portfolio standard deviation** — $\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}$ |
| 5 | **VaR** $= \mu_p + Z \times \sigma_p$ |

## Summary

- The variance-covariance method is **parametric** — it **assumes returns are
  normally distributed**, which reduces the whole distribution to a **mean and a
  standard deviation.**
- **Returns, not prices**, because a \$5 move is 50% on a \$10 stock and 5% on a
  \$100 one.
- The portfolio mean is the **weighted sum of the individual returns.**
- **The portfolio standard deviation is NOT the weighted sum of the individual
  standard deviations** — that ignores correlation, and is secretly the
  $\rho = 1$ case, which **overstates risk.**
- The correct route is the **covariance matrix**: $\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$,
  with **variances on the diagonal** (since $\rho = 1$ with itself) and
  correlations off it.
- $\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}$, and then
  **VaR $= \mu_p + Z \times \sigma_p$** — one lookup instead of a sort.

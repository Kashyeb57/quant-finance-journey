The [theory note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-variance-covariance-theory)
argued that summing weighted volatilities is wrong. This sheet computes it
**both ways**, side by side — so the size of the error is measurable rather than
asserted.

## The setup

Same portfolio as the
[historical sheet](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-historical-excel):

| | JPM | MS | BAC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Weight** | 0.3 | 0.4 | 0.3 |
| **Stock value** | \$300,000 | \$400,000 | \$300,000 |

on a **\$1,000,000** investment, over **251 daily returns**.

Prices are pulled from dedicated per-stock sheets (`=JPM!F2`, `=MS!F2`,
`=BAC!F2`), returns are `=(B7/B6)-1`, and the portfolio return is again
`=SUMPRODUCT(V7:X7,$AG$7:$AI$7)`.

## Step 3 — the portfolio mean, computed twice

The sheet works it out two different ways as a check:

| Route | Formula | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Average the portfolio returns** | `=AVERAGE(AR7:AR256)` | **0.00073708** |
| **Weight the individual means** | `=SUMPRODUCT(AV32:AX32, AV33:AX33)` | **0.00073708** |

with the individual mean returns being JPM **0.00155329**, MS **0.00062937**,
BAC **0.0000645**.

They agree exactly, and they must: averaging and weighting are both linear
operations, so their order does not matter.

$$
\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \left(\sum_i w_i r_{i,t}\right) = \sum_i w_i \left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t r_{i,t}\right)
$$

## Step 4 — the two portfolio volatilities

### The wrong one, computed explicitly

| | JPM | MS | BAC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Volatility** `=STDEV.S(...)` | 0.01625597 | 0.01716490 | 0.01821889 |

```excel
BJ8 = SUMPRODUCT(BJ6:BL6, BJ7:BL7)   →  0.01720842
```

The sheet labels this **"Portfolio Volatility"**. It is the weighted sum of the
individual volatilities — the formula the theory note marked ✗.

### The right one, via the covariance matrix

The diagonal uses `VAR.P`, the off-diagonal entries are $\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$
(the sheet computes `CORREL(JPM, MS) = 0.66461117` to show where they come
from):

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.00026320 & 0.00018444 & 0.00024080 \\
0.00018444 & 0.00029346 & 0.00023374 \\
0.00024080 & 0.00023374 & 0.00033061
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and then `BJ24`, an array formula, computes $\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}$:

$$
w^T \Sigma w = 0.0002441037
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0002441037} = \mathbf{0.01562379}
$$

Recomputed independently from the matrix above, that gives **0.01562382** —
matching the sheet to seven decimals.

### What the difference is worth

| | Portfolio volatility |
| --- | --- |
| **Naive** (weighted sum) | **0.01720842** |
| **Correct** (covariance matrix) | **0.01562379** |
| **Naive overstates by** | **10.14%** |

Carried through to the VaR figures on \$1M:

| CI | VaR (correct) | VaR (naive) | Overstated by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **99%** | −\$35,609.28 | −\$39,295.69 | **\$3,686** |
| **95%** | −\$24,961.76 | −\$27,568.25 | \$2,606 |
| **90%** | −\$19,285.60 | −\$21,316.40 | \$2,031 |

**That \$3,686 is the diversification benefit.** It is the amount of risk that
does not exist because JPM, MS and BAC are correlated at about 0.66 rather than
1.0 — and it is exactly what the naive formula throws away. For a bank sizing
capital against this number, ignoring the covariance matrix means holding 10%
more capital than the portfolio warrants.

Note also that all three banks are highly correlated (0.66 between JPM and MS)
because they are the *same sector*. A genuinely diversified portfolio would show
a much larger gap between the two volatilities.

## Step 5 — the VaR calculation

```excel
CI10 = NORM.S.INV(CI9)              ' Z from alpha
CI12 = (CI6 + CI7*CI10) * CI11      ' (mean + sigma*Z) * portfolio value
```

| | 99% | 95% | 90% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | 0.00073708 | ← | ← |
| Std dev | 0.01562379 | ← | ← |
| **Alpha** `=1-CI` | 0.01 | 0.05 | 0.10 |
| **Z** `=NORM.S.INV(alpha)` | **−2.32634787** | **−1.64485363** | **−1.28155157** |
| Portfolio value | \$1M | \$1M | \$1M |
| **1-day VaR** | **−\$35,609.28** | **−\$24,961.76** | **−\$19,285.60** |

`NORM.S.INV(0.01) = −2.326` is the standard normal quantile — the point with 1%
of the distribution below it. This is the payoff of the normality assumption:
the tail is a **lookup**, not a sort.

:::note The sheet mixes sample and population estimators

The individual volatilities in `BJ7:BL7` use **`STDEV.S`** — the *sample*
standard deviation, dividing by $n-1$. The covariance matrix diagonal uses
**`VAR.P`** — the *population* variance, dividing by $n$.

| JPM volatility | Value |
| --- | --- |
| `STDEV.S` (sample) | 0.01625597 |
| `SQRT(VAR.P)` (population) | 0.01622356 |
| Ratio | **1.001998** |

That ratio is exactly $\sqrt{n/(n-1)} = \sqrt{251/250} = 1.001998$, confirming
the two estimators are the only difference.

At **0.2%** it changes nothing material here, and it only affects the *naive*
volatility (which is not used for the final answer anyway). But the two blocks
are not measuring quite the same thing, and with a shorter sample the gap would
widen — with 20 observations it would be 2.6%.

:::

## Summary

- Same JPM/MS/BAC portfolio: weights **0.3 / 0.4 / 0.3**, **\$1M**, 251 returns.
- The **portfolio mean (0.00073708) computes identically** whether you average
  the portfolio returns or weight the individual means — averaging and weighting
  commute.
- The sheet computes **both** portfolio volatilities: the **naive weighted sum
  (0.01720842)** and the **covariance-matrix answer (0.01562379)**, verified
  independently as $\sqrt{w^T\Sigma w}$.
- **The naive method overstates volatility by 10.14%** and 99% VaR by
  **\$3,686** — that gap *is* the diversification benefit, and it exists because
  the three banks correlate at ~0.66 rather than 1.
- **VaR $= (\mu_p + \sigma_p Z) \times$ portfolio value**, with $Z$ from
  `NORM.S.INV(alpha)`: **−\$35,609.28** at 99%.
- Minor: the sheet uses **`STDEV.S` in one block and `VAR.P` in another** — a
  0.2% estimator mismatch at this sample size.

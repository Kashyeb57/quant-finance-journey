## What is already there

The
[Historical Simulation sheet](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-historical-excel)
has done most of the work:

| Range | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| `Q3:Q253` | Portfolio returns, `=SUMPRODUCT(returns, weights)` |
| **`AB3:AB253`** | **The same 251 returns, sorted ascending** |
| `AF8` | The VaR rank, `=ROUNDUP(n*alpha, 0)` |
| `AF9` | The return at that rank |
| `AF11` | VaR, `=AF10*AF9` |

ES needs one more cell.

## The one formula

ES is the average of every return **worse than** the VaR return. Since `AB` is
already sorted and `AF9` already holds the VaR return:

```excel
AF12 = AVERAGEIF($AB$3:$AB$253, "<"&AF9)     ' ES return
AF13 = AF10 * AF12                            ' ES in dollars
```

`AVERAGEIF` with the criterion `"<"&AF9` averages exactly the observations
strictly below the VaR return — ranks 1 to $k-1$ — which is the
[convention the notes use](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/expected-shortfall-theory)
when they divide by 4 rather than 5.

If you prefer the convention that **includes** the VaR observation, use `"<="`
instead.

## The results

Computed on the real sorted returns. The five worst days in the sample were:

$$
-5.03\%,\quad -3.74\%,\quad -3.63\%,\quad -3.50\%,\quad -3.31\%
$$

| CI | Rank | **VaR** | **Expected Shortfall** | ES / VaR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **99%** | 3 | −\$36,253.51 | **−\$43,818.72** | **1.21×** |
| **95%** | 13 | −\$24,160.85 | **−\$32,160.37** | **1.33×** |
| **90%** | 26 | −\$19,101.28 | **−\$26,954.11** | **1.41×** |

And for reference, the same figures under the inclusive convention:

| CI | ES (excluding VaR obs) | ES (including VaR obs) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | −\$43,818.72 | −\$41,296.98 |
| 95% | −\$32,160.37 | −\$31,545.03 |
| 90% | −\$26,954.11 | −\$26,652.08 |

The two conventions differ most at **99%**, where the average is taken over only
2 observations and adding a third moves it noticeably. By 90% — averaging over
25 observations — the choice barely matters. **The smaller the tail, the more
the convention matters.**

## What this actually tells you

**At 99%, VaR says \$36,254 and ES says \$43,819.** If the bad day arrives, the
typical loss is about **21% worse** than the number the risk report quoted.
That extra \$7,565 was always there in the data — VaR simply does not report it.

Then notice the pattern in the ratio: **it grows as the confidence level
falls.**

| CI | ES / VaR | Tail averaged over |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | 1.21× | 2 observations |
| 95% | 1.33× | 12 observations |
| 90% | 1.41× | 25 observations |

At 99% the VaR boundary already sits deep in the tail, so the handful of losses
beyond it are not much worse. At 90% the boundary is much closer to the middle
of the distribution, and the average beyond it reaches all the way out to the
−5.03% day. **The lower the confidence level, the more of the extreme tail gets
pulled into the ES average** — so the gap between the two measures widens.

Which is worth knowing when reading a risk report: **a 90% VaR is a much weaker
statement about tail exposure than a 99% VaR**, and ES makes that visible where
VaR does not.

## Adding it to the other two methods

The same single formula works on the variance-covariance and Monte Carlo sheets,
pointed at their own sorted-return columns. For variance-covariance there is
also a closed form, since ES under normality has an analytic expression:

$$
ES_\alpha = \mu_p - \sigma_p \cdot \frac{\phi\left(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\right)}{\alpha}
$$

where $\phi$ is the standard normal **density** and $\Phi^{-1}$ its inverse CDF
— the same `NORM.S.INV` the sheet already uses, with `NORM.S.DIST(..., FALSE)`
supplying the density. That mirrors how variance-covariance replaced sorting
with a lookup for VaR: assume normality and the tail average becomes a formula
too.

## Summary

- **The workbook contains no ES sheet** — this builds one on the existing
  sorted returns.
- **ES is one formula**: `AVERAGEIF($AB$3:$AB$253, "<"&VaR_return)`, then
  multiply by portfolio value.
- On the real portfolio: **99% VaR −\$36,254 against ES −\$43,819** — the
  typical tail loss is **21% worse** than VaR reports.
- **ES / VaR grows as confidence falls** (1.21× at 99%, 1.41× at 90%) because a
  lower boundary pulls more of the extreme tail into the average.
- The **inclusive vs exclusive convention** matters most when the tail holds few
  observations — a visible difference at 99%, negligible at 90%.
- Under normality ES also has a **closed form**, so the variance-covariance
  sheet can compute it without sorting.

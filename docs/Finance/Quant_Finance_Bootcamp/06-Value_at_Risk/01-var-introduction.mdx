> **VaR is a risk measure that quantifies the extent of potential financial
> losses within a firm or portfolio over a specific time frame at a specific
> confidence interval.**

Or, put as the question a risk manager actually asks:

> **What is the maximum loss I might expect over a given period, at a specific
> confidence interval?**

## Reading a VaR number

**1-day VaR at 95% CI is \$1M.**

Two pieces of that sentence are the settings, and one is the answer:

| Piece | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **1 day** | the **time frame** |
| **95%** | the **confidence interval** |
| **\$1M** | the **VaR** — the number you were solving for |

And it splits into a pair of statements that are two sides of the same coin:

- There is a **95% chance** that in 1 day losses will be **at most \$1M.**
- There is a **5% chance** that losses can be **more than \$1M.**

because $100\% = 1$, so the two probabilities have to sum to one.

**A second example.** **1-day VaR at 99% CI = \$5M.**

- **99% probability** that my losses will be **less than \$5M.**
- **But 1% probability** that my losses **can exceed \$5M.**

## The thing VaR does not tell you

This is the most important line on the page, and the module is built around it.

Take the 99% example. There is a 1% chance losses exceed \$5M — but **exceed it
by how much?**

$$
\$10\text{M?} \qquad \$100\text{M?} \qquad \$1\text{B?}
$$

> **That is something that VaR doesn't tell you.**

VaR marks **where the tail begins**. It says nothing about **how far the tail
goes.** A portfolio whose worst 1% of days lose \$5.1M and one whose worst 1%
lose \$1B have **identical VaR** — and are not remotely the same portfolio.

That gap is exactly what
[Expected Shortfall](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/expected-shortfall-theory)
exists to close, at the end of this module. Everything between here and there is
about computing the VaR number itself.

## Components of VaR

Only two inputs have to be chosen:

| | Component | Typical values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Time period** | **1 day**, **10 day** |
| 2 | **Confidence interval** | **99.9%**, **99%**, **95%** |

Change either and the number changes. A VaR figure quoted without both is
meaningless.

## Applications

**a) Risk management.** **Banks and financial institutions use VaR to determine
the capital reserve required to withstand the potential loss.** This is the
regulatory use — the number feeds directly into how much capital must be held.

**b) Portfolio management.** A **portfolio manager uses the concept of VaR to
understand the extent of financial loss in the portfolio**, which **helps them
adjust their portfolio so that they can maximise their profit and minimise their
losses.**

It also makes portfolios comparable. Two portfolios — $P_1$ holding ABC and XYZ,
$P_2$ holding A and E — can be set side by side on both **return** and **loss**,
rather than on return alone.

**c) Stress testing.** VaR is **used to understand how your portfolio will
perform in stressful situations** — extreme market conditions.

Running the model over a crisis window, such as **2007-08**, gives what is
called **Stressed VaR (S-VaR)**: the same calculation, fed deliberately
catastrophic data.

## The objective, and the three routes to it

Given **1-day VaR at 95% CI**, the objective is simply: **find the VaR number.**

There are three ways to do it:

| | Method |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **Variance-Covariance method** |
| 2 | **Historical method** |
| 3 | **Monte Carlo simulation method** |

Each gets a theory note and an Excel note in what follows. And an obvious
question, worth settling now:

> **Will all the methods give you the same VaR?**
>
> **No.** Three different methods → **three different VaR numbers.**

They make different assumptions about the shape of the return distribution, so
they disagree — sometimes materially. Part of the work in this module is seeing
*how much* they disagree on the same portfolio, and understanding which
assumption caused it.

## Summary

- **VaR quantifies potential financial loss** over a **specific time frame** at
  a **specific confidence interval.**
- "1-day VaR at 95% CI = \$1M" means **95% chance losses are at most \$1M**, and
  **5% chance they exceed it.**
- **Two components**: the **time period** (1 day, 10 day) and the **confidence
  interval** (95%, 99%, 99.9%).
- **Applications**: capital reserves for banks, portfolio adjustment for
  managers, and **stress testing** (Stressed VaR over a crisis window like
  2007-08).
- **Three methods** — variance-covariance, historical, Monte Carlo — and they
  **do not agree** with each other.
- **VaR tells you where the tail starts, not how deep it goes.** That is its
  defining limitation, and the reason Expected Shortfall closes the module.

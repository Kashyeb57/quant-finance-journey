Two worked examples, both with the numbers carried all the way through. The
first uses options to **reduce** risk; the second uses them to **magnify** it.
Same instrument, opposite purposes.

## 1. Hedging — the protective put

**The situation.** An investor owns **1,000 shares** of Company A, currently
trading at **\$28**. They are concerned about a possible share price decline
over the next two months and want protection.

**The choice.** Buy **ten July put option contracts** on the company's stock
with a strike price of **\$27.50**.

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Shares held | 1,000 @ \$28 |
| Strike $K$ | \$27.50 |
| Premium | \$1 per share |
| Contract size | 100 shares |
| Contracts bought | 10 → covers **1,000 shares** |
| **Total cost** | $1 \times 100 \times 10 =$ **\$1,000** |

> The strategy costs \$1,000, but **guarantees the shares can be sold for at
> least \$27.50 per share** during the life of the option.

That sentence is the whole idea of a hedge: you have bought a **floor**.

### What it does, price by price

Two separate P/Ls, then the combination:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Option P/L} \;&=\; \max(27.5 - S,\, 0) \times 1000 \;-\; 1000\\[2pt]
\text{Stock P/L} \;&=\; (S - 28) \times 1000
\end{aligned}
$$

| Stock price $S$ | Option P/L (\$) | Stock P/L (\$) | **Combined (\$)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | +6,500 | −8,000 | **−1,500** |
| 21 | +5,500 | −7,000 | **−1,500** |
| 22 | +4,500 | −6,000 | **−1,500** |
| 23 | +3,500 | −5,000 | **−1,500** |
| 24 | +2,500 | −4,000 | **−1,500** |
| 25 | +1,500 | −3,000 | **−1,500** |
| 26 | +500 | −2,000 | **−1,500** |
| 27 | −500 | −1,000 | **−1,500** |
| 28 | −1,000 | 0 | **−1,000** |
| 29 | −1,000 | +1,000 | **0** |
| 30 | −1,000 | +2,000 | **+1,000** |
| 32 | −1,000 | +4,000 | **+3,000** |
| 34 | −1,000 | +6,000 | **+5,000** |

Read the last column. **Below \$27.50 the combined result is frozen at
−\$1,500**, no matter how far the stock falls. At \$20 the unhedged shareholder
has lost \$8,000; the hedged one has lost \$1,500. Above the strike the put
expires worthless and the investor keeps the upside, minus the \$1,000 premium.

Where does −\$1,500 come from? The floor is \$27.50 per share, so the shares are
worth at least 27.50 × 1000 = **\$27,500**; subtract the \$1,000 premium and
the position is worth \$26,500 against the \$28,000 it started at.

$$
\text{maximum loss} \;=\; 28{,}000 - (27.50 \times 1000) + 1{,}000 \;=\; \$1{,}500
$$

**The trade-off:** the investor gave up \$1,000 of certain money to convert an
open-ended downside into a known, capped one. On the option leg alone, breakeven
is at $S = 26.50$ (where the \$1 premium is exactly recovered).

## 2. Speculation — 100 shares versus 20 call contracts

**The situation.** A speculator believes a stock will **increase** in value over
the next two months. The stock currently trades at **\$20**. A two-month call
option with a **\$22.50** strike is selling for **\$1**. The speculator is
willing to invest **\$2,000**.

Two ways to spend the same \$2,000:

| Alternative | What it buys |
| --- | --- |
| **A** | 100 shares at \$20 = \$2,000 |
| **B** | 2,000 call options (**20 contracts**) at \$1 = \$2,000 |

### The two outcomes

| December stock price | **A: 100 shares** | **B: 20 call contracts** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **\$15** (down) | −\$500 | **−\$2,000** |
| **\$27** (up) | +\$700 | **+\$7,000** |

The same \$2,000, the same view, and wildly different consequences. On the way
up the option returns **ten times** what the shares do. On the way down it loses
**everything**, while the shareholder is down only a quarter of their stake.

### Across the range

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Option P/L} \;&=\; \max(S - 22.5,\, 0) \times 2000 \;-\; 2000\\[2pt]
\text{Stock P/L} \;&=\; (S - 20) \times 100
\end{aligned}
$$

| $S$ | Option P/L (\$) | Stock P/L (\$) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15.5 | −2,000 | −450 |
| 16.5 | −2,000 | −350 |
| 17.5 | −2,000 | −250 |
| 18.5 | −2,000 | −150 |
| 19.5 | −2,000 | −50 |
| 20.5 | −2,000 | +50 |
| 21.5 | −2,000 | +150 |
| 22.5 | −2,000 | +250 |
| **23.5** | **0** | +350 |
| 24.5 | +2,000 | +450 |
| 25.5 | +4,000 | +550 |
| 26.5 | +6,000 | +650 |
| 27.5 | +8,000 | +750 |

Note the shape. The option line is **flat at −\$2,000** for every price below the
\$22.50 strike — the speculator is wiped out identically whether the stock ends
at 15.5 or 22.5. Then it turns and climbs at 2,000 per dollar, twenty times the
slope of the share position. Breakeven is at

$$
S = K + \text{premium} = 22.50 + 1 = \$23.50
$$

which is where the option column crosses zero.

### The lesson

> **Leverage the position with options.**

The option gives exposure to **2,000 shares** for the price of 100. That is the
leverage: a large exposure for a small outlay. It amplifies the return — and
amplifies the loss in exactly the same proportion. The shareholder needs the
stock to fall to zero to lose everything; the option holder only needs it to
finish a cent below \$22.50.

## Summary

- The **same instrument** hedges or speculates depending on how it is used
  against the position you already hold.
- A **protective put** buys a floor: the 1,000-share holder converted an
  \$8,000 loss at \$20 into a capped **\$1,500**, at a cost of \$1,000, while
  keeping the upside.
- A **long call** used for speculation gives leverage: \$2,000 controlled 2,000
  shares instead of 100, turning a \$700 gain into \$7,000 — and a \$500 loss
  into a total \$2,000 loss.
- Breakeven in both cases is the strike adjusted by the premium: $K - P$ for the
  put leg, $K + P$ for the call.

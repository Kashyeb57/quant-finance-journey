## What is a derivative?

The intuition first. Think about **orange juice** and **oranges**.

- If oranges are very expensive, orange juice is expensive.
- If oranges are cheap, orange juice is cheap.

Orange juice is *deriving its value from oranges*. It has no independent value —
it moves because the thing it is made from moves.

| Term | In the analogy |
| --- | --- |
| **Derivative** | Orange juice |
| **Underlying asset** | Oranges |

> **Definition.** A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is derived
> from the value of an underlying asset.

### What can be the underlying asset?

- Stocks
- Bonds
- Commodities
- Currencies
- Interest rates
- Market indices

## Where derivatives trade: Exchange vs OTC

There are exactly two venues:

- **Exchange**
- **Over the Counter (OTC)**

### Exchange

> An exchange is a marketplace or platform where various financial instruments
> are traded.

Examples: **BSE**, **NYSE**, **ICE** (Intercontinental Exchange, USA). What
trades there: stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities and derivatives.

**Key features of an exchange**

**a) Standardization.** Contracts are standardized in terms of size, expiration
date and other specifications. If the exchange defines the contract as *100 kg
of oranges*, everyone trades that same unit — only the price varies (102.5,
107.8, …).

**b) Regulation.** Exchange markets are heavily regulated, which gives greater
transparency between market participants.

**c) Clearing house.** A clearing house acts as an intermediary between buyer
and seller, guaranteeing that both parties honour the contract. Because it
stands in the middle, there is far **less default risk**.

**d) Liquidity.** Highly liquid instruments — contracts trade in large volume.

### Over the Counter (OTC)

> The OTC market is a decentralized marketplace where financial instruments are
> traded directly between two parties — a buyer and a seller.

**Key features of OTC**

**a) Customization.** Contracts can be customized to the specific needs of the
two parties, including size, maturity and underlying asset. Where an exchange
sells you a fixed 1,000 kg lot at \$1/kg, OTC lets you shape the deal.

**b) Regulation.** These are *less* regulated, which can lead to a lack of
transparency.

**c) Clearing house.** Historically there was **no** clearing house in OTC — the
two parties faced each other directly, so if one defaulted the other simply lost.

**d) Liquidity.** Because contracts are customized in nature, they are
**illiquid** — a bespoke contract is hard to hand on to someone else.

### What changed after 2007–08

The financial crisis changed the structure of OTC markets. Traditionally OTC was
a web of **bilateral agreements**: every participant faced every other
participant directly, and one failure propagated through the web.

Afterwards (in the USA first), a **CCP — Central Counter Party** was introduced
in the middle of OTC trades as well:

```
Before:   A ←──────────────→ B        (bilateral; if B defaults, A wears it)

After:    A ────→ [ CCP ] ←──── B     (both face the CCP, not each other)
```

With a CCP in between, **both parties are obligated to the contract** and the
chance of the other party defaulting is very low.

*(Hull sets this out in Figure 2.2 — the traditional web of bilateral OTC
agreements versus a single central counterparty acting as clearing house.)*

Swaps are the classic OTC example — **IRS** (interest-rate swaps) and
**CS** (currency swaps), traded between parties such as commercial banks.

## Future contracts

Futures trade **on an exchange**.

> A future contract is a **standardized** financial derivative that **obligates**
> the buyer to purchase, and the seller to sell, the asset at a **predetermined
> future date** and a **predetermined price**.

Note the three words that carry all the weight: *standardized*, *obligates*,
*predetermined*.

### Worked example — 100 barrels of oil

An investor enters a future contract to buy **100 barrels of oil at \$70 per
barrel**, expiring **3 months** from now.

- **A** buys 100 barrels of oil at \$70 (from B)
- **B** sells 100 barrels of oil at \$70

At $t = 0$ the agreed price is \$70. At $t = 3$ months, whatever the market
does, the deal happens at \$70:

| Market price at $t=3$ | Contract price | Outcome for the buyer (A) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \$65 | \$70 | **Loss** — paying 70 for something worth 65 |
| \$70 | \$70 | No profit, no loss |
| \$75 | \$70 | **Profit** — paying 70 for something worth 75 |

Whatever A loses, B gains, and vice versa. A future is a zero-sum contract.

### Worked example — the farmer

The same idea from the seller's side. Participants can be **individuals**
(a farmer) or **financial institutions**.

A farmer agrees to sell **1,000 kg of corn at \$2/kg** to Company A, three
months from now.

| Market price at $t=3$ | Locked price | Result for the farmer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \$1 | \$2 | Profit — sells at 2 what is worth 1 |
| \$2 | \$2 | No profit, no loss |
| \$5 | \$2 | Loss — could have sold at 5 |

### Characteristics of a future contract

**a) Standardized agreement** — fixed in terms of size, expiration date,
underlying asset, quantity and quality.

**b) Clearing house** acts as an intermediary between buyer and seller, making
sure the contract is honoured.

**c) Marked to market daily.** Gains and losses that occur in the contract are
settled **every day**, not just at expiry. If you hold 100 shares' worth at \$90
and the price moves to \$91, that difference is settled that day.

**d) Highly liquid** instrument.

**e) CCP in between** → **less counterparty risk**: the chance of the other
party defaulting is very small.

### Why mark-to-market matters

Marking to market is what stops a large loss from accumulating silently until
expiry. Consider a position that drifts against you over three months: on a
\$100 contract × 1,000 units, a \$10 adverse move is

$$
\$10 \times 1{,}000 = \$10{,}000
$$

If none of that is settled until the end, the losing party may simply be unable
to pay — and a default happens. Settling daily keeps the exposure small.

This is enforced through margin:

- **Initial margin** — money posted up front to open the position
- **Margin call** — a demand for more money when the daily settlement erodes it

## Payoff of a future contract

Two directions:

| You | Position |
| --- | --- |
| **Buy** the contract | **Long** position |
| **Sell** the contract | **Short** position |

$$
\text{Long payoff} = S_T - K
\qquad\qquad
\text{Short payoff} = K - S_T
$$

where

- $S_T$ — the price of the underlying **at maturity**
- $K$ — the **strike** (the predetermined price)

The two are exact mirror images: $K - S_T = -(S_T - K)$. One side's gain is the
other side's loss.

### Worked payoff table

A agrees to buy stock XYZ from B at **\$100**, six months from now. So $K = 100$.

| $S_T$ | $K$ | Long payoff $S_T - K$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 95 | 100 | −5 |
| 96 | 100 | −4 |
| 97 | 100 | −3 |
| 98 | 100 | −2 |
| 99 | 100 | −1 |
| **100** | 100 | **0** |
| 101 | 100 | +1 |
| 102 | 100 | +2 |
| 103 | 100 | +3 |
| 104 | 100 | +4 |
| 105 | 100 | +5 |

For the **long** position the upside is unlimited (the price can keep rising)
while the downside is limited — the price cannot fall below zero, so the worst
case is $-K$. For the **short** position it is exactly reversed.

## Forward contracts

> A forward contract is a **customized** agreement between two parties to buy or
> sell an asset at a **predetermined price** on a **predetermined date**.

Two things follow immediately:

- Forward contracts are traded **OTC only**.
- A forward is also an **obligation** — like a future, both sides must perform.

### What "customized" actually means

Everything is negotiable between the two parties:

- **Quality** can be changed
- **Quantity** can be changed
- **Price** can be negotiated

### Key characteristics of a forward contract

**a) Customizable agreement** between two parties.

**b) Traded OTC only.**

**c) Less liquid** — a bespoke contract is hard to pass on.

**d) Higher counterparty risk.**

**e) No margin requirement**, because there is no CCP in between. (On an
exchange, the CCP demands collateral/cash; in a plain forward there is nobody to
demand it.)

**f) Priced only at maturity** — there is no daily settlement.

---

## Futures vs Forwards

| | **Future** | **Forward** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agreement | Standardized | Customized |
| Venue | Exchange | OTC |
| Counterparty | CCP in between | No CCP (sometimes there can be one) |
| Liquidity | Highly liquid | Less liquid |
| Settlement | **Marked to market daily** | **Priced at maturity** |
| Margin | Required (initial margin, margin calls) | None |

Because the two instruments price differently, the same exposure can quote
differently in each market — e.g. \$1 at **Rs 84** in the futures market versus
**Rs 85** on a forward.

### Payoff (forward contract)

Identical in form to the future:

$$
\text{Long} = S_T - K
\qquad\qquad
\text{Short} = K - S_T
$$

The difference between forwards and futures is *not* the payoff — it is the
venue, the standardization, the counterparty and when the cash actually moves.

---

## Summary

- A derivative takes its value from an **underlying asset**.
- It trades either on an **exchange** (standardized, regulated, clearing house,
  liquid) or **OTC** (customized, lightly regulated, historically no clearing
  house, illiquid).
- After 2007–08 a **CCP** was introduced into OTC markets, cutting default risk.
- A **future** is a standardized, exchange-traded obligation, marked to market
  daily with a CCP in between.
- A **forward** is a customized, OTC obligation, priced only at maturity, with
  higher counterparty risk and no margin.
- Long payoff is $S_T - K$; short payoff is $K - S_T$. They are mirror images.

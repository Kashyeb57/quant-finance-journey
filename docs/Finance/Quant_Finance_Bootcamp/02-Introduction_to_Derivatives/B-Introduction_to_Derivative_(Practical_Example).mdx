## Recap: forwards and futures

| **Forward** | **Future** |
| --- | --- |
| Customized agreement between 2 parties | Standardized agreement between 2 parties |
| Buy or sell the contract at a predetermined **date** and predetermined **price** ($K$, the strike) | Buy or sell the underlying asset at a predetermined date and price |
| **OTC** | **Exchange** |

$$
\text{Long position} = S_T - K
\qquad
\text{Short position} = K - S_T = -(S_T - K)
$$

Long (**buy**) and short (**sell**) are exact opposites — see the
[forwards and futures note](/docs/Forwards-and-Futures) for the payoff diagrams.

## Why do we use derivatives?

Four reasons:

1. **Risk management** (hedging)
2. **Speculation**
3. **Arbitrage**
4. **Leverage**

---

### 1. Hedging — managing or mitigating risk

The farmer again. He will have **1,000 kg of corn** to sell in three months.

- **His risk:** the price could *decline* by the time he harvests.
- **His action:** sell a future contract now — 1,000 kg of corn at a strike of
  **\$2 per kg**, three months from now.

At $t = 3$ the market price is **\$1/kg**. But he is contractually selling at
**\$2/kg**. He has protected himself against exactly the fall he was worried
about. The uncertainty is gone.

That is what hedging is: giving up the upside in order to remove the downside.

Note the structural point — with a **forward or future, both parties are
obligated**. A buys at $F_0$, B sells at $F_0$, and neither can walk away. (An
**option** is the instrument where the buyer is *not* obligated — that is the
whole difference, and it is the subject of the second half of this note.)

### 2. Speculation

> Investors and traders use derivatives to speculate on the future price
> movement of the asset. This allows for **potential profit if the price moves
> in their favour**, though it also carries a **high level of risk**.

You are betting on a certain position. If XYZ moves from \$50 to \$100 and you
had bought exposure for \$25, the gain is large relative to what you put up —
and the loss would have been just as sharp in the other direction.

### The three types of trader

| Trader | What they are doing |
| --- | --- |
| **Hedgers** | Risk management |
| **Speculators** | Betting on a certain position |
| **Arbitrageurs** | Finding price discrepancies |

### 3. Arbitrage

> Price discrepancies between the underlying asset and the derivative — or for
> the same asset across venues.

Stock XYZ quotes at:

- **NYSE:** \$100 → **buy** here
- **ICE:** \$105 → **sell** here

That is a **\$5 arbitrage opportunity**, locked in with no view on direction.
The same thing happens across currencies and borders — XYZ at **\$100** on NYSE
against **£84** on the LSE.

An arbitrageur's job is to find those discrepancies. In an efficient market they
close quickly, which is precisely *because* arbitrageurs act on them.

### 4. Leverage

> Derivatives allow an investor to gain a **large exposure** to an asset with a
> **relatively small initial investment** — known as leverage. This can amplify
> potential returns, but can increase the risk of losses.

With \$2,000 to deploy:

- Buying the **stock** directly might get you \$1,500 of exposure.
- Buying a **derivative (option)** on it could get you \$7,000 of exposure.

The same move in the underlying is now worth several times more to you — in both
directions. Leverage is not free money; it is a magnifier.

## Worked hedging example — currency risk

**Question.** A US-based company expects to receive **€1 million in 3 months**.

**Risk:** the Euro might weaken against the Dollar. If it does, that €1M converts
into fewer dollars than it would today. The company's revenue is exposed to
something that has nothing to do with its business.

**Action:** enter a **forward contract** to lock the exchange rate. That
**eliminates the uncertainty of the exchange-rate movement**.

### The same idea in numbers

Today, **1 USD → Rs 84**, and you hold **1,000 USD**. Your worry is that the
Rupee strengthens (so each dollar buys fewer rupees), say to **1 USD → Rs 80**.

| Rate | USD | Rupees received |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 84 | 1,000 | 84 × 1,000 = **84,000** |
| 80 | 1,000 | 80 × 1,000 = **80,000** |

A Rs 4,000 swing purely from the exchange rate. Now lock it with a
future/forward at **1 USD = Rs 83.5**:

$$
83.5 \times 1{,}000 = \text{Rs } 83{,}500
$$

You have given up the gain if the rate moves to Rs 85, in exchange for
certainty. That is the trade every hedger makes.

## Options

> An option is a type of financial derivative which gives the holder the
> **right, but not the obligation**, to buy or sell the underlying asset at a
> **predetermined price** on a **predetermined date**.

That single phrase — *right but not the obligation* — is the entire difference
from a forward or a future.

### Why that matters

You hold an option contract expiring at $t = 3$. At expiry you look at the
market:

- The deal is **profitable** → you **exercise** it and buy.
- The deal is a **loss** → you simply **do not execute the deal**. You walk away.

With a forward or a future there is **no concept of not executing**. You are
obligated; you execute the contract. With an option you have a **choice**.

| | Forward / Future | Option |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At expiry | **Obligation** — must execute | **Choice** — exercise or not |

### Key characteristics of an option contract

**a) Underlying asset** — stocks, commodities, currencies, bonds, etc.

**b) Strike price** — the price at which the holder can buy or sell the
underlying asset. This is the predetermined price, $K$.

**c) Premium** — the price **paid by the buyer (long position) to the seller
(short position)**. This is what you pay for the privilege of having a choice.
It flows one way, at the start:

```
   Buyer of option  ──── premium ────→  Seller of option
     (long)                                (short)
```

**d) Expiration date** — the date at which the option must be exercised;
otherwise it **expires worthless**.

**e) Exercise style** — see below.

### Exercise style

| Style | When it can be exercised |
| --- | --- |
| **American option** | At **any point** in time up to expiry |
| **European option** | **Only at maturity** |
| **Asian option** | (Path-dependent — payoff depends on an average price) |

So for a 3-month option: an American option can be exercised at $t=1$, $t=1.5$,
or anywhere along the way if it is profitable; a European option can only be
exercised at $t=3$.

## Types of option

There are two: the **call** and the **put**.

### Call option

> A call option gives the holder the **right to buy** the underlying asset at
> the strike price $K$ on a predetermined date.

Since you would only want the right to *buy* at a fixed price if you expect the
price to rise, a call is a view that **the price of the asset will go up**.

| Position | What you did | What you hold | Expectation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Long call** | *Bought* the right to buy | The **right** to buy the underlying | Price will **go up** |
| **Short call** | *Sold* the right to buy | An **obligation** to sell if exercised | Price will **decline** |

### Put option

> A put option gives the holder the **right to sell** the underlying asset.

| Position | What you did | What you hold | Expectation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Long put** | *Bought* the right to sell | The **right** to sell the underlying | Price will **go down** |
| **Short put** | *Sold* the right to sell | An **obligation** to buy if exercised | Price will hold up |

### The general rule

$$
\textbf{Call} \rightarrow \text{right to \textbf{buy}}
\qquad
\textbf{Put} \rightarrow \text{right to \textbf{sell}}
$$

and, cutting across both:

- **Long position** → you hold the **right** to buy/sell the underlying asset.
- **Short position** → you are **obligated** to buy/sell if the holder exercises.

### The four positions

Two option types × two directions = **four** positions. This table is the one
worth memorising:

| # | Position | You have |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Long call** | The **right to buy** |
| 2 | **Short call** | The **obligation to sell** |
| 3 | **Long put** | The **right to sell** |
| 4 | **Short put** | The **obligation to buy** |

In every case the **premium flows from the long to the short**. The short side
is being paid to take on an obligation; the long side is paying for a choice.

## Summary

- Derivatives are used for **hedging**, **speculation**, **arbitrage** and
  **leverage**.
- Hedging removes uncertainty by locking a price — the farmer locking \$2/kg,
  the exporter locking an FX rate — at the cost of the upside.
- The three trader types are **hedgers**, **speculators** and **arbitrageurs**.
- An **option** gives the holder a **right, not an obligation** — the one thing
  a forward or future never does.
- An option is defined by five things: **underlying asset, strike price,
  premium, expiration date, exercise style**.
- **American** = exercise any time; **European** = exercise only at maturity.
- **Call** = right to buy; **Put** = right to sell. **Long** = holds the right;
  **Short** = carries the obligation, and receives the premium.

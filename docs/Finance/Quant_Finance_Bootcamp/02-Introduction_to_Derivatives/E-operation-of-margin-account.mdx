A futures contract is **marked to market daily**: gains and losses are settled
every single day rather than accumulating quietly until expiry. That is what
stops a losing party from walking away at maturity owing more than they can
pay — but it only works if there is money in the account to settle *against*.

That money is the **margin**.

## The four terms

**a) Margin account** — a type of account that traders must maintain with their
broker in order to engage in trading.

**b) Initial margin (IM)** — the minimum amount of capital a trader must
deposit in the margin account **to open** a futures position.

- It is usually a **small percentage of the contract's total value**, typically
  ranging from about **2% to 12%**.
- Where in that range depends on the **volatility of the asset** — a more
  volatile underlying demands more margin.
- It acts as **collateral**, ensuring the trader can cover potential losses.

**c) Maintenance margin (MM)** — a **threshold level** the margin account must
maintain *after* the trade has been initiated. It sits below the initial margin.

**d) Margin call** — if the account balance **falls below the maintenance
margin**, the broker issues a margin call, and the trader must deposit
additional funds to bring the account **back up to the initial margin level**.

**Variation margin** is the name for that top-up: the amount of money required
to restore the account to the initial margin level.

Note the asymmetry that trips people up: the call is *triggered* by falling
below **MM**, but the amount you must pay restores you all the way to **IM** —
not merely back to MM.

### A minimal example

Initial margin \$12,000, maintenance margin \$9,000:

| | Balance | Below MM? | Margin call |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day 1 | \$11,000 | No | — |
| Day 2 | \$10,000 | No | — |
| Day 3 | **\$8,000** | **Yes** | Deposit **\$4,000** to restore \$12,000 |

That \$4,000 is the variation margin.

## Worked example — two gold futures contracts

This is the full mechanic, day by day.

A trader contacts their broker to **buy two December gold futures contracts**.
The current futures price is **\$1,250 per ounce**, and the contract size is
**100 ounces**, so the trader has contracted to buy **200 ounces** at that price.

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Position | Long 2 contracts = **200 oz** |
| Trade price | **\$1,250 / oz** |
| Initial margin | \$6,000 per contract = **\$12,000** |
| Maintenance margin | **\$9,000** |

Each day the position is marked to market. Because the trader is **long** 200
ounces, the daily gain is

$$
\text{daily gain} = (\text{today's settlement} - \text{yesterday's settlement}) \times 200
$$

| Day | Settlement (\$/oz) | Daily gain (\$) | Cumulative (\$) | Balance (\$) | Margin call |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| — | 1,250.00 | — | — | 12,000 | |
| 1 | 1,241.00 | −1,800 | −1,800 | 10,200 | |
| 2 | 1,238.30 | −540 | −2,340 | 9,660 | |
| 3 | 1,244.60 | +1,260 | −1,080 | 10,920 | |
| 4 | 1,241.30 | −660 | −1,740 | 10,260 | |
| 5 | 1,240.10 | −240 | −1,980 | 10,020 | |
| 6 | 1,236.20 | −780 | −2,760 | 9,240 | |
| **7** | 1,229.90 | −1,260 | −4,020 | **7,980** | **← below \$9,000: call for \$4,020** |
| 8 | 1,230.80 | +180 | −3,840 | 12,180 | |
| 9 | 1,225.40 | −1,080 | −4,920 | 11,100 | |
| 10 | 1,228.10 | +540 | −4,380 | 11,640 | |
| **11** | 1,211.00 | −3,420 | −7,800 | **8,220** | **← below \$9,000: call for \$3,780** |
| 12 | 1,211.00 | 0 | −7,800 | 12,000 | |
| 13 | 1,214.30 | +660 | −7,140 | 12,660 | |
| 14 | 1,216.10 | +360 | −6,780 | 13,020 | |
| 15 | 1,223.00 | +1,380 | −5,400 | 14,400 | |
| 16 | 1,226.90 | +780 | −4,620 | 15,180 | |

Reading it:

- **Day 6** ends at \$9,240. Uncomfortable, but still above the \$9,000
  maintenance margin — **no call**.
- **Day 7** the price drops to 1,229.90, a \$1,260 loss, taking the balance to
  **\$7,980**. That is below the maintenance margin, so the broker calls for
  **\$4,020** — the amount that restores the account to the \$12,000 initial
  margin, not to the \$9,000 threshold.
- **Day 11** is the big one: gold falls 17.10 in a day, which on 200 ounces is a
  **\$3,420** loss. Balance \$8,220 → a second call for **\$3,780**.
- The position then recovers. By day 16 the balance is \$15,180.

Sanity check on the whole thing: the trader deposited
\$12,000 + \$4,020 + \$3,780 = **\$19,800** and finished with \$15,180 — a net
loss of **\$4,620**, which is exactly the cumulative column, and exactly
(1,226.90 − 1,250.00) × 200. The margin account never changes the
economics; it only guarantees the money is there to settle them.

## Position limits and exercise limits

Two related restrictions, both **established by regulatory bodies** (and so
differing by country).

### Position limit

> The **maximum number of option contracts** an investor or entity can hold
> **on the same side of the market**.

"Same side" means positions expressing the same directional view:

| Side | Positions |
| --- | --- |
| **Bullish** | long call **and** short put |
| **Bearish** | short call **and** long put |

So a long call and a short put count *together* against the limit — you cannot
evade it by splitting a bullish bet across two instruments.

With a limit of, say, **250,000 contracts**, you cannot hold more than 250,000
option contracts on one side. Since one option contract is **100 shares**, that
is

$$
250{,}000 \times 100 = 25{,}000{,}000 \text{ shares}
$$

**Why the limit exists:** to stop large entities from controlling too much of
the market in a particular stock's options, and so to **prevent market
manipulation or liquidity problems**.

### Exercise limit

> Restricts the **number of option contracts** an investor or entity can
> **exercise** over a specific period — typically **five consecutive business
> days**.

The position limit caps what you can *hold*; the exercise limit caps what you
can *act on* in a window. Both close off the same abuse from different sides.

## Summary

- Futures are marked to market daily, and the **margin account** is what makes
  that settlement possible.
- **Initial margin** opens the position (~2–12% of contract value, driven by
  volatility). **Maintenance margin** is the lower threshold you must stay above.
- Fall below the MM and you get a **margin call** — and the **variation margin**
  you must post restores the account to the **initial** margin, not the
  threshold.
- On the gold example, two calls (\$4,020 and \$3,780) kept a position alive
  whose total loss was \$4,620.
- **Position limits** cap contracts held on the same side of the market;
  **exercise limits** cap contracts exercised over ~5 business days. Both exist
  to prevent manipulation and liquidity problems.

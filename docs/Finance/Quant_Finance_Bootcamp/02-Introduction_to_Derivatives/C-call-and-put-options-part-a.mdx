A forward and a future are an **obligation**. An option is a **right, but not
the obligation**. Everything in this note follows from that one difference.

## The difference, in one example

You hold an option contract to **buy XYZ stock at \$100**, three months from
now. At $t = 3$ the market can be anywhere. Two cases:

**Case 1 — the stock rises to \$110.**
Your contract lets you buy at \$100 while the market charges \$110. You
**exercise**: buy at 100, and you are \$10 better off.

**Case 2 — the stock falls to \$90.**
Your contract says \$100, the market says \$90. Exercising would mean paying
\$100 for something worth \$90 — a \$10 loss. So you **do not execute the
deal**. You walk away.

With a forward or a future, Case 2 is not a choice: you are obligated, and you
take the \$10 loss. The option gives you the **flexibility to buy or not**.

## Buyer and seller are not symmetric

| | **Buyer (holder)** of the option | **Seller (writer / underwriter)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Has | The **right** to buy or sell | — |
| Obliged? | **Not** obligated | **Always obligated** to buy or sell the underlying |

The buyer picks whichever outcome suits them; the seller has to live with that
choice. Which raises the obvious question.

### Why does the buyer pay a premium?

Because the seller is at a structural disadvantage. They carry the obligation
and have no say in whether the contract is exercised. The **premium** is what
compensates them for taking on that obligation. It flows once, at the start,
from buyer to seller:

```
   Buyer (long)  ──── premium ────→  Seller (short)
   holds the right                   carries the obligation
```

## The two types

### Call option

> A call option gives the holder the **right to buy** the underlying asset at
> the strike price $K$, on a predetermined date.

You would only want a fixed price to *buy* at if you think the price is going
**up**.

| Position | What you did | Expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Long call** | Bought the right to buy | Price will **rise** → **bullish** |
| **Short call** | Sold the right to buy | Price will **decline** → **bearish** |

### Put option

> A put option gives the holder the **right to sell** the underlying asset at
> $K$, on a predetermined date.

| Position | What you did | Expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Long put** | Bought the right to sell | Price will **fall** → **bearish** |
| **Short put** | Sold the right to sell | Price will **rise** → **bullish** |

A memory aid: a **bull** attacks by throwing its horns **up**; a **bear** swipes
**down**.

Note the pairing is not "call = bullish". It is the *combination*: long call and
short put are bullish; short call and long put are bearish.

## The five characteristics of an option

1. **Underlying asset** — stocks, commodities, currencies, bonds.
2. **Strike price** $K$ — the price the holder can buy or sell at.
3. **Expiration date** — after which the option expires worthless.
4. **Exercise style** — **American** (exercisable at *any* point) or
   **European** (exercisable *only at maturity*). These names are conventions,
   **not geographical locations** — a European-style option is not a European
   product.
5. **Premium** — the price paid by the holder to the seller.

## Payoff and profit are not the same thing

This distinction matters and is easy to blur:

- **Payoff** is the value of the option at expiry, **ignoring the premium**.
- **Profit** is the payoff **minus the premium you paid** (or plus the premium
  you received).

**Payoff** — premium removed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Long call} \;&=\; \max(S_T - K,\, 0) &\qquad
\text{Short call} \;&=\; -\max(S_T - K,\, 0)\\[2pt]
\text{Long put} \;&=\; \max(K - S_T,\, 0) &\qquad
\text{Short put} \;&=\; -\max(K - S_T,\, 0)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Profit** — what you actually walk away with:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Long call} \;&=\; \max(S_T - K,\, 0) - \text{premium}\\[2pt]
\text{Short call} \;&=\; \text{premium} - \max(S_T - K,\, 0)\\[2pt]
\text{Long put} \;&=\; \max(K - S_T,\, 0) - \text{premium}\\[2pt]
\text{Short put} \;&=\; \text{premium} - \max(K - S_T,\, 0)
\end{aligned}
$$

The `max(·, 0)` is the option's whole character: you never exercise into a loss,
so the payoff can never go below zero. Only the *profit* can.

Everything below is **profit** — the version with the premium in it, since that
is what determines whether the trade made money.

The short is always the negative of the long — the seller's payoff is
$-(\text{long payoff})$. Derivatives are a **zero-sum game**: what one side
gains, the other loses, exactly.

Note also who pays and who receives:

- **Long call and long put → pay** the premium.
- **Short call and short put → receive** the premium.

The short put can also be written $\text{premium} + \min(S_T - K,\, 0)$, which
is the same thing.

### Long call, worked out

Take $K = 95$ and a premium of $1$.

| $S_T$ | $\max(S_T-K,0)$ | − premium | **Payoff** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 90 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 91 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 92 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 93 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 94 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 95 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| **96** | 1 | −1 | **0** ← breakeven |
| 97 | 2 | −1 | **+1** |
| 98 | 3 | −1 | **+2** |
| 99 | 4 | −1 | **+3** |
| 100 | 5 | −1 | **+4** |

Below the strike the option is never exercised, so the loss is flat at the
premium. Above the strike each dollar of stock is a dollar of payoff. Breakeven
is at

$$
S_T = K + \text{premium} = 95 + 1 = 96
$$

### Short call, worked out

The mirror image, same $K = 95$, premium $1$:

| $S_T$ | **Payoff** $= 1 - \max(S_T-95,0)$ |
| --- | --- |
| 90–95 | **+1** (keeps the whole premium) |
| **96** | **0** |
| 97 | **−1** |
| 98 | **−2** |
| 99 | **−3** |
| 100 | **−4** |

### Long put, worked out

Same $K = 95$, premium $1$ — now the payoff runs the other way:

| $S_T$ | $\max(K-S_T,0)$ | − premium | **Payoff** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 90 | 5 | −1 | **+4** |
| 91 | 4 | −1 | **+3** |
| 92 | 3 | −1 | **+2** |
| 93 | 2 | −1 | **+1** |
| **94** | 1 | −1 | **0** ← breakeven |
| 95 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 96 | 0 | −1 | **−1** |
| 97+ | 0 | −1 | **−1** |

Breakeven for a put is $K - \text{premium} = 94$.

Worked from the contract's side: you bought the right to sell XYZ at \$100. If
the market goes to \$110 you do **not** execute — why sell at 100 what fetches
110? If it drops to \$90 you **do** execute, gaining $K - S_T = 100 - 90 = \$10$.

The asymmetry is the point:

| Position | Loss | Profit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Long call** | Limited (the premium) | **Unlimited** — the price can keep rising |
| **Short call** | **Unlimited** | Limited (the premium) |
| **Long put** | Limited (the premium) | Limited — the price can only fall to zero |
| **Short put** | Limited | Limited (the premium) |

Selling a naked call is the one position here with genuinely unbounded loss.

## Summary

- An option is a **right, not an obligation** — the holder chooses, the writer
  must comply.
- The **premium** exists to compensate the writer for carrying that obligation.
- **Call** = right to buy, **put** = right to sell; **long** = holds the right,
  **short** = carries the obligation and receives the premium.
- Bullish: long call, short put. Bearish: short call, long put.
- All four payoffs are built from `max(·, 0)`, and long and short are exact
  negatives — a zero-sum game.
- Breakeven is $K + \text{premium}$ for a call and $K - \text{premium}$ for a put.

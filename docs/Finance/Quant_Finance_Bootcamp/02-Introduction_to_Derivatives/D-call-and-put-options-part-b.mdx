[Part A](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Introduction_to_Derivatives/C-call-and-put-options-part-a)
covered what calls and puts are and what they pay. This note covers the two
things that decide *whether you would exercise at all* — moneyness — and the
corporate actions that quietly rewrite the contract underneath you.

## Moneyness

> The moneyness of an option refers to the **relationship between the strike
> price of the option and the current price of the underlying asset**.

So it is a statement about the pair $(S_T,\, K)$, and there are only three
possible relationships:

$$
S_T > K \qquad S_T = K \qquad S_T < K
$$

Three names follow:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **In the money (ITM)** | Money coming *in* — exercising is profitable |
| **At the money (ATM)** | No profit, no loss |
| **Out of the money (OTM)** | Money going *out* — exercising would lose |

### Which is which depends on the option

For a **call** (right to buy), you want the market *above* your strike:

| | Call |
| --- | --- |
| **ITM** | $S_T > K$ → profit |
| **ATM** | $S_T = K$ → zero |
| **OTM** | $S_T < K$ → loss |

For a **put** (right to sell), it is exactly **reversed** — you want the market
*below* your strike:

| | Put |
| --- | --- |
| **ITM** | $S_T < K$ |
| **ATM** | $S_T = K$ |
| **OTM** | $S_T > K$ |

Side by side:

| | **Call** | **Put** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ITM** | $S_T > K$ | $S_T < K$ |
| **ATM** | $S_T = K$ | $S_T = K$ |
| **OTM** | $S_T < K$ | $S_T > K$ |

### One thing to be careful about

**Moneyness does not consider the premium.** It is purely the intrinsic
relationship between $S_T$ and $K$.

That matters, because an option can be *in the money* and still lose you money
overall. From Part A: a long call with $K = 95$ and a premium of 1, at
$S_T = 95.5$, is **ITM** — but the payoff is $0.5 - 1 = -0.5$. Moneyness tells
you whether you would **exercise**; it does not tell you whether the trade was
profitable.

So moneyness decides one thing at expiry: whether the option is **exercised**
or left to **expire**.

## Exercise style, in time

The other half of "when can I act" is the exercise style:

- **American** — exercisable at **any point** during the life of the contract.
  Its moneyness can be checked, and acted on, all the way along.
- **European** — exercisable **only at maturity**. Moneyness only matters on
  the final date.

## Corporate actions: splits and dividends

An option contract is written on a number of shares at a strike price. A
**corporate action** can change what a "share" even means — so the contract has
to be adjusted, or the holder would be arbitrarily enriched or wiped out.

### Stock split

> In a **3-for-1 stock split**, three new shares are issued for each existing
> share. One XYZ share becomes three XYZ shares.

The company is not worth more afterwards; the same value is just divided into
more, cheaper pieces. So the option contract is adjusted to keep the holder
exactly where they were.

**The rule:**

| Quantity | Adjustment for an $n$-for-1 split |
| --- | --- |
| **Strike price** | **Divided** by $n$ → $K/n$ |
| **Number of shares** | **Multiplied** by $n$ |

### Worked example — 3-for-1

Suppose you hold a call option:

| | Pre-split | Post-split (3-for-1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strike $K$ | \$90 | $90/3 =$ **\$30** |
| Shares per contract | 100 | $100 \times 3 =$ **300** |
| **Total strike value** | $90 \times 100 = $ **\$9,000** | $30 \times 300 = $ **\$9,000** |

Identical. The split changed the units, not your position.

### Worked example — 4-for-1

The same rule with $n = 4$: strike becomes $K/4$, share count becomes
$\times 4$.

| | Pre-split | Post-split |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strike | \$100 | **\$25** |
| Shares | 100 | **400** |
| **Total** | **\$10,000** | **\$10,000** |

Again unchanged — which is the entire point of the adjustment.

### Stock dividend

> A stock dividend involves a company issuing **more shares** to its existing
> stakeholders.

A stock dividend is quoted as a percentage, and the trick is to convert that
percentage into an equivalent split:

$$
\frac{100\%}{\text{dividend }\%} \;=\; \text{shares you must already hold to receive one new share}
$$

| Stock dividend | $100/\%$ | Meaning | Equivalent split |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **10%** | 10 | 1 new share for every **10** held | 11-for-10 |
| **20%** | 5 | 1 new share for every **5** held | 6-for-5 |
| **25%** | 4 | 1 new share for every **4** held | **5-for-4** |

Once you have the equivalent split, the adjustment is the same rule as before.
For a 25% stock dividend (a 5-for-4 split):

$$
\text{shares} \;\to\; \times \tfrac{5}{4}
\qquad\qquad
K \;\to\; \times \tfrac{4}{5}
$$

### Worked example — a put, and a 25% stock dividend

Consider a put option contract to sell **100 shares** of the company at
**\$15 per share**. The company then announces a **25% stock dividend**.

$100\% / 25\% = 4$, so one new share for every four held — a **5-for-4 split**.

| | Pre-dividend | Post-dividend |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of shares | 100 | $100 \times \tfrac{5}{4} =$ **125** |
| Strike $K$ | \$15 | $15 \times \tfrac{4}{5} =$ **\$12** |
| **Total value** | $100 \times 15 =$ **\$1,500** | $125 \times 12 =$ **\$1,500** |

Unchanged, exactly as with a split.

### Cash dividend — the exception

This one catches people out:

> A **cash dividend does NOT adjust the option contract.**

If XYZ pays \$3 per share in cash, the option contract is left alone. Only
**stock** dividends and **stock splits** change the strike and the share count.

(Cash dividends do still matter for *pricing* — they are an input to the
Black-Scholes-Merton model — but they do not rewrite the contract terms.)

## Summary

- **Moneyness** is the relationship between $S_T$ and $K$ — ITM, ATM or OTM.
- For a **call** ITM means $S_T > K$; for a **put** it is **reversed**,
  $S_T < K$.
- Moneyness **ignores the premium**. It decides whether you *exercise*, not
  whether you *profited*.
- **American** options can be exercised any time, **European** only at maturity.
- A **stock split** divides the strike and multiplies the share count, leaving
  the contract's total value untouched.
- A **stock dividend** does the same thing, once you convert the percentage into
  an equivalent split via $100\%/\text{dividend }\%$ — a 25% dividend is a
  5-for-4 split.
- A **cash dividend does not adjust the contract at all.**

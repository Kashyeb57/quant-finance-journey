Sharpe, Sortino and Treynor Ratios

Return alone says nothing. 23% earned by taking enormous risk is not the same achievement as 23% earned quietly, and these three ratios are how that difference is made visible.

All three share the same numerator — excess return — and differ only in what they divide it by.

1 — Sharpe ratio​

The Sharpe ratio measures the excess return earned per unit of total risk.

  Sharpe ratio=Rp−Rfσp  \boxed{\;\text{Sharpe ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}\;}Sharpe ratio=σp​Rp​−Rf​​​
RpR_pRp​Portfolio return
RfR_fRf​Risk-free rate
σp\sigma_pσp​Standard deviation of the portfolio

The numerator Rp−RfR_p - R_fRp​−Rf​ is the excess return — what you earned above what you could have had risk-free. There is no credit for matching the Treasury rate.

A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted return.

SharpeReading
> 1Good investment — generating decent return relative to the risk
< 1Bad investment — poor; the return you are generating does not justify the level of risk you are taking

The notes' illustration: a portfolio returning 12% with 20% volatility has a Sharpe below 1 — you are being paid 12 for carrying 20.

2 — Sortino ratio​

The Sortino ratio refines the Sharpe ratio by considering only the downside risk — negative volatility.

  Sortino ratio=Rp−Rfσd  \boxed{\;\text{Sortino ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_d}\;}Sortino ratio=σd​Rp​−Rf​​​

where σd\sigma_dσd​ is the downside volatility.

This metric is more significant for risk-averse investors.

A high Sortino ratio indicates better return relative to the downside risk.

Reason: it penalises the investment only for negative deviation from the threshold.

That is the whole argument for it. Sharpe's denominator punishes a portfolio for going up unexpectedly, which no investor actually minds. Sortino removes that penalty.

3 — Treynor ratio​

The Treynor ratio measures the excess return per unit of systematic risk (beta).

  Treynor ratio=Rp−Rfβp  \boxed{\;\text{Treynor ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p}\;}Treynor ratio=βp​Rp​−Rf​​​

where βp\beta_pβp​ is the portfolio beta.

Goal: the Treynor ratio focuses on how well the portfolio compensates the investor for market risk rather than total risk.

The distinction matters for anyone holding more than one portfolio. Risk specific to a holding can be diversified away across a wider book; market risk cannot. So for a portfolio that is one sleeve of something larger, market risk is the only risk that genuinely survives — and Treynor is the ratio that prices it.

Same numerator, three denominators. Choosing between them is choosing which risk you think the investor actually bears.

What they say​

Sharpe of 0.576 is below 1 — by the deck's own rule of thumb, the return does not justify the total risk taken. 23% sounds excellent until you notice it came with 32% volatility.

Sortino of 0.866 is markedly higher than Sharpe — a 50% improvement. That gap is informative: it means a large share of this portfolio's volatility was upside, which Sharpe penalised and Sortino does not. Downside deviation (21.6%) is only two-thirds of total volatility (32.5%).

Treynor of 0.158 is not comparable to the other two — its denominator is a beta, not a percentage, so the number lives on a different scale entirely. Treynor is only meaningful compared against another portfolio's Treynor, never against a Sharpe.

One number to check before you compare anything

These three ratios are internally consistent — all use annualised return, volatility and risk-free rate.

But as the previous note flagged, alpha in the same summary table is left in daily units (0.0176%, or 4.44% annualised). Mixing conventions inside one table is the kind of quiet error that survives review, because every individual number looks reasonable.

It is precisely what SR 11-7's process verification is meant to catch.

Summary​

  • All three ratios share the numerator excess return Rp−RfR_p - R_fRp​−Rf​ and differ only in the denominator.
  • Sharpe divides by total risk (σ\sigmaσ): above 1 is good, below 1 means the return does not justify the risk.
  • Sortino divides by downside risk (σd\sigma_dσd​), penalising only negative deviation from the threshold — better suited to risk-averse investors.
  • Treynor divides by beta, pricing market risk rather than total risk — the right measure when the portfolio sits inside a larger, diversified book.
  • On the real portfolio: Sharpe 0.576, Sortino 0.866, Treynor 0.158, from a 23.03% return against a 4.323% risk-free rate.
  • Sortino exceeding Sharpe by 50% shows much of the volatility was upside — which Sharpe wrongly penalised.
  • Treynor is not comparable to Sharpe or Sortino — different denominator, different scale.

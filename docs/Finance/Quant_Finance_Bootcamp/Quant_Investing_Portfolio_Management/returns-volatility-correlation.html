Returns, Volatility and Correlation

The final module of the bootcamp, and the one that measures whether an investment was actually any good. It starts with three quantities everything else is built from.

What is a return?​

A return is the money made or lost on an investment over a period of time.

Returns are expressed in percentage. A positive return represents a profit, a negative return represents a loss — $100 falling to $98 is a return of −2%.

There are two ways to compute one.

Simple return​

Simple return=St−St−1St−1or equivalently  StSt−1−1  \text{Simple return} = \frac{S_t - S_{t-1}}{S_{t-1}} \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad \boxed{\;\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}} - 1\;}Simple return=St−1​St​−St−1​​or equivalentlySt−1​St​​−1​

Log return​

  Log return=ln⁡(StSt−1)  \boxed{\;\text{Log return} = \ln\left(\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}}\right)\;}Log return=ln(St−1​St​​)​
In Excel, LN is the one you want

The notes flag a trap: Excel's LN is the natural logarithm — that is what "log return" means here. Excel's LOG is base 10 and is not what you want.

When to use which​

Performing short-term analysis → simple return.

Simple returns are the default for short-term investment analysis, because they are what people actually understand. But they have three limitations.

1) Less accurate with large price fluctuations.

The notes' example is Bitcoin — with moves that large, simple returns distort, and log returns are the right choice.

2) Not suitable for statistical modelling.

This is the substantive one, and it connects back through the whole course:

ModelAssumes
Black-Scholesthe stock price follows log returns
Time series volatility models — ARCH, GARCHlog returns
Linear regressionnormality of residuals

Log returns are preferred because they are stable and often closer to a normal distribution.

Which is exactly the point Black-Scholes rests on: returns are normal, the price is log-normal. Feed a model simple returns when it assumes log returns and you have created model risk of the "used incorrectly" kind.

3) Not suitable for high-frequency data.

Second-by-second data — 15-second, 10-second, 1-second bars — should use log returns.

So why not always use log returns?​

It is less interpretable for a non-technical audience.

A 2% simple return means you have 2% more money. A 1.98% log return does not mean that, quite. When the audience is not quantitative, interpretability wins.

Volatility​

Volatility (or standard deviation) is a statistical measure that quantifies the amount of variation in a stock. In the context of finance it measures the volatility of an asset or portfolio.

Annualising​

  σannual=σdaily×252  \boxed{\;\sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sigma_{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{252}\;}σannual​=σdaily​×252​​

And the notes derive it rather than asserting it, which is worth following:

Variance is the square of standard deviation, V=σ2V = \sigma^2V=σ2, so σ=V\sigma = \sqrt{V}σ=V​. Variance increases linearly with time:

Vannual=Vdaily×252V_{\text{annual}} = V_{\text{daily}} \times 252Vannual​=Vdaily​×252

Take the square root of both sides:

Vannual=Vdaily×252⟹σannual=σdaily×252\sqrt{V_{\text{annual}}} = \sqrt{V_{\text{daily}}} \times \sqrt{252} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sigma_{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{252}Vannual​​=Vdaily​​×252​⟹σannual​=σdaily​×252​

252 because a year has 365 days minus Saturdays, Sundays and holidays.

This is the same T\sqrt{T}T​ rule that scaled VaR across horizons and the same Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ that scaled the Brownian shock in GBM — it is variance that adds, so standard deviation grows with the square root.

Downside standard deviation​

Downside standard deviation is a measure of the downside risk of an investment. It focuses only on negative deviations.

Unlike standard deviation, which captures both upside volatility and downside volatility.

That distinction matters because ordinary volatility treats a $100 stock going to $120 as exactly as "risky" as it going to $80. An investor does not.

Downside standard deviation considers only downside volatility. This is particularly useful for risk-averse investors.

  Downside std dev=∑min⁡(Ri−T,  0)2n  \boxed{\;\text{Downside std dev} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum \min\left(R_i - T,\; 0\right)^2}{n}}\;}Downside std dev=n∑min(Ri​−T,0)2​​​

The min is a filter. Good days contribute nothing to the risk measure; only shortfalls below the threshold do.

Correlation​

Correlation is a statistical measure that quantifies the relationship between 2 variables, showing how they move relative to each other.

It is always between −1 and 1:

CorrelationMeaning
+1Perfect positive correlation — the stocks move in the same direction
−1Perfect negative correlation — the stocks move in opposite directions
0No correlation — the stocks are independent of each other

Why sector matters: AAPL and MSFT are both technology, so their correlation is higher — because they are influenced by similar industry trends.

Correlations are not fixed

NOTE: Correlations are not fixed. They can change over time.

This is the assumption that breaks in a crisis, and it broke expensively in 2008. A portfolio diversified on historical correlations can find those correlations converging toward 1 exactly when diversification was supposed to help.

It is also why the variance-covariance VaR note measured a 10.14% diversification benefit on a bank portfolio and then noted that all three banks correlated at ~0.66 because they were the same sector. The benefit is real, and it is not guaranteed to persist.

The real portfolio​

The workbook runs all of this on a 50/50 JPM + MS portfolio benchmarked against SPY, over 1,256 daily returns from 2020.

JPMMSPortfolio
Mean daily return0.0007450.0010830.000914
Annualised return18.78%27.29%23.03%
Daily volatility0.0204980.0225070.020451
Annualised volatility32.54%35.73%32.47%

Two things are visible immediately.

The portfolio's volatility (32.47%) is below both constituents (32.54% and 35.73%) even though its return sits between them. That gap is the diversification benefit, produced by a JPM-MS correlation of 0.8088 — high, because both are banks, but still below 1.

Downside deviation (21.61%) is well below total volatility (32.47%) — about two-thirds. Much of what ordinary volatility was penalising was upside movement.

Cumulative return​

Cumulative return=∏(1+rt)−1=142.67%\text{Cumulative return} = \prod\left(1 + r_t\right) - 1 = \mathbf{142.67\%}Cumulative return=∏(1+rt​)−1=142.67%

Computed as =PRODUCT(L3:L1258)-1, chaining every daily return geometrically — not by summing them. Summing daily returns would overstate the result, because returns compound.

Summary​

  • A return is money made or lost over a period, expressed as a percentage.
  • Simple return =St/St−1−1= S_t/S_{t-1} - 1=St​/St−1​−1; log return =ln⁡(St/St−1)= \ln(S_t/S_{t-1})=ln(St​/St−1​) — and in Excel that is LN, not LOG.
  • Simple for short-term analysis and non-technical audiences; log for large fluctuations, statistical modelling and high-frequency data, because log returns are more stable and closer to normal.
  • Volatility is the standard deviation of returns; high volatility means more risk. Annualise with σannual=σdaily252\sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sigma_{\text{daily}}\sqrt{252}σannual​=σdaily​252​, which follows from variance growing linearly with time.
  • Downside deviation counts only returns below a threshold — the min zeroes out good days — and matters to risk-averse investors.
  • Correlation runs −1 to +1; same-sector stocks correlate highly, and correlations change over time.
  • On the real 50/50 JPM/MS portfolio: 23.03% annualised return, 32.47% volatility, 21.61% downside deviation, 142.67% cumulative — with portfolio volatility below both constituents thanks to a 0.8088 correlation.

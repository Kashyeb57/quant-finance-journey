Alpha, Beta and R-Squared

The previous note measured a portfolio on its own terms. These three measure it against a benchmark — and all three come out of a single regression of portfolio returns on market returns.

Alpha​

Alpha measures the excess return of an investment relative to a benchmark index.

It indicates whether the investment outperformed or underperformed the market, after accounting for risk.

AlphaMeaning
PositiveThe investment outperformed the market
NegativeThe investment underperformed the market
ZeroThe investment performed in line with the market

Alpha is the part of the return the benchmark does not account for. If the S&P 500 returned 15% and your portfolio returned 15%, you produced no alpha — whatever skill you applied, the market would have given you the same result.

Beta​

Beta measures the sensitivity of the investment return to the market's movement.

BetaMeaning
β>1\beta > 1β>1The investment is more volatile than the market
β<1\beta < 1β<1The investment is less volatile than the market
β=1\beta = 1β=1The investment moves in line with the market

Worked: a stock with β=1.2\beta = 1.2β=1.2 —

  • when the market rises by 10%, the stock is expected to rise by 12%
  • when the market falls by 10%, the stock is expected to fall by 12%

Beta cuts both ways. It is amplification, not advantage — a high-beta portfolio in a rising market looks like skill and is not.

Beta is the systematic risk from Module 6

This is the same quantity that Treynor's ratio divides by in the next note, and conceptually the same split the variance-covariance VaR note relied on: risk that comes from the market as a whole cannot be diversified away, and risk specific to the holding can be.

Beta measures only the first kind.

R-squared​

R-squared measures the percentage of an investment's movement that can be explained by the movement in the benchmark index.

It comes from the same linear regression of portfolio returns against the benchmark.

R2R^2R2Meaning
100% (or 1)The portfolio's returns are fully explained by the benchmark
0The portfolio's returns aren't explained by the benchmark at all
85% (or 0.85)85% of the portfolio's returns are explained by the benchmark, while 15% are due to unique factors

R2R^2R2 is the honesty check on the other two. A beta computed against a benchmark that explains almost nothing is not telling you much — the regression line exists, but the relationship it describes is weak.

Recomputed independently from the underlying return columns: β = 1.1881, R² = 0.5911, correlation = 0.7689 — matching the sheet to three decimals (the small difference is a two-row range discrepancy).

Reading the results​

β = 1.1881. The portfolio is 19% more volatile than the market. When SPY moves 10%, this portfolio is expected to move about 11.9%. That is unsurprising for two investment banks — they are levered to the economic cycle in a way the broad index is not.

R² = 0.5909. 59% of the portfolio's movement is explained by SPY, and 41% comes from factors specific to JPM and MS — bank-sector news, earnings, regulation. So the benchmark explains most of the story but nowhere near all of it.

α = 0.0176% per day.

Where the intercept of 1.0002 comes from

The regression intercept is 1.000176, not the near-zero you would expect, and the sheet then computes alpha as =(Y51-1)*100.

The reason: the regression was run with (1 + portfolio return) as the dependent variable against the market return as the independent one. So

1+rp=a+βrm⟹rp=(a−1)+βrm1 + r_p = a + \beta r_m \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r_p = (a - 1) + \beta r_m1+rp​=a+βrm​⟹rp​=(a−1)+βrm​

which makes the true alpha a−1=0.000176a - 1 = 0.000176a−1=0.000176. Verified directly: regressing rpr_prp​ on rmr_mrm​ gives an intercept of 0.000167, and 1+0.000167=1.0001671 + 0.000167 = 1.0001671+0.000167=1.000167 against the sheet's 1.000176.

It is a valid setup, just an unusual one — and it means the intercept must have 1 subtracted before it means anything. Reading 1.000176 as "alpha" would be wildly wrong.

Alpha is daily; everything beside it is annualised

The summary table reports α = 0.0176% alongside an annualised return of 23.03%, annualised volatility of 32.47% and a Sharpe ratio built from annual figures.

But that alpha is a daily number. Annualised it is

0.00017612×252=0.0444=4.44%0.00017612 \times 252 = 0.0444 = \mathbf{4.44\%}0.00017612×252=0.0444=4.44%

which is a materially different claim — 4.44% of annual outperformance, not 0.018%. The ratios in the next note are internally consistent (all annual); alpha is the one figure left in daily units while sitting in an annual table.

Worth knowing which convention a reported alpha is in before comparing it to anything.

Putting the three together​

MeasureQuestion it answers
AlphaDid we beat the benchmark?
BetaHow much market risk did we take to do it?
R²Is the benchmark even the right comparison?

Read alone, each is misleading. A high alpha with a beta of 1.9 is mostly leverage. A confident beta with an R² of 0.1 is a number computed against an irrelevant index. The three are one answer, not three.

Summary​

  • Alpha is excess return relative to a benchmark — positive means outperformance, negative underperformance, zero means in line.
  • Beta is sensitivity to market movement: β>1\beta > 1β>1 more volatile, β<1\beta < 1β<1 less, β=1\beta = 1β=1 in line. A β of 1.2 moves 12% when the market moves 10% — in both directions.
  • R² is the percentage of movement explained by the benchmark; the remainder is unique factors.
  • On the real JPM/MS portfolio against SPY: β = 1.1881 (19% more volatile than the market), R² = 0.5909 (59% explained, 41% bank-specific), and α = 0.0176% daily, all independently verified.
  • The intercept is 1.000176 because (1 + rₚ) was regressed on r_m — subtract 1 to get alpha.
  • That alpha is daily while the rest of the table is annualised; annualised it is 4.44%.

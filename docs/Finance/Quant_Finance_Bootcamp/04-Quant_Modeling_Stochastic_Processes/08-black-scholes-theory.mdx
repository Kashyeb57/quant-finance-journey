> The Black-Scholes model is a mathematical model for **pricing European-style
> options.**

It was developed by **Fischer Black** and **Myron Scholes** in the early 1970s,
and it provides a **theoretical estimate** of the price of a European-style
option.

## Why do we need it?

Three reasons, and only the first is the obvious one.

**a) Fair pricing.** The model helps determine the **fair market value** of the
option. This ensures **neither the buyer nor the seller is at a disadvantage** —
there is a defensible number, rather than two parties guessing.

**b) Regulatory compliance.** Financial institutions are **required to mark
their positions to market** and ensure they are **accurately valued**. Doing
that at scale means using a **quantitative model** — and Black-Scholes is the
one.

**c) Hedging the portfolio.** This is the link back to everything in the
previous two modules:

$$
\text{BS model} \longrightarrow \text{price of option} \longrightarrow \text{Greeks} \longrightarrow \text{hedging}
$$

The model produces a **price**. Differentiate that price and you get the
[**Greeks**](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/greeks-for-options-part-a)
— **delta, gamma, rho, theta, vega**. Delta in particular:

$$
\Delta = \frac{\text{change in option price}}{\text{change in the underlying}}
$$

where **Black-Scholes supplies the numerator**. And delta is what
[delta hedging](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Risk_Management_Greeks/delta-hedging)
runs on. So the chain closes: **BS → price → Greeks → hedging.** Without a
pricing model there are no Greeks, and without Greeks there is no hedge.

## Assumptions

Six of them, and the lecturer flags this section hard — the assumptions are
where the model's failures live.

**(a) No dividends** are paid during the life of the option.

**(b) No transaction cost** in buying the option.

**(c) The returns of the underlying asset are normally distributed.**

This is the one that breaks. The margin note names the counterexamples
directly: **2007-2008** and the **pandemic**, where returns spiked far outside
what a normal distribution allows. The response is either **other models** or
**tweak the existing model**.

distinction carefully:

**Returns** are normally distributed — symmetric, taking negative and positive
values alike. **The stock price is not.** A stock cannot go negative; it is
floored at **0**. So the price is **log-normally distributed** while the
*returns* are normally distributed. Those are two different statements, and the
model only claims the second.

**(d) The model can be used only for European options** — options **exercised
only at maturity**. An **American option can be exercised at any point in time**,
so **Black-Scholes does not apply to it.** The notes mark this with an explicit
✗.

**(e) The risk-free rate and the volatility of the underlying are known and
constant.** In the
[geometric Brownian motion](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/model-stock-gbm-excel)
that underlies the model, these are the two parameters:

| GBM term | Symbol | Set to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drift** | $\mu$ | the **risk-free rate** |
| **Diffusion** | $\sigma$ | the volatility |

**(f) Markets are random**, so **market movements cannot be predicted.** Which
is why **Black-Scholes is a stochastic model** — it does not forecast the
price, it describes the distribution the price is drawn from.

## The formula

For a European **call**:

$$
\boxed{\;C = S_0\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)\;}
$$

with

$$
d_1 = \frac{\ln\!\left(\frac{S_0}{K}\right) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}
\qquad\qquad
d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}
$$

And for a European **put**:

$$
\boxed{\;P = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S_0\,N(-d_1)\;}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $C$ | Call option price |
| $P$ | Put option price |
| $K$ | Strike price |
| $S_0$ | Current price of the underlying asset |
| $r$ | Risk-free interest rate |
| $t$ | Time to maturity |
| $N(\cdot)$ | **CDF of the standard normal distribution** |

## The components

**$S_0$** — the current price of the underlying asset.

**$K$** — the price at which you can **buy** (call) or **sell** (put) the
underlying asset in the future.

**$T$** — the time remaining until the option expires, **expressed in years**.
A single trading day is therefore

$$
T \rightarrow \frac{1}{252}
$$

**$r$** — the risk-free rate, and the notes are specific about how to pick it:
**match the maturity of the option.**

| Option expiring in | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **1 month** | the **1-month Treasury rate** |
| **3 months** | the **3-month US Treasury rate** |

The source is the **US Department of the Treasury** — **US Treasury rates are
the risk-free rate in the US.** The notes leave the boxes for **India** and
**Germany** blank, which is the point: the risk-free rate is
jurisdiction-specific, and you use the government curve of the currency you are
pricing in.

## Volatility

> **Volatility** ($\sigma$) is a measure of how much the price of the underlying
> asset is **expected to fluctuate over a year.**

**Higher volatility means the asset price can move more dramatically.** It comes
in two kinds:

| | Looks | Is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Historical volatility** | **backward** | How the price of the underlying **has fluctuated in the past** — computed from historical data |
| **Implied volatility** | **forward** | **Derived from the market price of the option**; represents the **market's expectation of future volatility** |

### When to use historical volatility

**a) Historical analysis** — when performing any analysis **based on past price
movements**, and for **backtesting a trading strategy or risk management**.

**b) Comparative study** — when you **compare past volatility across different
assets**.

### When to use implied volatility

**a) Forward-looking analysis** — when the goal is to **incorporate the market's
expectation of future volatility**, and **for pricing options**.

**b) Trading and hedging** — for a trader it is **very important to understand
market sentiment and expectation**. This is the branch that feeds
**Black-Scholes → hedging.**

The division is simple: you price with **implied**, you study the past with
**historical**.

## What N(d₁) and N(d₂) actually mean

This is the part worth slowing down on, because the two look similar and are
not.

### N(d₁) — the probability of finishing in the money

> $N(d_1)$ is the probability that the **stock price will be higher than the
> strike price at maturity** — the probability the option **expires ITM.**

For a call:

$$
S_T > K \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{In the Money}
$$

And the reason a CDF appears at all:

$$
N(\cdot) \longrightarrow \text{CDF of the standard normal} \longrightarrow \text{probability} \longrightarrow \text{ITM} \longrightarrow \text{exercised}
$$

**Example.** $N(d_1) = 60\%$, or 0.6 — there is a **60% chance the option
expires in the money**, i.e. that $S_T > K$.

### N(d₂) — the probability of making money

> $N(d_2)$ is the probability that the stock price will **end up above the
> strike price**, but **adjusted to account for the time value of money.**

It is also a CDF of the standard normal. The distinction is what it is the
probability *of*: **that you will actually make money from the option.**

**Example.** $N(d_2) = 55\%$, or 0.55 — there is a **55% chance you will
actually make money** from the option.

### Side by side

| | Answers |
| --- | --- |
| $N(d_1)$ | **Will it be in the money, or not?** |
| $N(d_2)$ | **Will you make money, or not?** |

The gap between 60% and 55% is not noise — it is **time and the risk-free
rate**. Finishing above the strike is not the same as profiting, because the
strike you pay is paid *later*, and must be discounted:

$$
N(d_2)\,e^{-rT}
$$

An option can expire in the money and still lose you money. $N(d_1)$ does not
know that; $N(d_2)$ does.

## Reading the formula term by term

With that, the call formula stops being opaque:

$$
C = \underbrace{S_0\,N(d_1)}_{\text{what you get}} \;-\; \underbrace{K e^{-rT} N(d_2)}_{\text{what you pay}}
$$

**First term — $S_0 N(d_1)$.** The **present value of the expected payoff if the
option is exercised**, weighted by the probability $N(d_1)$ that it finishes
**ITM**. This is the asset you receive.

**Second term — $K e^{-rT} N(d_2)$.** The **present value of the strike price**,
weighted by $N(d_2)$. This is the cash you hand over, discounted back from
expiry because you do not pay it today.

So the price of a call is **what you expect to receive minus what you expect to
pay**, each weighted by the probability that you receive or pay it at all. The
put formula is the same idea with the two sides swapped and the probabilities
mirrored, $N(-d_1)$ and $N(-d_2)$.

## Summary

- Black-Scholes prices **European options only** — Black and Scholes, early
  1970s — and exists for **fair pricing, regulatory mark-to-market, and
  hedging.**
- It is the head of the chain **BS → price → Greeks → hedging**: the model
  supplies the option price that every Greek is a derivative of.
- **Six assumptions**: no dividends, no transaction costs, normally distributed
  **returns**, European exercise only, known and constant $r$ and $\sigma$, and
  random markets.
- **Returns are normal; the stock price is log-normal** — because a price cannot
  go below zero.
- $C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)$, with $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}$ and
  $N(\cdot)$ the **standard normal CDF**.
- **$N(d_1)$ is the probability of expiring in the money; $N(d_2)$ is the
  probability of making money.** The difference between them is the time value
  of money.
- Match $r$ to the option's maturity, and use **implied** volatility to price,
  **historical** volatility to study the past.

The [Asian option](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-asian-option)
was path-dependent through an **average**. A barrier option is path-dependent
through a **threshold** — and the whole contract can be switched on or off by a
single moment along the way.

## Definition

> Barrier options are a type of **exotic contract** where the payoff depends on
> **whether the underlying asset price reaches a predetermined barrier level or
> not** — at any point **during the life of the option contract.**

That last clause is what makes it path-dependent. Not the price at expiry; the
price at *every* moment.

## The three types

| Type | Variants |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Knock-in** | **Up & In**, **Down & In** |
| **2. Knock-out** | **Up & Out**, **Down & Out** |
| **3. Double barrier** | **Double knock-in**, **Double knock-out** |

And the rule that governs all six:

$$
\textbf{In} \longrightarrow \text{Active}
\qquad\qquad
\textbf{Out} \longrightarrow \text{Deactivate}
$$

### Knock-in — off until touched

> These options **become active (knock in) only if the underlying asset hits a
> certain barrier level during the life of the option contract.**

> **If the barrier is never reached, the option expires worthless.**

| Path | Result | Payoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hits the barrier** | **Active** | $\max(S_T - K,\, 0)$ for a call, $\max(K - S_T,\, 0)$ for a put |
| **Never touches it** | Inactive | **0** |

- **Up & In** — the path crosses an **up barrier from below** → active.
- **Down & In** — the path crosses a **down barrier from above** → active.

> The contract becomes active when the barrier is crossed (up or down),
> otherwise the contract will **expire worthless.**

### Knock-out — on until touched

The mirror image: **the option deactivates when the barrier is crossed.**

| | Crosses the barrier | Never crosses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Up & Out** | **Inactive** | Active |
| **Down & Out** | **Inactive** | Active |

So a knock-out holder is *hoping the level never trades*, and a knock-in holder
is hoping it does.

### Double barrier — two levels at once

There is both an **up barrier and a down barrier**.

| | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Double knock-in** | If **either** barrier is crossed, the contract becomes **active**; otherwise inactive, **payoff = 0** |
| **Double knock-out** | If **either** barrier is breached, the contract becomes **inactive**; if not breached, **active** |

For a double knock-in, a path that wanders quietly **between** the two barriers
is worth nothing — it needed to break out in one direction or the other. For a
double knock-out, that same quiet path is the only one that pays.

## Testing the barrier

In a simulation, "did it touch?" is just a question about the **range of the
path**:

| Barrier | Test |
| --- | --- |
| **Up barrier** | $\max(\text{range}) \ge B$ |
| **Down barrier** | $\min(\text{range}) \le B$ |

The notes work a down-barrier example: with $B = 100$, a path that drifts down
to **90** gives $\min(\text{range}) = 90 \le 100$ → **barrier crossed.**

Two lines of logic, and they are the entire difference between a barrier option
and a vanilla one.

## Valuing it with Monte Carlo

Seven steps, of which only step 4 and 5 are new:

| # | Step |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **Initialise the parameters** |
| 2 | **Generate random numbers** using the **standard normal distribution** |
| 3 | Use those numbers in the **GBM equation and simulate the stock price** |
| 4 | **Check if, during the life of the option, any barrier is crossed or not** |
| 5 | Apply the **knock-in / knock-out** rule to get the payoff |
| 6 | **Average the payoffs** |
| 7 | **Discount** the average |

Step 5 branches:

$$
\text{Crossed} \Rightarrow \text{Active} \Rightarrow \max(S_T - K,\, 0)
\qquad\qquad
\text{Not crossed} \Rightarrow \text{Inactive} \Rightarrow \text{Payoff} = 0
$$

and then it is the familiar ending:

$$
\text{Avg payoff} = \frac{\text{Payoff}_1 + \text{Payoff}_2 + \cdots + \text{Payoff}_{10{,}000}}{10{,}000}
$$

$$
\text{Price of barrier option} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]
$$

## The Excel sheet

Same 100-path, 6-step grid as before. The barrier logic is four rows:

| Cell | Label | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| K7 | **Up barrier** | **557** |
| O9 | Max value | `=MAX(O3:O8)` |
| O10 | Barrier crossed (?) | `=IF(O9>$K$7,"Yes","No")` |
| **O11** | **Check barrier (call)** | **`=IF(MAX(O3:O8)>=$K$7, MAX(O8-$B$6,0), 0)`** |
| O12 | Check barrier (put) | `=IF(MAX(O3:O8)>=$K$7, MAX($B$6-O8,0), 0)` |
| O13 | Average payoff (call) | `=AVERAGE(O11:DJ11)` |
| O14 | Discounted price (call) | `=O13*EXP(-B8*B7)` |

`O11` is the whole contract in one cell: **if the path's maximum reached the
barrier, pay the ordinary call payoff; otherwise pay nothing.** With an up
barrier and payment *on* crossing, this sheet implements an **up-and-in call**.

:::note Two small inconsistencies in the sheet

`O10` — the human-readable "Yes/No" — tests `O9 > K7` (strictly greater), while
the payoff cells `O11`/`O12` test `>= K7`. A path whose maximum lands exactly on
557 is reported as **"No"** but paid as though it crossed. Harmless in floating
point, but the two should agree.

The path generator also carries the **same drift error** flagged in the
[European note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-european-option)
— drift multiplied by `SQRT(N3)` instead of `N3`, and a daily mean return in
place of the risk-free rate. The barrier logic above is correct; the prices it
produces are not.

:::

## Checking it: in + out must equal the vanilla

There is no closed form here either — but barrier options come with an identity
that makes them self-checking:

$$
\text{Up-and-in} + \text{Up-and-out} = \text{Vanilla European}
$$

Every path either touches the barrier or it doesn't. The knock-in collects the
payoff in the first case, the knock-out collects it in the second, and between
them they collect every payoff exactly once. Simulated over 400,000 paths on the
SPY contract, barrier 557:

| | Call |
| --- | --- |
| **Up-and-in** | 1.6658 |
| **Up-and-out** | 0.0357 |
| **Sum** | **1.7015** |
| **European** | **1.7015** |

Exact. That identity is the barrier equivalent of the European note's
Black-Scholes check — a way to know the simulation is right when no formula is
available to compare against.

Two things it reveals about *this* contract:

**31% of paths reach the barrier.** With six trading days, an 11.1% volatility
and the barrier only 8 points above spot, most paths never get there.

**The up-and-out call is nearly worthless** — 0.0357 against the vanilla's
1.7015. To pay anything it must finish **above 555** while never having touched
**557**: a two-dollar window it has to land in and stay in. Almost every path
that finishes in the money got there by going through the barrier first, and
knocked itself out on the way.

Which is the general lesson about barriers. Moving a barrier a couple of points
can take most of the value out of an option, because it is not the destination
being priced — it is every step of the route.

## Module 4, end to end

That completes the module's four pricing methods on the same SPY contract:

| Method | Handles | Call price | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Binomial tree** | European, American | 1.7131 | Discrete up/down lattice, valued backwards |
| **Black-Scholes** | European only | 1.7054 | Closed-form formula |
| **Monte Carlo** | European, American, **exotic** | 1.7065 | Simulate, payoff, discount, average |
| **Monte Carlo**, up-and-in | **Barrier** | 1.6658 | The same, plus a path condition |

The three methods that price the vanilla call **agree to within a cent** — a
lattice, a formula and 400,000 random walks converging on the same number from
three unrelated directions.

And the progression through the module is one of increasing generality bought
with increasing computation. Black-Scholes is instant but only handles one
contract type. The binomial tree adds early exercise. Monte Carlo is the
slowest and handles everything, because it does not need a formula for the
payoff — it only needs to be able to *simulate the path and read the payoff off
it.* Averaging, thresholds, or anything else you can express as a rule on a
price series.

## Summary

- **Barrier options** are exotic contracts whose payoff depends on **whether the
  price reaches a predetermined level during the life of the contract.**
- **Knock-in** options are **inactive until the barrier is touched** — if it is
  never reached, the option **expires worthless**. **Knock-out** options are
  **active until the barrier is touched**, then deactivate.
- Six variants: **Up/Down × In/Out**, plus **double knock-in and double
  knock-out**, which trigger on **either** of two barriers.
- The test is on the **range of the path**: $\max(\text{range}) \ge B$ for an up
  barrier, $\min(\text{range}) \le B$ for a down barrier.
- Valuation adds two steps to Monte Carlo — **check the barrier, apply the
  in/out rule** — then average and discount as always.
- The Excel sheet builds an **up-and-in call** in a single `IF`.
- **In + out = vanilla** is the identity that verifies the simulation; here
  $1.6658 + 0.0357 = 1.7015$, matching the European exactly.
- A barrier a few points away can remove almost all of an option's value — the
  **route** is being priced, not just the destination.

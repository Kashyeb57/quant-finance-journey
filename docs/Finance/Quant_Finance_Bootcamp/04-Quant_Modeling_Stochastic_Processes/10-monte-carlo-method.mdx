> The Monte Carlo method is a **computational technique used to model and solve
> financial problems that involve uncertainty or complex dynamics.**

In computational finance it is *the* fallback — and the notes are precise about
when you reach for it.

## When is it used?

> It is particularly used in areas where the **closed-form analytical solution
> is difficult to derive.**

The notes make the idea of a "closed form" concrete with a quadratic equation:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
$$

That second expression **provides a direct formula for $x$.** Put the numbers
in, get the answer out — no iteration, no simulation. That is a closed form.

Options split the same way:

| Contract | Closed form? | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **European call / put** | ✅ Yes | [Black-Scholes](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/black-scholes-theory) |
| **Exotic contract** (e.g. **Asian option**) | ❌ **Not possible** | **Monte Carlo** |

$$
C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)
$$

is the closed form for a European call. For an **exotic contract whose closed
form is not possible**, you simulate instead.

## Applications

**a) Option pricing.** Monte Carlo is used for pricing **exotic options whose
payoff depends on the *path* of the underlying asset** — not just where it ends
up.

That word *path* is the whole distinction:

| Option | Payoff | Depends on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **European** | $\max(S_T - K,\, 0)$ | The **terminal** price only |
| **Asian** | $\max(\bar{S}_T - K,\, 0)$ | The **average** along the way |

where the average is built from prices sampled during the contract's life:

$$
\bar{S}_T = \frac{S_1 + S_2 + S_3 + S_4}{4}
$$

Black-Scholes cannot see the middle of the path. Monte Carlo generates the whole
path, so it can.

Which method covers which contract:

| Method | Applies to |
| --- | --- |
| **Black-Scholes** | European |
| **Binomial** | American (and European) |
| **Monte Carlo** | **European, American, and exotic contracts** |

Monte Carlo is the most general of the three. That generality is what you buy
with the computation.

**b) Risk management.** Financial institutions use the method to **assess the
risk of their portfolio under various scenarios** — they **simulate the stock
prices** in the portfolio, which gives **Value at Risk**.

> **VaR** — how much potential loss you can have at a **specific confidence
> interval** in a **given time frame.**

VaR itself has three approaches: **Monte Carlo**, the **historical method**, and
the **variance-covariance method.**

**c) Portfolio optimization.**

## The steps

**a) Define the problem statement** — e.g. determine the price of an **exotic
option.**

**b) Identify the inputs of the model** — what has to be simulated:

- **simulate the interest rate** (the notes name the **Vasicek** model)
- **simulate the stock price**

**c) Generate random numbers.** Use a random generator to **produce input for
the model. These inputs should represent the uncertainty.**

The stock price is modelled with **geometric Brownian motion**:

$$
S_t = S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]
$$

where **$W_t$ is the Wiener process / Brownian motion**, built from a
**standard normal random variable $Z$** scaled by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$.

:::note The drift term is written without its t on pages 5 and 6

The source writes this formula as $S_0\exp\left(\left(\mu - \sigma^2/2\right) + \sigma W_t\right)$
— the $t$ multiplying the drift is missing — and page 14 additionally drops the
square, writing $\mu - \sigma/2$. Pages 16, 17 and 18 use the correct
$\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t$ form, and that is what the Excel
implementation uses. Read the early pages as shorthand.

:::

**d) Run simulation trials.** **Input the random numbers generated in step 3
into the model to run individual trials. Each trial (run) will produce an
outcome based on the random input.**

> **Each run simulates the path of the stock price up to the expiration of the
> option, and then we can calculate the payoff.**

At maturity, for a call:

$$
C = \max(S_T - K,\, 0)
$$

**e) Aggregate the result.** Every path produced a payoff at expiry; each is
**discounted back to today.**

**f) Take the average of the discounted payoffs.**

$$
\text{Option price} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \text{discounted payoff}_i
$$

which, written out for five paths, is exactly:

$$
\text{Option price} = \frac{1}{5}\left[e^{-rT}P_1 + e^{-rT}P_2 + \cdots + e^{-rT}P_5\right]
$$

**Simulate many paths → take each terminal price → compute its payoff →
discount every payoff back to today → average them.** That is the entire
method. The average of the discounted payoffs *is* the option price.

## Why averaging works at all

The notes stop here to justify the method, and the justification is two
theorems.

### The class-average analogy

Three students score **100, 90, 80**. The class average is

$$
\frac{100 + 90 + 80}{3}
$$

Nobody finds that controversial. Monte Carlo is doing the same thing with
payoffs instead of marks — the only question is whether the average of a sample
tells you about the truth. Which is exactly what the two theorems answer.

### 1. The Central Limit Theorem

> The CLT states that under certain conditions, the **average of a large number
> of iid random variables** with **finite mean and variance** will approximate a
> **normal distribution.**

**iid** = **independent, identically distributed** random variables.

This is why the method's machinery is legitimate: the **increments of a Wiener
process are iid and normally distributed**, drawn as $Z\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with the
same distribution at every step. And:

> According to the CLT, as the **number of random samples rises**, the
> distribution of the **sample mean** will approximate the **normal
> distribution.**

Monte Carlo methods are often used to **estimate the expected value of a complex
random process** — and the CLT is what says the estimate is well-behaved.

### 2. The Law of Large Numbers

> The LLN states that as the **number of trials (or runs) in the random process
> increases**, the **average of the results** obtained from these trials is
> likely to **converge to the expected value of the random process.**

And the line that matters:

> **This convergence is the reliability of the Monte Carlo method.**

The CLT tells you the *shape* of the estimate's error. The LLN tells you the
error **goes away** if you run enough paths. Together they are the reason a
simulated average is a price and not a guess.

This is also why the notes keep writing **10,000**: more paths, **so that you
can get higher accuracy.** The more simulations you run, the closer the average
sits to the true expected value of the option.

## Setting up a run

**Step 1 — define the input parameters.** All of them feed GBM:

| Parameter | Meaning | Value in the notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $S_0$ | Initial price | |
| $\mu$ | Drift (expected return) | |
| $\sigma$ | Annualised volatility | |
| $\Delta t$ | Time step | $1/252$ |
| $T$ | Maturity | |
| $N$ | Number of steps | $N = T/\Delta t$ |
| $n$ | Number of simulated paths | **10,000** |

**Step 2 — simulate random numbers.**

$$
Z_t \sim N(0,1)
$$

> **Generate a random number from the standard normal distribution for each time
> step and each path.**

Note *each time step and each path* — a 10,000-path run over 252 days needs
2.52 million draws. This is why the method is computational.

**Step 3 — calculate the stock price** using the GBM equation.

**Step 4 — payoff, discount, average** → the option price.

## Path dependence, which is the point

A worked illustration of why any of this is necessary. A path visits:

| Point | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price | 100 | 102 | 104 | 106 | 108 |

A **European** option looks only at the last number: $\max(108 - K,\, 0)$.

An **Asian** option takes the **average of the stock at certain points of the
contract**:

$$
\bar{S}_T = \frac{100 + 102 + 104 + 106 + 108}{5} = 104
$$

The notes call this a **path-dependent variable**, and it is precisely what a
closed-form formula cannot reach. Black-Scholes prices a *destination*; Monte
Carlo prices a *journey*.

## Summary

- Monte Carlo is a **computational technique for problems involving uncertainty
  or complex dynamics** — used **where no closed-form solution exists.**
- A closed form is a **direct formula**, like the quadratic root formula or
  Black-Scholes. **Exotic contracts do not have one.**
- Applications: **option pricing, risk management (VaR), portfolio
  optimisation.** VaR's three methods are **Monte Carlo, historical, and
  variance-covariance.**
- The method: **define the problem → identify inputs → generate random numbers →
  run trials → aggregate → average the discounted payoffs.**
- $\text{Option price} = \frac{1}{N}\sum e^{-rT}\times\text{payoff}$ — simulate,
  discount, average.
- It works because of the **CLT** (the sample mean is normally distributed) and
  the **Law of Large Numbers** (the sample mean converges to the expected
  value). **That convergence is the reliability of the method.**
- **More paths means more accuracy** — hence 10,000.
- It is the only one of the three methods that handles **path dependence.**

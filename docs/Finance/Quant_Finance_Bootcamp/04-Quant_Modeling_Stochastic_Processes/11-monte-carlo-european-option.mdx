A European option **already has a closed form**. So why simulate one?

Because it is the **test case**. If Monte Carlo cannot reproduce Black-Scholes
on a contract where the answer is known, there is no reason to trust it on an
[exotic](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-asian-option)
one where it is not. That check is done at the end of this note — and it is the
most useful thing in it.

## The procedure

**1) Initialise all the parameters.** The notes' worked values:

$$
\mu = 5\% \qquad \sigma = 5.5\% \qquad \Delta t = \frac{1}{252}
$$

feeding **GBM → stock price.**

**2) Generate a random number.** $Z_t$ is a **random number drawn from the
standard normal distribution.**

:::note The margin note that matters

Beside this step the notes write **μ → r ↳ risk-free rate.**

That is the risk-neutral pricing rule, and it is not a detail. When pricing an
option you do **not** drift the stock at its historical expected return $\mu$ —
you drift it at the **risk-free rate $r$**. Otherwise you are pricing your own
forecast rather than the contract. Keep this in mind; the workbook does not
follow it.

:::

**3) Put $Z_t$ into the GBM equation.**

$$
S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t \exp\left[\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_t\right]
$$

**4) Calculate the payoff** at maturity:

$$
\text{Payoff}_{\text{call}} = \max(S_T - K,\, 0)
\qquad\qquad
\text{Payoff}_{\text{put}} = \max(K - S_T,\, 0)
$$

**5) Average the payoffs.**

$$
\text{Avg payoff} = \frac{\text{Payoff}_1 + \text{Payoff}_2 + \cdots + \text{Payoff}_{100}}{100}
$$

**6) Discount the average payoff.**

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Option price} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]\;}
$$

## Walking one path

> **For every path, you will generate the price of the stock every day.**

The chain is recursive — each day starts from the previous day's price:

$$
S_1 = S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_1\right]
$$

$$
S_2 = S_1 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_2\right]
$$

$$
\vdots
$$

$$
S_7 = S_6 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_7\right]
$$

and $S_7$ is the **stock price at maturity for path 1**. A fresh $Z$ is drawn at
every step of every path.

Then that single terminal price produces a single payoff, and the whole path is
reduced to one number. Repeat 100 times, average, discount.

## The Excel implementation

Built on **SPY240709C00555000** — the same contract the
[binomial tree](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/pricing-european-option-binomial-tree)
and [Black-Scholes](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/black-scholes-excel)
notes priced, so all three are directly comparable.

| Cell | Input | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B5 | Stock price $S$ | 549 |
| B6 | Strike $K$ | 555 |
| B7 | Maturity $t$ | `=6/252` |
| B8 | Risk-free rate $r$ | 0.055 |
| K1 | Initial stock price | `=B5` |
| K2 | Drift rate $\mu$ | `=AVERAGE(F3:F253)` → 0.00090561 |
| K3 | Volatility $\sigma$ | `=STDEV.S(F3:F253)*SQRT(252)` → 0.11102 |
| N3:N8 | Time increment | `=1/252` |

The grid itself:

| Region | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| Row 2 | $S_0 = 549$, repeated across every path |
| Rows 3-8 | The **6 daily steps** |
| Columns **O:DJ** | **100 simulated paths**, one per column |
| Row 9 | Call payoff — `=MAX(O8-$B$6,0)` |
| Row 10 | Put payoff — `=MAX($B$6-O8,0)` |
| O11 | `=AVERAGE(O9:DJ9)` — the average call payoff |
| O12 | `=O11*EXP(-B8*B7)` — **discounted → the call price** |
| O13 / O14 | The same two steps for the put |

That is steps 4, 5 and 6 in three rows: **payoff, average, discount.**

The per-step formula is:

```excel
O3 = O2*EXP(($K$2-0.5*$K$3*$K$3)*SQRT(N3)+$K$3*SQRT(N3)*NORM.S.INV(RAND()))
```

`NORM.S.INV(RAND())` is how Excel draws a standard normal: take a uniform draw
on (0,1), then invert the normal CDF. That is $Z_t$.

## Does it work? Check it against Black-Scholes

This is the point of pricing a European option by simulation. Running the
procedure **as the notes describe it** — risk-neutral drift $r$, drift scaled by
$\Delta t$ — over 400,000 paths:

| Method | Call | Put |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Black-Scholes** (closed form) | **1.7054** | **6.9791** |
| **Monte Carlo**, done correctly | **1.7065** | **6.9760** |
| Difference | 0.0011 | 0.0031 |

**Monte Carlo converges on Black-Scholes.** Two completely different methods —
one an analytic formula, one 400,000 random walks — agree to about a tenth of a
cent. That agreement is the whole justification for using simulation on
contracts where no formula exists.

It is also the [Law of Large Numbers](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-method)
made visible: the average of the simulated payoffs converged to the expected
value, and the expected value is what Black-Scholes computes directly.

## Two defects in the source workbook

The workbook does **not** reproduce that agreement, and it is worth
understanding why rather than copying it.

**a) The drift is scaled by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ instead of $\Delta t$.** In the
formula above, `SQRT(N3)` multiplies **both** terms. But GBM scales the two
terms differently — drift with time, diffusion with the square root of time:

$$
\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\underbrace{\Delta t}_{\text{not } \sqrt{\Delta t}} + \sigma\underbrace{\sqrt{\Delta t}}_{\text{correct}}Z_t
$$

With $\Delta t = 1/252$: $\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.06299$ against $\Delta t =
0.003968$ — the drift term is applied **15.9× too strongly.**

**b) $\mu$ is a daily mean return, but $\sigma$ is annualised.** K2 is
`AVERAGE(F3:F253)`, the mean **daily** return (0.0009). K3 is `STDEV.S(...)
*SQRT(252)`, an **annualised** volatility (0.111). The two sit in the same
expression in different units. And per the notes' own margin annotation, the
drift should have been **$r$**, the risk-free rate, not a historical average at
all.

What it costs:

| | Call | Put |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Black-Scholes | 1.7054 | 6.9791 |
| MC as the workbook builds it | **1.2543** | **8.2804** |
| Workbook's saved values (100 paths) | 1.5993 | 7.9632 |

The method is biased **26% low on the call** and **19% high on the put**. The
saved values sit between the two because **100 paths is a very small sample** —
the sampling noise on a single recalculation is larger than the bias itself,
which is exactly why the error is easy to miss when you are looking at one draw.

Fix the two scaling issues and the sheet lands on Black-Scholes. Left as-is, it
demonstrates the mechanics correctly but does not price the option.

## Summary

- The procedure: **initialise parameters → draw $Z_t$ → step GBM → payoff →
  average → discount.**
- $\text{Option price} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]$, with a fresh
  $Z$ at **every step of every path**.
- The Excel sheet lays out **100 paths as columns, 6 daily steps as rows**, then
  does payoff / average / discount in three rows.
- **Done correctly, Monte Carlo reproduces Black-Scholes to ~0.001** — that
  agreement is the reason simulation can be trusted on exotics.
- **Price with the risk-free rate $r$, not the historical drift $\mu$** — the
  notes say so in the margin.
- The source workbook **scales drift by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ instead of $\Delta t$**
  and **mixes daily drift with annualised volatility**, which biases the call
  26% low. Understand the layout from it; do not copy the formula.

This is the contract Monte Carlo exists for. An Asian option has **no closed-form
solution**, so the
[European note's](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-european-option)
check against Black-Scholes was the last time an independent answer was
available. From here, simulation *is* the answer.

## Where it sits

| Type | Contracts |
| --- | --- |
| **Standard contract** | European, American |
| **Exotic contract** | **Asian**, **Barrier**, Digital |

And Monte Carlo handles all of them — European, Asian, Barrier.

## Definition

> Asian options are **exotic options where the payoff depends on the average
> price of the underlying asset over a specified period.**

The contrast is the whole idea:

> **Unlike a European option, which considers the price of the stock at
> expiry / maturity, an Asian option reduces the risk and uncertainty by taking
> the average price of the stock.**

## The payoffs

**European** — the terminal price only:

$$
C = \max(S_T - K,\, 0) \qquad\qquad P = \max(K - S_T,\, 0)
$$

where $S_T$ is the **stock price at maturity.**

**Asian** — the average instead:

$$
\boxed{\;C = \max(S_{avg} - K,\, 0)\;}
\qquad\qquad
\boxed{\;P = \max(K - S_{avg},\, 0)\;}
$$

where $S_{avg}$ is the **average of the stock price over all the time steps:**

$$
S_{avg} = \frac{a + b + c + d + e + f}{6}
$$

## A worked path

A path generated by GBM visits these prices over six steps to maturity:

| $t$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price | 100 | 101 | 102 | 103 | 104 | 105 |

$$
S_{avg} = \frac{100 + 101 + 102 + 103 + 104 + 105}{6} = \mathbf{102.5}
$$

A European option on this path would settle on **105**. The Asian settles on
**102.5**. Same path, different number — and the difference is the point of the
contract.

## Running the simulation

The structure from the notes: every simulation is a column, every time step a
row, all starting from the same price.

| Step | Sim 1 | Sim 2 | Sim 3 | … | Sim 10,000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0** | 49 | 49 | 49 | ⋮ | 49 |
| 1 | ▢ | ▢ | ⋮ | | ▢ |
| 2 | ▢ | ▢ | ⋮ | | ▢ |
| ⋮ | | | | | |
| 6 | ▢ | ▢ | ⋮ | | ▢ |

Each column steps forward recursively under GBM. Then each column collapses to
a single number:

$$
\text{Path 1} \rightarrow S_{avg}
\qquad
\text{Path 2} \rightarrow S_{avg}
\qquad \cdots \qquad
\text{Path 10,000} \rightarrow S_{avg}
$$

Each $S_{avg}$ becomes a payoff:

$$
C = \max(S_{avg} - K,\, 0)
$$

and from there it is the standard Monte Carlo ending — average, then discount:

$$
\text{Avg payoff} = \frac{\text{Payoff}_1 + \text{Payoff}_2 + \cdots + \text{Payoff}_{10{,}000}}{10{,}000}
$$

$$
\text{Discounted payoff} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]
$$

which is the **price of the Asian call option.**

Note where the only change lives: **one extra averaging step per path**,
inserted between the simulation and the payoff. Everything else — GBM, average
across paths, discount — is identical to the European case. That is how cheaply
Monte Carlo absorbs path dependence.

## Arithmetic versus geometric averaging

The notes are careful that "average" is ambiguous, and both forms are used.

**Arithmetic average:**

$$
S_{avg} = \frac{S_1 + S_2 + S_3 + \cdots + S_6}{6}
$$

**Geometric average:**

$$
S_{avg} = \left(S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3 \times S_4 \times S_5 \times S_6\right)^{1/6}
$$

which generalises to the **geometric mean**:

$$
\text{Geometric mean} = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i\right)^{1/n}
$$

where $\prod$ means **product** — the multiplicative counterpart of $\sum$.

And for a one-year Asian option, $n = 252$ trading days:

$$
\left(S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3 \times \cdots \times S_{252}\right)^{1/252}
$$

## The Excel sheet

Same contract, same layout as the European sheet — **100 paths across columns
O:DJ, 6 daily steps down rows 3-8** — with one row inserted:

| Cell | Label | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| K5 | Number of steps (days) | 6 |
| K6 | No. of sims | 100 |
| O3:O8 | The GBM path | steps forward from `O2` |
| **O9** | **Average stock price** | **`=AVERAGE(O3:O8)`** |
| O10 | Option payoff (call) | `=MAX(O9-$B$6,0)` |
| O11 | Option payoff (put) | `=MAX($B$6-O9,0)` |
| O12 | Average payoff (call) | `=AVERAGE(O10:DJ10)` |
| O13 | Discounted price (call) | `=O12*EXP(-B8*B7)` |
| O14 / O15 | The same two steps for the put | |

**Row 9 is the entire Asian modification.** The European sheet reads `O8`, the
terminal price; the Asian sheet reads `AVERAGE(O3:O8)`. One cell.

Note also that the average runs **O3:O8** and excludes `O2` — the starting price
$S_0$ is not part of the average, only the six simulated steps are. That is
correct: $S_0$ is known, not sampled.

:::warning This sheet inherits the European sheet's drift error

The per-step formula is the same one flagged in the
[European note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-european-option):
it multiplies the drift by `SQRT(N3)` instead of `N3`, and uses a daily mean
return where the risk-free rate belongs. The averaging logic above is sound —
the path generator underneath it is not.

:::

## Does averaging really reduce risk?

The notes claim it does. It is checkable, and it is true — with the risk-neutral
generator, 400,000 paths on the SPY contract:

| | Std dev |
| --- | --- |
| Terminal price $S_T$ | **9.41** |
| Path average $S_{avg}$ | **6.10** |

The average is **35% less variable** than the terminal price. Averaging six
draws cancels some of the noise in each — the same reason a sample mean is
steadier than a single observation.

And because an option is priced on uncertainty, less uncertainty means a cheaper
option:

| | Call | Put |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **European** | 1.7063 | 6.9642 |
| **Asian** | **0.6060** | **6.1748** |

The Asian call costs about **a third** of the European. That is not a
mispricing — it is the contract being genuinely less exposed. An Asian option
cannot be made valuable by one lucky day at expiry, and it cannot be ruined by
one unlucky one.

Which is also why they exist commercially: a corporate hedging a year of fuel
purchases cares about the *average* price it paid, not the price on one
arbitrary December afternoon. The contract matches the exposure.

## Summary

- **Asian options are exotic**: the payoff depends on the **average price** of
  the underlying over a period, not the terminal price.
- $C = \max(S_{avg} - K,\, 0)$ and $P = \max(K - S_{avg},\, 0)$.
- Worked: a path visiting 100…105 averages to **102.5**, where a European would
  settle on 105.
- The average can be **arithmetic** or **geometric**, the latter being
  $\left(\prod x_i\right)^{1/n}$ — 252 factors for a one-year contract.
- In the simulation, the only change is **one averaging step per path**, before
  the payoff. Average across paths and discount exactly as before.
- In Excel the whole modification is **`=AVERAGE(O3:O8)`** in place of reading
  the terminal cell — and $S_0$ is correctly excluded.
- **Averaging cuts variability by ~35%**, which makes the Asian call worth about
  **a third** of the equivalent European. Less uncertainty, cheaper option.

## Binomial tree

Binomial Tree is a mathematical model which is used to price derivatives
(European Option, American Option).

It basically assumes that **stock prices can move in 2 directions**, i.e.
**up direction or down direction**. This is the **assumption**.

## One step binomial model and no arbitrage argument

- Stock price → **\$20**.
- It is known that at **T = 3 months**, the stock price will be **22 or 18**.
- Interest → **value a call option**: buy the stock at **\$21** at **t = 3 months**.
- **K = \$21**.

The stock-price and option-payoff tree is:

```text
                                 Stock = $22; K = $21
                                / Option payoff = $1
             Stock price = $20
                                \ Stock = $18; K = $21
                                  Option payoff = $0
             Today                t = 3 months
```

When the stock is **\$22**, the option payoff is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Option payoff} &= \max(S_T-K,0) \\
&= \max(22-21,0) \\
&= 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

When the stock is **\$18**, the option payoff is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Option payoff} &= \max(S_T-K,0) \\
&= \max(18-21,0) \\
&= 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Consider a portfolio

Consider a portfolio consisting of:

- **Long position in Δ shares** of stock.
- **Short position in 1 call option**.

If the stock price **moves from \$20 to \$22**:

$$
\text{Total value of portfolio} = 22\Delta-1.
$$

If the stock **moves down from \$20 to \$18**, the option is **0**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Total value of portfolio} &= 18\Delta-0 \\
&= 18\Delta.
\end{aligned}
$$

**No arbitrage → value of portfolio (up) = value of portfolio (down).**

Choose Δ to make these two portfolio values equal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
22\Delta-1 &= 18\Delta \\
22\Delta-18\Delta &= 1 \\
4\Delta &= 1 \\
\Delta &= \boxed{0.25.}
\end{aligned}
$$

### A riskless portfolio

A riskless portfolio is:

- Long = **Δ shares = 0.25 share**.
- Short = **1 option = 1 option**, whose price today is **f**.

**If stock price goes up (\$20 → \$22):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Portfolio value} &= 22\Delta-1 \\
&= 22\times(0.25)-1 \\
&= 5.5-1 \\
&= \boxed{\$4.5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**If stock price goes down (\$20 → \$18):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Portfolio value} &= 18\Delta \\
&= 18\times(0.25) \\
&= \boxed{\$4.5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Regardless of whether the stock price goes up or down, the value of the
portfolio is always **\$4.5 at the end of the life of the option**.

$$
\text{Portfolio value} = \$4.5.
$$

### Present value of the portfolio

$$
\text{Present value of portfolio} = 4.5\left(e^{-rT}\right).
$$

The **risk-free rate** and time are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= 4\%, \\
T &= 3\text{ months} \\
&= \frac{3}{12} = 0.25.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the portfolio value is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Portfolio value} &= 4.5\times\left(e^{-0.04\times\frac{3}{12}}\right), \\
\text{PV}_{\text{today}} &\approx \boxed{4.455.} \qquad (1)
\end{aligned}
$$

### Option price today

The value of our stock price today is **\$20**.

**f → option price.** The margin also records the notation reminder
“John C Hull → BT → f”.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Value of portfolio today} &= 20\Delta-f \\
&= 20\times(0.25)-f \\
&= 5-f. \qquad (2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Equating (1) and (2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
5-f &\approx 4.455, \\
f &\approx \boxed{0.545.}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **option price today**. In the absence of arbitrage opportunities,
the value of the option must be **\$0.545**, to the precision used here.

## Generalization: one step binomial tree

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0 &\longrightarrow \text{stock price today}, \\
f &\longrightarrow \text{option price today}.
\end{aligned}
$$

- If the stock price moves **up**, its price is **S₀u**; the payoff from the
  option is **fᵤ**.
- If the stock price moves **down**, its price is **S₀d**; the payoff from the
  option is **f_d**.

```text
                        S₀u
                       / fᵤ
             S₀
             f         \ S₀d
             t = 0       f_d
```

The up and down factors written beside the tree are:

$$
\boxed{u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}},
\qquad d=\frac{1}{u},
\qquad \boxed{d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}}.
$$

Here **u = up factor**, **d = down factor**; σ is volatility and Δt is the
length of a time step. For this one-step tree, Δt = T.

If there is an **up movement in stock price**, the portfolio value is:

$$
\boxed{S_0u\Delta-f_u}. \qquad (1)
$$

If there is a **down movement**, the portfolio value is:

$$
\boxed{S_0d\Delta-f_d}. \qquad (2)
$$

### No arbitrage condition and the hedge ratio

Irrespective of the stock going up/down, the value of the portfolio will remain
the same. The notes label this the **no arbitrage condition**: (1) = (2).

$$
S_0u\Delta-f_u=S_0d\Delta-f_d.
$$

Rearrange:

$$
S_0u\Delta-S_0d\Delta=f_u-f_d.
$$

Factor out Δ:

$$
\Delta\left[S_0u-S_0d\right]=f_u-f_d.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\Delta=\frac{f_u-f_d}{S_0u-S_0d}}.
$$

The earlier example gives **Δ = 0.25**. The margin beside the general formula
leaves **fᵤ =** and **f_d =** as unfilled reminders and boxes **S₀u**.

*Clarification:* choosing this Δ makes the portfolio riskless in the two-state
model. No arbitrage then requires that riskless portfolio to earn the risk-free
rate; it does not require every portfolio to have equal up/down values.

### Value today and cost of setting the portfolio

The **value of portfolio (today)**, using the up outcome, is:

$$
\left(S_0u\Delta-f_u\right)e^{-rT}.
$$

The **cost of setting the portfolio** is:

$$
S_0\Delta-f.
$$

Equate these:

$$
S_0\Delta-f=\left(S_0u\Delta-f_u\right)e^{-rT}.
$$

Expand the right-hand side:

$$
S_0\Delta-f=S_0u\Delta e^{-rT}-f_u e^{-rT}.
$$

Rearrange to isolate f:

$$
S_0\Delta-S_0u\Delta e^{-rT}+f_u e^{-rT}=f.
$$

$$
f=S_0\Delta\left[1-u e^{-rT}\right]+f_u e^{-rT}.
$$

### Substitute the value of Δ in the above equation

$$
\Delta=\frac{f_u-f_d}{S_0u-S_0d}.
$$

Substitute:

$$
f=S_0\left[\frac{f_u-f_d}{S_0u-S_0d}\right]
\left[1-u e^{-rT}\right]+f_u e^{-rT}.
$$

Cancel S₀:

$$
f=\left[\frac{f_u-f_d}{u-d}\right]
\left[1-u e^{-rT}\right]+f_u e^{-rT}.
$$

Expand the numerator:

$$
f=\frac{f_u\left(1-u e^{-rT}\right)}{u-d}
-\frac{f_d\left(1-u e^{-rT}\right)}{u-d}
+f_u e^{-rT}.
$$

**Take out the negative** from the f_d term and group the fᵤ terms:

$$
f=f_u\left(\frac{1-u e^{-rT}}{u-d}+e^{-rT}\right)
+\frac{f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}.
$$

Put the fᵤ terms over a common denominator:

$$
f=f_u\left[\frac{1-u e^{-rT}+u e^{-rT}-d e^{-rT}}{u-d}\right]
+\frac{f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}.
$$

Cancel the opposite u terms:

$$
\boxed{
f=\frac{f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right)+f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}
}.
$$

## The risk-neutral probability

The **probability of up movement** is:

$$
p=\frac{e^{rT}-d}{u-d}.
$$

The **probability of down movement** is:

$$
1-p=\frac{u-e^{rT}}{u-d}.
$$

:::note Handwritten sign correction

On pages 8–9, the handwritten down-probability expression uses a negative
exponent:

$$
1-p=\frac{u-e^{-rT}}{u-d}.
$$

Page 9 also writes:

$$
(1-p)(u-d)=u-e^{-rT}.
$$

These two source expressions have a sign error. Subtracting the stated p from
1 gives **u − eʳᵀ**, with a positive exponent. The derivation below uses that
correction; the original expressions are retained here so the discrepancy is
visible.

:::

### Let's simplify the fᵤ term

Start with:

$$
f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right).
$$

Expand p:

$$
p=\frac{e^{rT}-d}{u-d}.
$$

$$
p(u-d)=e^{rT}-d.
$$

**Multiply by e⁻ʳᵀ**:

$$
p(u-d)e^{-rT}=1-d e^{-rT}.
$$

So:

$$
1-d e^{-rT}=p(u-d)e^{-rT}.
$$

**Multiply by fᵤ**:

$$
\boxed{f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right)=p(u-d)e^{-rT}f_u}.
$$

### Simplify the f_d term

Now take:

$$
f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right).
$$

Use the **down probability**, with the sign correction above:

$$
1-p=\frac{u-e^{rT}}{u-d}.
$$

$$
(1-p)(u-d)=u-e^{rT}.
$$

**Multiply the whole equation by e⁻ʳᵀ**:

$$
(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}=u e^{-rT}-1.
$$

$$
u e^{-rT}-1=(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}.
$$

**Let's multiply by f_d**:

$$
\boxed{f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)=f_d(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}}.
$$

### Substitute both terms into the option value

Return to:

$$
f=\frac{f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right)+f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}.
$$

Substitute the two expressions:

$$
f=\frac{f_u p(u-d)e^{-rT}+f_d(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}}{u-d}.
$$

Cancel u − d:

$$
f=f_u p e^{-rT}+f_d(1-p)e^{-rT}.
$$

The **one step binomial tree** formula is:

$$
\boxed{f=e^{-rT}\left[p f_u+(1-p)f_d\right]}.
$$

```text
                       p          S₀u
                        /         fᵤ
             S₀
             f          \         S₀d
                       1 − p      f_d
```

The tree labels the up branch **p** and the down branch **1 − p**; the option
values **fᵤ** and **f_d** are brought back to **f** using the formula above.

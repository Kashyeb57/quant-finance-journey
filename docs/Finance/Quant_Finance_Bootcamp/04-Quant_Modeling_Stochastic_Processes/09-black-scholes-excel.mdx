The [theory note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/black-scholes-theory)
ends with a formula. This one builds it, on the **same contract** the
[binomial tree](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/pricing-european-option-binomial-tree)
priced — so the two models can be put side by side at the end.

## The inputs

| Cell | Input | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B1 | Contract name | **SPY240709C00555000** |
| B2 | Underlying | **SPY** |
| B3 | Current date | 2024-07-02 |
| B4 | Expiry date | 2024-07-09 |
| B5 | Stock price $S$ | **549** |
| B6 | Strike price $K$ | **555** |
| B7 | Maturity $t$ | `=J54/252` → **0.0238095** |
| B8 | Risk-free rate $r$ | **0.055** |

Maturity is **6 trading days over 252** — the option has a week to live. And
$r = 5.5\%$ is the one-week Treasury rate, following the
**match-the-maturity** rule from the theory note.

The sheet also states the assumption it is working under, in its own words:
**“there are no dividends considered in the life of the option”** — the plain
Black-Scholes case. Dividends come back later as the Merton extension.

## Volatility from the price history

Same construction as the binomial note: one year of daily closes in column O,
returns in column P.

| Cell | Calculation | Formula | Value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P3 | Daily return | `=O3/O2-1` | 251 rows |
| B9 | Daily volatility | `=STDEV.S(P3:P253)` | **0.00699372** |
| B10 | Annualised volatility | `=B9*SQRT(252)` | **0.11102193** |

So $\sigma = 11.1\%$ — the **historical** volatility, which the theory note
warned is the backward-looking one. Hold that thought for the comparison at the
end.

## d₁ and d₂

| Cell | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| B11 ($d_1$) | `=(LN(B5/B6)+(B8+((B10*B10)/2))*B7)/(B10*SQRT(B7))` |
| B12 ($d_2$) | `=(LN(B5/B6)+(B8-((B10*B10)/2))*B7)/(B10*SQRT(B7))` |

$$
d_1 = -0.54949 \qquad\qquad d_2 = -0.56663
$$

Both negative, which already tells you the option is out of the money — the
stock is at 549 against a 555 strike.

:::note The sheet computes d₂ the long way, and it is not a mistake

The theory note gives $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}$. The sheet instead rebuilds
$d_2$ from scratch, flipping **+σ²/2** to **−σ²/2**. These are the same thing:

$$
d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}
= \frac{\ln\!\left(\frac{S}{K}\right) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t - \sigma^2 t}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}
= \frac{\ln\!\left(\frac{S}{K}\right) + \left(r - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}
$$

Subtracting $\sigma\sqrt{t}$ removes $\sigma^2 t$ from the numerator, which
turns $+\sigma^2/2$ into $-\sigma^2/2$. Checked numerically against the sheet:
$d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t} = -0.566625039$, and B12 = $-0.566625039$. Identical.

:::

## The prices

| Cell | Formula | Model | Market | Sheet's comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B16 | `=B5*NORM.S.DIST(B11,TRUE)-B6*EXP(-B8*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(B12,TRUE)` | **1.70542** | 0.40 | Undervalued |
| B17 | `=B6*EXP(-B8*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(-B12,TRUE)-B5*NORM.S.DIST(-B11,TRUE)` | **6.97911** | 9.95 | Overvalued |

`NORM.S.DIST(x, TRUE)` is Excel's standard normal **CDF** — the cumulative
form, which is what the `TRUE` argument selects. That is $N(\cdot)$.

Both formulas map straight onto the theory:

$$
C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rt} N(d_2)
\qquad\qquad
P = K e^{-rt} N(-d_2) - S\,N(-d_1)
$$

## Adding dividends — the Merton extension

The sheet labels this step **BS → BSM**, after **Merton**. SPY pays dividends,
and plain Black-Scholes assumes it does not, so the fix is to compute a
**dividend yield** $q$ and discount the stock leg by it.

| Quarter | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Q3 2023 | 1.58 |
| Q4 2023 | 1.91 |
| Q1 2024 | 1.60 |
| Q2 2024 | 1.76 |
| **Total** | **6.85** |

$$
q = \frac{6.85}{549} = 0.0124772
$$

Four trailing quarters of dividends over the current stock price — a **1.25%
annual yield**. The workbook cites Yahoo Finance's SPY dividend history as the
source.

The call then becomes:

$$
C_{BSM} = S e^{-qt} N(d_1) - K e^{-rt} N(d_2)
$$

| Cell | Model | Market | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B18 — Call (dividends) | **1.65791** | 0.40 | Undervalued |

The call is **worth less** once dividends are accounted for, which is the right
direction: a dividend paid before expiry leaves the stock lower, and the call
holder does not receive it.

:::danger The put-with-dividends formula in the source workbook is wrong

B19 reads:

```excel
=B6*EXP(-B8*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(-B12,TRUE)-B5*EXP(-B13*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(-B12,TRUE)
```

`-B12` appears **twice**. The second term should use `-B11`, i.e. $N(-d_1)$, not
$N(-d_2)$. The correct Merton put is:

$$
P_{BSM} = K e^{-rt} N(-d_2) - S e^{-qt} N(-d_1)
$$

The consequence, computed both ways:

| | Value |
| --- | --- |
| As written in the workbook | **3.88465** |
| Correct | **7.09467** |

An error of **more than 3.20 on a put worth 7.09** — it nearly halves the
price. The other three formulas (B16, B17, B18) reproduce exactly when checked
independently, so this is an isolated typo rather than a bad method. It is
recorded here as it stands in the source, not silently corrected, but **do not
copy that cell.**

:::

## Black-Scholes against the binomial tree

Both models have now priced the same contract, on the same inputs:

| | Call | Put |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Binomial tree**, 5 steps | 1.71306 | 6.98675 |
| **Black-Scholes** | 1.70542 | 6.97911 |
| **Difference** | 0.00765 | 0.00765 |
| **Market** | 0.40 | 9.95 |

Two things are worth noticing.

**The two models agree to under a cent**, on a five-step tree. That is the
binomial tree converging toward Black-Scholes — the tree is a discrete
approximation of the same process, and even five steps gets most of the way
there.

**The difference is identical for both.** That is not a coincidence: both
models satisfy **put-call parity**,

$$
C - P = S - K e^{-rt}
$$

which gives $-5.27369$ for the tree and $-5.27374$ for Black-Scholes. Any error
in one price shows up equally in the other, so the two gaps must match.

**And both models are far from the market.** They say the call is worth 1.71
when it trades at 0.40. As the binomial note put it, that is not free money —
it is the models telling you that **historical volatility of 11.1% is the wrong
input** for a contract six days from expiry. The market is pricing a much lower
implied volatility. This is the concrete version of the theory note's rule:
**price with implied volatility, study the past with historical.** Feed a model
backward-looking volatility and it will confidently misprice a short-dated
option.

## Summary

- The spreadsheet is the theory formula, cell by cell: inputs, $\sigma$ from
  historical returns, $d_1$ and $d_2$, then `NORM.S.DIST(·, TRUE)` for
  $N(\cdot)$.
- $\sigma$ comes from **`STDEV.S` of daily returns × √252**; maturity is
  **trading days / 252**; $r$ matches the option's tenor.
- The sheet computes $d_2$ as $(r - \sigma^2/2)$ rather than $d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}$
  — **algebraically identical**, verified numerically.
- **Dividends → the Merton extension**: $q$ = four trailing quarters / spot =
  1.25%, and the stock leg is discounted by $e^{-qt}$, which lowers the call.
- **The workbook's dividend-put formula is wrong** — it repeats $N(-d_2)$
  instead of using $N(-d_1)$, pricing 3.88 against a correct 7.09.
- Black-Scholes and a five-step binomial tree agree to **0.008**, both satisfy
  **put-call parity**, and both sit far from the market price — because the
  input volatility is historical, not implied.

Monte Carlo — Barrier Option

The Asian option was path-dependent through an average. A barrier option is path-dependent through a threshold — and the whole contract can be switched on or off by a single moment along the way.

Definition​

Barrier options are a type of exotic contract where the payoff depends on whether the underlying asset price reaches a predetermined barrier level or not — at any point during the life of the option contract.

That last clause is what makes it path-dependent. Not the price at expiry; the price at every moment.

The three types​

TypeVariants
1. Knock-inUp & In, Down & In
2. Knock-outUp & Out, Down & Out
3. Double barrierDouble knock-in, Double knock-out

And the rule that governs all six:

In⟶ActiveOut⟶Deactivate\textbf{In} \longrightarrow \text{Active} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Out} \longrightarrow \text{Deactivate}In⟶ActiveOut⟶Deactivate

Knock-in — off until touched​

These options become active (knock in) only if the underlying asset hits a certain barrier level during the life of the option contract.

If the barrier is never reached, the option expires worthless.

PathResultPayoff
Hits the barrierActivemax⁡(ST−K, 0)\max(S_T - K,\, 0)max(ST​−K,0) for a call, max⁡(K−ST, 0)\max(K - S_T,\, 0)max(K−ST​,0) for a put
Never touches itInactive0
  • Up & In — the path crosses an up barrier from below → active.
  • Down & In — the path crosses a down barrier from above → active.

The contract becomes active when the barrier is crossed (up or down), otherwise the contract will expire worthless.

Knock-out — on until touched​

The mirror image: the option deactivates when the barrier is crossed.

Crosses the barrierNever crosses
Up & OutInactiveActive
Down & OutInactiveActive

So a knock-out holder is hoping the level never trades, and a knock-in holder is hoping it does.

Double barrier — two levels at once​

There is both an up barrier and a down barrier.

Rule
Double knock-inIf either barrier is crossed, the contract becomes active; otherwise inactive, payoff = 0
Double knock-outIf either barrier is breached, the contract becomes inactive; if not breached, active

For a double knock-in, a path that wanders quietly between the two barriers is worth nothing — it needed to break out in one direction or the other. For a double knock-out, that same quiet path is the only one that pays.

Testing the barrier​

In a simulation, "did it touch?" is just a question about the range of the path:

BarrierTest
Up barriermax⁡(range)≥B\max(\text{range}) \ge Bmax(range)≥B
Down barriermin⁡(range)≤B\min(\text{range}) \le Bmin(range)≤B

The notes work a down-barrier example: with B=100B = 100B=100, a path that drifts down to 90 gives min⁡(range)=90≤100\min(\text{range}) = 90 \le 100min(range)=90≤100 → barrier crossed.

Two lines of logic, and they are the entire difference between a barrier option and a vanilla one.

Valuing it with Monte Carlo​

Seven steps, of which only step 4 and 5 are new:

#Step
1Initialise the parameters
2Generate random numbers using the standard normal distribution
3Use those numbers in the GBM equation and simulate the stock price
4Check if, during the life of the option, any barrier is crossed or not
5Apply the knock-in / knock-out rule to get the payoff
6Average the payoffs
7Discount the average

Step 5 branches:

Crossed⇒Active⇒max⁡(ST−K, 0)Not crossed⇒Inactive⇒Payoff=0\text{Crossed} \Rightarrow \text{Active} \Rightarrow \max(S_T - K,\, 0) \qquad\qquad \text{Not crossed} \Rightarrow \text{Inactive} \Rightarrow \text{Payoff} = 0Crossed⇒Active⇒max(ST​−K,0)Not crossed⇒Inactive⇒Payoff=0

and then it is the familiar ending:

Avg payoff=Payoff1+Payoff2+⋯+Payoff10,00010,000\text{Avg payoff} = \frac{\text{Payoff}_1 + \text{Payoff}_2 + \cdots + \text{Payoff}_{10{,}000}}{10{,}000}Avg payoff=10,000Payoff1​+Payoff2​+⋯+Payoff10,000​​ Price of barrier option=e−rT[Avg payoff]\text{Price of barrier option} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]Price of barrier option=e−rT[Avg payoff]

The Excel sheet​

Same 100-path, 6-step grid as before. The barrier logic is four rows:

CellLabelFormula
K7Up barrier557
O9Max value=MAX(O3:O8)
O10Barrier crossed (?)=IF(O9>$K$7,"Yes","No")
O11Check barrier (call)=IF(MAX(O3:O8)>=$K$7, MAX(O8-$B$6,0), 0)
O12Check barrier (put)=IF(MAX(O3:O8)>=$K$7, MAX($B$6-O8,0), 0)
O13Average payoff (call)=AVERAGE(O11:DJ11)
O14Discounted price (call)=O13*EXP(-B8*B7)

O11 is the whole contract in one cell: if the path's maximum reached the barrier, pay the ordinary call payoff; otherwise pay nothing. With an up barrier and payment on crossing, this sheet implements an up-and-in call.

Two small inconsistencies in the sheet

O10 — the human-readable "Yes/No" — tests O9 > K7 (strictly greater), while the payoff cells O11/O12 test >= K7. A path whose maximum lands exactly on 557 is reported as "No" but paid as though it crossed. Harmless in floating point, but the two should agree.

The path generator also carries the same drift error flagged in the European note — drift multiplied by SQRT(N3) instead of N3, and a daily mean return in place of the risk-free rate. The barrier logic above is correct; the prices it produces are not.

Checking it: in + out must equal the vanilla​

There is no closed form here either — but barrier options come with an identity that makes them self-checking:

Up-and-in+Up-and-out=Vanilla European\text{Up-and-in} + \text{Up-and-out} = \text{Vanilla European}Up-and-in+Up-and-out=Vanilla European

Every path either touches the barrier or it doesn't. The knock-in collects the payoff in the first case, the knock-out collects it in the second, and between them they collect every payoff exactly once. Simulated over 400,000 paths on the SPY contract, barrier 557:

Call
Up-and-in1.6658
Up-and-out0.0357
Sum1.7015
European1.7015

Exact. That identity is the barrier equivalent of the European note's Black-Scholes check — a way to know the simulation is right when no formula is available to compare against.

Two things it reveals about this contract:

31% of paths reach the barrier. With six trading days, an 11.1% volatility and the barrier only 8 points above spot, most paths never get there.

The up-and-out call is nearly worthless — 0.0357 against the vanilla's 1.7015. To pay anything it must finish above 555 while never having touched 557: a two-dollar window it has to land in and stay in. Almost every path that finishes in the money got there by going through the barrier first, and knocked itself out on the way.

Which is the general lesson about barriers. Moving a barrier a couple of points can take most of the value out of an option, because it is not the destination being priced — it is every step of the route.

Module 4, end to end​

That completes the module's four pricing methods on the same SPY contract:

MethodHandlesCall priceHow it works
Binomial treeEuropean, American1.7131Discrete up/down lattice, valued backwards
Black-ScholesEuropean only1.7054Closed-form formula
Monte CarloEuropean, American, exotic1.7065Simulate, payoff, discount, average
Monte Carlo, up-and-inBarrier1.6658The same, plus a path condition

The three methods that price the vanilla call agree to within a cent — a lattice, a formula and 400,000 random walks converging on the same number from three unrelated directions.

And the progression through the module is one of increasing generality bought with increasing computation. Black-Scholes is instant but only handles one contract type. The binomial tree adds early exercise. Monte Carlo is the slowest and handles everything, because it does not need a formula for the payoff — it only needs to be able to simulate the path and read the payoff off it. Averaging, thresholds, or anything else you can express as a rule on a price series.

Summary​

  • Barrier options are exotic contracts whose payoff depends on whether the price reaches a predetermined level during the life of the contract.
  • Knock-in options are inactive until the barrier is touched — if it is never reached, the option expires worthless. Knock-out options are active until the barrier is touched, then deactivate.
  • Six variants: Up/Down × In/Out, plus double knock-in and double knock-out, which trigger on either of two barriers.
  • The test is on the range of the path: max⁡(range)≥B\max(\text{range}) \ge Bmax(range)≥B for an up barrier, min⁡(range)≤B\min(\text{range}) \le Bmin(range)≤B for a down barrier.
  • Valuation adds two steps to Monte Carlo — check the barrier, apply the in/out rule — then average and discount as always.
  • The Excel sheet builds an up-and-in call in a single IF.
  • In + out = vanilla is the identity that verifies the simulation; here 1.6658+0.0357=1.70151.6658 + 0.0357 = 1.70151.6658+0.0357=1.7015, matching the European exactly.
  • A barrier a few points away can remove almost all of an option's value — the route is being priced, not just the destination.

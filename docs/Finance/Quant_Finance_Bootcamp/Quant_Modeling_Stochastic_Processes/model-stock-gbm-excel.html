Model Stock Using Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) (Excel)

Geometric Brownian motion​

GBM is a stochastic process which is widely used to model the behaviour of assets (stock price).

GBM ensures that the asset price remains positive and incorporates randomness proportional to the current price level, making it suitable for quantitative modeling.

The stock-price margin contrasts “−ve ?? ” with “0 or +ve”. GBM with a positive starting price stays strictly positive at finite times.

The notes say: we use GBM to model stock price; we don't use it to model interest rate. The interest-rate branch instead points to mean reversion process → stochastic interest-rate model. This describes the course's model choices.

The GBM equation​

Start with the generalized Wiener equation:

dXt=μ dt+σ dWt.dX_t=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.dXt​=μdt+σdWt​.

For GBM, the change is proportional to the current value of Sₜ:

dStSt=μ dt+σ dWt.\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.St​dSt​​=μdt+σdWt​.

Therefore the GBM equation, an SDE to solve for modeling stock prices, is:

dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWt.\boxed{dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t}.dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​​.
  • μ: the margin lists both risk-free rate and stock's rate of return. These correspond to risk-neutral pricing and a real-world return model, respectively; the spreadsheet below uses its specified drift.
  • σ: volatility of the stock price.
  • Quant model → assumption; quant processes → assumption.

Randomness and ordinary differential equations​

The reminder dWₜ = Z × sqrt(t) points to a standard normal process. The margin also names Poisson and Bernoulli as other distributions; they are not used for the standard-normal Z in this GBM construction.

dXt=μ dt+σ dWt.dX_t=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.dXt​=μdt+σdWt​.

The comparison labels DE / regular calculus with the handwritten schematic dXₜ = μdt + σ, and SDE / random with dXₜ = μdt + σdWₜ. The dWₜ term is boxed as the Wiener process; an unfilled distribution reminder is drawn as [ ] N [ ].

Solution of the stock-price SDE​

dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWt.dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t.dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​.

Step 1: let's assume Yₜ = ln(Sₜ)​

Instead of modeling Sₜ directly, let:

Yt=ln⁡(St).Y_t=\ln(S_t).Yt​=ln(St​).

The ln → +ve / −ve margin is the reminder to take a real logarithm of a positive price. The logarithm itself can be negative when the price is between 0 and 1.

Step 2: let's use Itô's lemma to solve​

dYt=d(ln⁡(St))=1St dSt−121St2(dSt)2.dY_t=d\bigl(\ln(S_t)\bigr) =\frac{1}{S_t}\,dS_t-\frac12\frac{1}{S_t^2}(dS_t)^2.dYt​=d(ln(St​))=St​1​dSt​−21​St2​1​(dSt​)2.

The notes label the logarithm stochastic in nature and divide the right side into 1st component and 2nd component.

Step 3: substitute the SDE into dYₜ​

dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWt.dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t.dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​.

1st component:

1St dSt=1St(μSt dt+σSt dWt)=μ dt+σ dWt.\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{S_t}\,dS_t &=\frac{1}{S_t}\left(\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t\right) \\ &=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t. \end{aligned}St​1​dSt​​=St​1​(μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​)=μdt+σdWt​.​

2nd component:

121St2(dSt)2=121St2(μSt dt+σSt dWt)2.\frac12\frac{1}{S_t^2}(dS_t)^2 =\frac12\frac{1}{S_t^2}\left(\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t\right)^2.21​St2​1​(dSt​)2=21​St2​1​(μSt​dt+σSt​dWt​)2.

The annotations say ignore the higher-order dt contributions: dt → small = 1/252, dt² → infinitely small = 1/(252)², with a zero-like oval beside the latter. In Itô differential algebra, the rules being used are (dt)² = 0, dt dWₜ = 0, and (dWₜ)² = dt; the finite number 1/(252)² is small, not literally zero.

Thus:

121St2(dSt)2=121St2[σSt dWt]2=121St2[σ2St2(dWt)2]=12σ2(dWt)2=12σ2 dt.\begin{aligned} \frac12\frac{1}{S_t^2}(dS_t)^2 &=\frac12\frac{1}{S_t^2}\left[\sigma S_t\,dW_t\right]^2 \\ &=\frac12\frac{1}{S_t^2}\left[\sigma^2 S_t^2(dW_t)^2\right] \\ &=\frac12\sigma^2(dW_t)^2 \\ &=\frac12\sigma^2\,dt. \end{aligned}21​St2​1​(dSt​)2​=21​St2​1​[σSt​dWt​]2=21​St2​1​[σ2St2​(dWt​)2]=21​σ2(dWt​)2=21​σ2dt.​

Property of Wiener process:

(dWt)2=dt.\boxed{(dW_t)^2=dt}.(dWt​)2=dt​.

Combining the terms, we get:

dYt=μ dt+σ dWt−12σ2 dt.dY_t=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t-\frac12\sigma^2\,dt.dYt​=μdt+σdWt​−21​σ2dt. dYt=(μ−12σ2)dt+σ dWt.\boxed{dY_t=\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)dt+\sigma\,dW_t}.dYt​=(μ−21​σ2)dt+σdWt​​.

Step 4: integrate the above equation from 0 to t​

∫0tdYs=∫0t(μ−12σ2)ds+∫0tσ dWs.\int_0^t dY_s =\int_0^t\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)ds +\int_0^t\sigma\,dW_s.∫0t​dYs​=∫0t​(μ−21​σ2)ds+∫0t​σdWs​.

The two right-hand integrals are labelled 1st and 2nd. The first gives the factor t − 0. For constant μ and σ, with W₀ = 0:

Yt−Y0=(μ−12σ2)(t−0)+σWt.Y_t-Y_0=\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)(t-0)+\sigma W_t.Yt​−Y0​=(μ−21​σ2)(t−0)+σWt​. Yt−Y0=(μ−12σ2)t+σWt.\boxed{Y_t-Y_0=\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t}.Yt​−Y0​=(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​​.

The margin explains integrate = sum, with small increments across 0 to t:

∫0tσ dWs=σWt.\int_0^t\sigma\,dW_s=\sigma W_t.∫0t​σdWs​=σWt​.

It then returns to:

Yt=ln⁡(St),Y0=ln⁡(S0).Y_t=\ln(S_t),\qquad Y_0=\ln(S_0).Yt​=ln(St​),Y0​=ln(S0​).
Integrated Wiener notation in the handwritten derivation

The source correctly evaluates the stochastic integral as σWₜ, but several subsequent lines on pages 21–22 write σdWₜ instead. This includes the displayed difference Yₜ − Y₀, both logarithm equations and the final exponential. After integration over 0 to t, these terms are σWₜ, as used here. For one simulation interval, the corresponding increment is σ√Δt Z.

Step 5: substitute Yₜ and Y₀ in the above equation​

ln⁡(St)−ln⁡(S0)=(μ−12σ2)t+σWt.\ln(S_t)-\ln(S_0)=\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t.ln(St​)−ln(S0​)=(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​.

Use:

ln⁡A−ln⁡B=ln⁡(AB).\ln A-\ln B=\ln\left(\frac{A}{B}\right).lnA−lnB=ln(BA​). ln⁡(StS0)=(μ−12σ2)t+σWt.\ln\left(\frac{S_t}{S_0}\right) =\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t.ln(S0​St​​)=(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​.

Step 6: taking exp on both sides​

exp⁡(ln⁡(StS0))=exp⁡[(μ−12σ2)t+σWt].\exp\left(\ln\left(\frac{S_t}{S_0}\right)\right) =\exp\left[\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t\right].exp(ln(S0​St​​))=exp[(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​]. StS0=exp⁡[(μ−12σ2)t+σWt].\frac{S_t}{S_0}=\exp\left[\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t\right].S0​St​​=exp[(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​]. St=S0exp⁡[(μ−12σ2)t+σWt].\boxed{S_t=S_0\exp\left[\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t\right]}.St​=S0​exp[(μ−21​σ2)t+σWt​]​.

The final annotation, continued by the single word on page 23, reads “equation to model stock price”.

Simulation of GBM​

The instruction image in both workbooks says: To simulate GBM in discrete time, use:

St+Δt=Ste(μ−σ2/2)Δt+σΔtZ.S_{t+\Delta t}=S_t e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}Z}.St+Δt​=St​e(μ−σ2/2)Δt+σΔt​Z.

The increment instruction in the expanded workbook is:

dWt=dt Z,dW_t=\sqrt{dt}\,Z,dWt​=dt​Z,

where Z ∼ N(0, 1) is a standard normal random variable, and dt is the time step. The accompanying bell curve illustrates that standard normal distribution.

The two Excel examples​

The course ships two workbooks. Everything below is the model they contain — the parameter block, the formula in each column, and the first computed step — so the sheet can be rebuilt from scratch rather than opened:

  • The first workbook: a Brownian Motion sheet and a GBM sheet.
  • The expanded workbook: Brownian Motion, Generalized BM, GBM, and Correlated Process (GBM). The last sheet belongs to the next Cholesky lesson.

Rebuilding it is worth more than reading it, because the whole point is that the path changes every time Excel recalculates.

Brownian Motion sheet​

The headings are Step, Random No, Delta T, Weiner Process, Increment; the expanded file also labels column F dWt.

W0 = 0. Under Stand Norm Dist, Mean = 0, Var = 1, and Sigma = SQRT(Var) = 1.

The original notes beside the table read Col B → Random Var, Wt+1 → Wt + sigma * sqrt(t) * Z, sigma = 1, Z → Rand Var, T → Time. The expanded file writes Wt+1 = Wt + sqrt(t) * Z, *Wt+1 − Wt = sqrt(t)Z, dWt = Z * sqrt(t), and Z → Standard Normal Rand Var.

The first saved process value is 0.02728321027829932 in the first workbook and −0.05507000898776514 in the expanded one. Recalculating RAND produces different simulations.

Generalized BM sheet​

The embedded definition reads: The generalized Wiener process is defined by the stochastic differential equation (SDE):

dXt=μ dt+σ dWt.dX_t=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.dXt​=μdt+σdWt​.

At t = 0, X0 = 100; u = 5, sigma = 0.2, and delta t = 1/252. Here the sheet's u is the drift μ, an absolute change per unit time, not the binomial up factor or a displayed 5% percentage.

GBM sheets​

Both use So = 100, Drift (μ) = 0.1, Volatility (σ) = 0.2, and delta t = 1/252.

The first workbook has Step, Rand, Sims 1, with:

E2 = NORM.S.INV(RAND())
F2 = B1*EXP(($B$2-0.5*$B$3^2)*$B$4+$B$3*SQRT($B$4)*E2)
F3 = F2*EXP(($B$2-0.5*$B$3^2)*$B$4+$B$3*SQRT($B$4)*E3)

The first saved simulated price is 99.6788836872274, followed by 98.3324804365328. The sheet's notes also record max(ST−K, 0) and Stock → Log Normal.

The expanded workbook has Rand 1–5 and Sims 1–5, with independent normal draws in E:I and paths in L:P. Each next row uses its own previous price:

L2 = B1*EXP(($B$2-0.5*$B$3^2)*$B$4+$B$3*SQRT($B$4)*E2)
L3 = L2*EXP(($B$2-0.5*$B$3^2)*$B$4+$B$3*SQRT($B$4)*E3)

The five first saved prices are 99.04175431504902, 101.80931475977961, 100.07065575917649, 100.60882433022049, and 101.40397372526257. The five paths run for 1000 steps. The first file's single path includes 1001 steps; its last populated step row is 1002.

The expanded sheet also writes S1 = S0exp(mu − 0.5sigmasigma)t + sigmadWt as a shorthand reminder; the actual Excel formula above puts the complete drift-plus-random term inside the exponential. It labels ABC → S1, XYZ → S2, Correlation, and BS / BT / MC as a bridge to subsequent pricing and correlated-process lessons.

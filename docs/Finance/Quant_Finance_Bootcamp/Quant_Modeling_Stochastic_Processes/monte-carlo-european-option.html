Monte Carlo — European Option

A European option already has a closed form. So why simulate one?

Because it is the test case. If Monte Carlo cannot reproduce Black-Scholes on a contract where the answer is known, there is no reason to trust it on an exotic one where it is not. That check is done at the end of this note — and it is the most useful thing in it.

The procedure​

1) Initialise all the parameters. The notes' worked values:

μ=5%σ=5.5%Δt=1252\mu = 5\% \qquad \sigma = 5.5\% \qquad \Delta t = \frac{1}{252}μ=5%σ=5.5%Δt=2521​

feeding GBM → stock price.

2) Generate a random number. ZtZ_tZt​ is a random number drawn from the standard normal distribution.

The margin note that matters

Beside this step the notes write μ → r ↳ risk-free rate.

That is the risk-neutral pricing rule, and it is not a detail. When pricing an option you do not drift the stock at its historical expected return μ\muμ — you drift it at the risk-free rate rrr. Otherwise you are pricing your own forecast rather than the contract. Keep this in mind; the workbook does not follow it.

3) Put ZtZ_tZt​ into the GBM equation.

St+Δt=Stexp⁡[(μ−12σ2)Δt+σΔt Zt]S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t \exp\left[\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_t\right]St+Δt​=St​exp[(μ−21​σ2)Δt+σΔt​Zt​]

4) Calculate the payoff at maturity:

Payoffcall=max⁡(ST−K, 0)Payoffput=max⁡(K−ST, 0)\text{Payoff}_{\text{call}} = \max(S_T - K,\, 0) \qquad\qquad \text{Payoff}_{\text{put}} = \max(K - S_T,\, 0)Payoffcall​=max(ST​−K,0)Payoffput​=max(K−ST​,0)

5) Average the payoffs.

Avg payoff=Payoff1+Payoff2+⋯+Payoff100100\text{Avg payoff} = \frac{\text{Payoff}_1 + \text{Payoff}_2 + \cdots + \text{Payoff}_{100}}{100}Avg payoff=100Payoff1​+Payoff2​+⋯+Payoff100​​

6) Discount the average payoff.

  Option price=e−rT[Avg payoff]  \boxed{\;\text{Option price} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]\;}Option price=e−rT[Avg payoff]​

Walking one path​

For every path, you will generate the price of the stock every day.

The chain is recursive — each day starts from the previous day's price:

S1=S0exp⁡[(μ−12σ2)Δt+σΔt Z1]S_1 = S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_1\right]S1​=S0​exp[(μ−21​σ2)Δt+σΔt​Z1​] S2=S1exp⁡[(μ−12σ2)Δt+σΔt Z2]S_2 = S_1 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_2\right]S2​=S1​exp[(μ−21​σ2)Δt+σΔt​Z2​] ⋮\vdots⋮ S7=S6exp⁡[(μ−12σ2)Δt+σΔt Z7]S_7 = S_6 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_7\right]S7​=S6​exp[(μ−21​σ2)Δt+σΔt​Z7​]

and S7S_7S7​ is the stock price at maturity for path 1. A fresh ZZZ is drawn at every step of every path.

Then that single terminal price produces a single payoff, and the whole path is reduced to one number. Repeat 100 times, average, discount.

The Excel implementation​

Built on SPY240709C00555000 — the same contract the binomial tree and Black-Scholes notes priced, so all three are directly comparable.

The grid itself:

RegionHolds

That is steps 4, 5 and 6 in three rows: payoff, average, discount.

The per-step formula is:

O3 = O2*EXP(($K$2-0.5*$K$3*$K$3)*SQRT(N3)+$K$3*SQRT(N3)*NORM.S.INV(RAND()))

NORM.S.INV(RAND()) is how Excel draws a standard normal: take a uniform draw on (0,1), then invert the normal CDF. That is ZtZ_tZt​.

Does it work? Check it against Black-Scholes​

This is the point of pricing a European option by simulation. Running the procedure as the notes describe it — risk-neutral drift rrr, drift scaled by Δt\Delta tΔt — over 400,000 paths:

MethodCallPut
Black-Scholes (closed form)1.70546.9791
Monte Carlo, done correctly1.70656.9760
Difference0.00110.0031

Monte Carlo converges on Black-Scholes. Two completely different methods — one an analytic formula, one 400,000 random walks — agree to about a tenth of a cent. That agreement is the whole justification for using simulation on contracts where no formula exists.

It is also the Law of Large Numbers made visible: the average of the simulated payoffs converged to the expected value, and the expected value is what Black-Scholes computes directly.

Two defects in the source workbook​

The workbook does not reproduce that agreement, and it is worth understanding why rather than copying it.

a) The drift is scaled by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ instead of Δt\Delta tΔt. In the formula above, SQRT(N3) multiplies both terms. But GBM scales the two terms differently — drift with time, diffusion with the square root of time:

(μ−12σ2)Δt⏟not Δt+σΔt⏟correctZt\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\underbrace{\Delta t}_{\text{not } \sqrt{\Delta t}} + \sigma\underbrace{\sqrt{\Delta t}}_{\text{correct}}Z_t(μ−21​σ2)not Δt​Δt​​+σcorrectΔt​​​Zt​

With Δt=1/252\Delta t = 1/252Δt=1/252: Δt=0.06299\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.06299Δt​=0.06299 against Δt=0.003968\Delta t = 0.003968Δt=0.003968 — the drift term is applied 15.9× too strongly.

b) μ\muμ is a daily mean return, but σ\sigmaσ is annualised. K2 is AVERAGE(F3:F253), the mean daily return (0.0009). K3 is STDEV.S(...) *SQRT(252), an annualised volatility (0.111). The two sit in the same expression in different units. And per the notes' own margin annotation, the drift should have been rrr, the risk-free rate, not a historical average at all.

What it costs:

CallPut
Black-Scholes1.70546.9791
MC as the workbook builds it1.25438.2804
Workbook's saved values (100 paths)1.59937.9632

The method is biased 26% low on the call and 19% high on the put. The saved values sit between the two because 100 paths is a very small sample — the sampling noise on a single recalculation is larger than the bias itself, which is exactly why the error is easy to miss when you are looking at one draw.

Fix the two scaling issues and the sheet lands on Black-Scholes. Left as-is, it demonstrates the mechanics correctly but does not price the option.

Summary​

  • The procedure: initialise parameters → draw ZtZ_tZt​ → step GBM → payoff → average → discount.
  • Option price=e−rT[Avg payoff]\text{Option price} = e^{-rT}\left[\text{Avg payoff}\right]Option price=e−rT[Avg payoff], with a fresh ZZZ at every step of every path.
  • The Excel sheet lays out 100 paths as columns, 6 daily steps as rows, then does payoff / average / discount in three rows.
  • Done correctly, Monte Carlo reproduces Black-Scholes to ~0.001 — that agreement is the reason simulation can be trusted on exotics.
  • Price with the risk-free rate rrr, not the historical drift μ\muμ — the notes say so in the margin.
  • The source workbook scales drift by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ instead of Δt\Delta tΔt and mixes daily drift with annualised volatility, which biases the call 26% low. Understand the layout from it; do not copy the formula.

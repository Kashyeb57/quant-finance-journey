The Monte Carlo Method

The Monte Carlo method is a computational technique used to model and solve financial problems that involve uncertainty or complex dynamics.

In computational finance it is the fallback — and the notes are precise about when you reach for it.

When is it used?​

It is particularly used in areas where the closed-form analytical solution is difficult to derive.

The notes make the idea of a "closed form" concrete with a quadratic equation:

ax2+bx+c=0⟹x=−b±b2−4ac2aax^2 + bx + c = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}ax2+bx+c=0⟹x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​

That second expression provides a direct formula for xxx. Put the numbers in, get the answer out — no iteration, no simulation. That is a closed form.

Options split the same way:

ContractClosed form?Method
European call / put✅ YesBlack-Scholes
Exotic contract (e.g. Asian option)❌ Not possibleMonte Carlo
C=S0N(d1)−Ke−rTN(d2)C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)C=S0​N(d1​)−Ke−rTN(d2​)

is the closed form for a European call. For an exotic contract whose closed form is not possible, you simulate instead.

Applications​

a) Option pricing. Monte Carlo is used for pricing exotic options whose payoff depends on the path of the underlying asset — not just where it ends up.

That word path is the whole distinction:

OptionPayoffDepends on
Europeanmax⁡(ST−K, 0)\max(S_T - K,\, 0)max(ST​−K,0)The terminal price only
Asianmax⁡(SˉT−K, 0)\max(\bar{S}_T - K,\, 0)max(SˉT​−K,0)The average along the way

where the average is built from prices sampled during the contract's life:

SˉT=S1+S2+S3+S44\bar{S}_T = \frac{S_1 + S_2 + S_3 + S_4}{4}SˉT​=4S1​+S2​+S3​+S4​​

Black-Scholes cannot see the middle of the path. Monte Carlo generates the whole path, so it can.

Which method covers which contract:

MethodApplies to
Black-ScholesEuropean
BinomialAmerican (and European)
Monte CarloEuropean, American, and exotic contracts

Monte Carlo is the most general of the three. That generality is what you buy with the computation.

b) Risk management. Financial institutions use the method to assess the risk of their portfolio under various scenarios — they simulate the stock prices in the portfolio, which gives Value at Risk.

VaR — how much potential loss you can have at a specific confidence interval in a given time frame.

VaR itself has three approaches: Monte Carlo, the historical method, and the variance-covariance method.

c) Portfolio optimization.

The steps​

a) Define the problem statement — e.g. determine the price of an exotic option.

b) Identify the inputs of the model — what has to be simulated:

  • simulate the interest rate (the notes name the Vasicek model)
  • simulate the stock price

c) Generate random numbers. Use a random generator to produce input for the model. These inputs should represent the uncertainty.

The stock price is modelled with geometric Brownian motion:

St=S0exp⁡[(μ−σ22)t+σWt]S_t = S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]St​=S0​exp[(μ−2σ2​)t+σWt​]

where WtW_tWt​ is the Wiener process / Brownian motion, built from a standard normal random variable ZZZ scaled by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​.

The drift term is written without its t on pages 5 and 6

The source writes this formula as S0exp⁡((μ−σ2/2)+σWt)S_0\exp\left(\left(\mu - \sigma^2/2\right) + \sigma W_t\right)S0​exp((μ−σ2/2)+σWt​) — the ttt multiplying the drift is missing — and page 14 additionally drops the square, writing μ−σ/2\mu - \sigma/2μ−σ/2. Pages 16, 17 and 18 use the correct (μ−12σ2)Δt\left(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t(μ−21​σ2)Δt form, and that is what the Excel implementation uses. Read the early pages as shorthand.

d) Run simulation trials. Input the random numbers generated in step 3 into the model to run individual trials. Each trial (run) will produce an outcome based on the random input.

Each run simulates the path of the stock price up to the expiration of the option, and then we can calculate the payoff.

At maturity, for a call:

C=max⁡(ST−K, 0)C = \max(S_T - K,\, 0)C=max(ST​−K,0)

e) Aggregate the result. Every path produced a payoff at expiry; each is discounted back to today.

f) Take the average of the discounted payoffs.

Option price=1N∑i=1Ndiscounted payoffi\text{Option price} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \text{discounted payoff}_iOption price=N1​i=1∑N​discounted payoffi​

which, written out for five paths, is exactly:

Option price=15[e−rTP1+e−rTP2+⋯+e−rTP5]\text{Option price} = \frac{1}{5}\left[e^{-rT}P_1 + e^{-rT}P_2 + \cdots + e^{-rT}P_5\right]Option price=51​[e−rTP1​+e−rTP2​+⋯+e−rTP5​]

The whole method in one picture​

Simulate many paths → take each terminal price → compute its payoff → discount every payoff back to today → average them. That is the entire method. The average of the discounted payoffs is the option price.

The Excel version of the same six steps​

#Step
1Generate random number ZZZ
2GBM equation
3Simulate the GBM equation
4At maturity, compute the call or put payoff
5Discount the payoff
6Take the average

Why averaging works at all​

The notes stop here to justify the method, and the justification is two theorems.

The class-average analogy​

Three students score 100, 90, 80. The class average is

100+90+803\frac{100 + 90 + 80}{3}3100+90+80​

Nobody finds that controversial. Monte Carlo is doing the same thing with payoffs instead of marks — the only question is whether the average of a sample tells you about the truth. Which is exactly what the two theorems answer.

1. The Central Limit Theorem​

The CLT states that under certain conditions, the average of a large number of iid random variables with finite mean and variance will approximate a normal distribution.

iid = independent, identically distributed random variables.

This is why the method's machinery is legitimate: the increments of a Wiener process are iid and normally distributed, drawn as ZΔtZ\sqrt{\Delta t}ZΔt​ with the same distribution at every step. And:

According to the CLT, as the number of random samples rises, the distribution of the sample mean will approximate the normal distribution.

Monte Carlo methods are often used to estimate the expected value of a complex random process — and the CLT is what says the estimate is well-behaved.

2. The Law of Large Numbers​

The LLN states that as the number of trials (or runs) in the random process increases, the average of the results obtained from these trials is likely to converge to the expected value of the random process.

And the line that matters:

This convergence is the reliability of the Monte Carlo method.

The CLT tells you the shape of the estimate's error. The LLN tells you the error goes away if you run enough paths. Together they are the reason a simulated average is a price and not a guess.

This is also why the notes keep writing 10,000: more paths, so that you can get higher accuracy. The more simulations you run, the closer the average sits to the true expected value of the option.

Step 2 — simulate random numbers.

Zt∼N(0,1)Z_t \sim N(0,1)Zt​∼N(0,1)

Generate a random number from the standard normal distribution for each time step and each path.

Note each time step and each path — a 10,000-path run over 252 days needs 2.52 million draws. This is why the method is computational.

Step 3 — calculate the stock price using the GBM equation.

Step 4 — payoff, discount, average → the option price.

Path dependence, which is the point​

A worked illustration of why any of this is necessary. A path visits:

Point12345
Price100102104106108

A European option looks only at the last number: max⁡(108−K, 0)\max(108 - K,\, 0)max(108−K,0).

An Asian option takes the average of the stock at certain points of the contract:

SˉT=100+102+104+106+1085=104\bar{S}_T = \frac{100 + 102 + 104 + 106 + 108}{5} = 104SˉT​=5100+102+104+106+108​=104

The notes call this a path-dependent variable, and it is precisely what a closed-form formula cannot reach. Black-Scholes prices a destination; Monte Carlo prices a journey.

Summary​

  • Monte Carlo is a computational technique for problems involving uncertainty or complex dynamics — used where no closed-form solution exists.
  • A closed form is a direct formula, like the quadratic root formula or Black-Scholes. Exotic contracts do not have one.
  • Applications: option pricing, risk management (VaR), portfolio optimisation. VaR's three methods are Monte Carlo, historical, and variance-covariance.
  • The method: define the problem → identify inputs → generate random numbers → run trials → aggregate → average the discounted payoffs.
  • Option price=1N∑e−rT×payoff\text{Option price} = \frac{1}{N}\sum e^{-rT}\times\text{payoff}Option price=N1​∑e−rT×payoff — simulate, discount, average.
  • It works because of the CLT (the sample mean is normally distributed) and the Law of Large Numbers (the sample mean converges to the expected value). That convergence is the reliability of the method.
  • More paths means more accuracy — hence 10,000.
  • It is the only one of the three methods that handles path dependence.

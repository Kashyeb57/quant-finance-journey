Black-Scholes in Excel

The theory note ends with a formula. This one builds it, on the same contract the binomial tree priced — so the two models can be put side by side at the end.

The inputs​

CellInputValue
B1Contract nameSPY240709C00555000
B2UnderlyingSPY
B3Current date2024-07-02
B4Expiry date2024-07-09
B5Stock price SSS549
B6Strike price KKK555
B7Maturity ttt=J54/252 → 0.0238095
B8Risk-free rate rrr0.055

Maturity is 6 trading days over 252 — the option has a week to live. And r=5.5%r = 5.5\%r=5.5% is the one-week Treasury rate, following the match-the-maturity rule from the theory note.

The sheet also states the assumption it is working under, in its own words: “there are no dividends considered in the life of the option” — the plain Black-Scholes case. Dividends come back later as the Merton extension.

Volatility from the price history​

Same construction as the binomial note: one year of daily closes in column O, returns in column P.

CellCalculationFormulaValue
P3Daily return=O3/O2-1251 rows
B9Daily volatility=STDEV.S(P3:P253)0.00699372
B10Annualised volatility=B9*SQRT(252)0.11102193

So σ=11.1%\sigma = 11.1\%σ=11.1% — the historical volatility, which the theory note warned is the backward-looking one. Hold that thought for the comparison at the end.

d₁ and d₂​

CellFormula
B11 (d1d_1d1​)=(LN(B5/B6)+(B8+((B10*B10)/2))*B7)/(B10*SQRT(B7))
B12 (d2d_2d2​)=(LN(B5/B6)+(B8-((B10*B10)/2))*B7)/(B10*SQRT(B7))
d1=−0.54949d2=−0.56663d_1 = -0.54949 \qquad\qquad d_2 = -0.56663d1​=−0.54949d2​=−0.56663

Both negative, which already tells you the option is out of the money — the stock is at 549 against a 555 strike.

The sheet computes d₂ the long way, and it is not a mistake

The theory note gives d2=d1−σtd_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}d2​=d1​−σt​. The sheet instead rebuilds d2d_2d2​ from scratch, flipping +σ²/2 to −σ²/2. These are the same thing:

d1−σt=ln⁡ ⁣(SK)+(r+σ22)t−σ2tσt=ln⁡ ⁣(SK)+(r−σ22)tσtd_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t} = \frac{\ln\!\left(\frac{S}{K}\right) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t - \sigma^2 t}{\sigma\sqrt{t}} = \frac{\ln\!\left(\frac{S}{K}\right) + \left(r - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}d1​−σt​=σt​ln(KS​)+(r+2σ2​)t−σ2t​=σt​ln(KS​)+(r−2σ2​)t​

Subtracting σt\sigma\sqrt{t}σt​ removes σ2t\sigma^2 tσ2t from the numerator, which turns +σ2/2+\sigma^2/2+σ2/2 into −σ2/2-\sigma^2/2−σ2/2. Checked numerically against the sheet: d1−σt=−0.566625039d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t} = -0.566625039d1​−σt​=−0.566625039, and B12 = −0.566625039-0.566625039−0.566625039. Identical.

The prices​

CellFormulaModelMarketSheet's comment
B16=B5*NORM.S.DIST(B11,TRUE)-B6*EXP(-B8*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(B12,TRUE)1.705420.40Undervalued
B17=B6*EXP(-B8*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(-B12,TRUE)-B5*NORM.S.DIST(-B11,TRUE)6.979119.95Overvalued

NORM.S.DIST(x, TRUE) is Excel's standard normal CDF — the cumulative form, which is what the TRUE argument selects. That is N(⋅)N(\cdot)N(⋅).

Both formulas map straight onto the theory:

C=S N(d1)−Ke−rtN(d2)P=Ke−rtN(−d2)−S N(−d1)C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rt} N(d_2) \qquad\qquad P = K e^{-rt} N(-d_2) - S\,N(-d_1)C=SN(d1​)−Ke−rtN(d2​)P=Ke−rtN(−d2​)−SN(−d1​)

Adding dividends — the Merton extension​

The sheet labels this step BS → BSM, after Merton. SPY pays dividends, and plain Black-Scholes assumes it does not, so the fix is to compute a dividend yield qqq and discount the stock leg by it.

QuarterAmount
Q3 20231.58
Q4 20231.91
Q1 20241.60
Q2 20241.76
Total6.85
q=6.85549=0.0124772q = \frac{6.85}{549} = 0.0124772q=5496.85​=0.0124772

Four trailing quarters of dividends over the current stock price — a 1.25% annual yield. The workbook cites Yahoo Finance's SPY dividend history as the source.

The call then becomes:

CBSM=Se−qtN(d1)−Ke−rtN(d2)C_{BSM} = S e^{-qt} N(d_1) - K e^{-rt} N(d_2)CBSM​=Se−qtN(d1​)−Ke−rtN(d2​)
CellModelMarketComment
B18 — Call (dividends)1.657910.40Undervalued

The call is worth less once dividends are accounted for, which is the right direction: a dividend paid before expiry leaves the stock lower, and the call holder does not receive it.

The put-with-dividends formula in the source workbook is wrong

B19 reads:

=B6*EXP(-B8*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(-B12,TRUE)-B5*EXP(-B13*B7)*NORM.S.DIST(-B12,TRUE)

-B12 appears twice. The second term should use -B11, i.e. N(−d1)N(-d_1)N(−d1​), not N(−d2)N(-d_2)N(−d2​). The correct Merton put is:

PBSM=Ke−rtN(−d2)−Se−qtN(−d1)P_{BSM} = K e^{-rt} N(-d_2) - S e^{-qt} N(-d_1)PBSM​=Ke−rtN(−d2​)−Se−qtN(−d1​)

The consequence, computed both ways:

Value
As written in the workbook3.88465
Correct7.09467

An error of more than 3.20 on a put worth 7.09 — it nearly halves the price. The other three formulas (B16, B17, B18) reproduce exactly when checked independently, so this is an isolated typo rather than a bad method. It is recorded here as it stands in the source, not silently corrected, but do not copy that cell.

Black-Scholes against the binomial tree​

Both models have now priced the same contract, on the same inputs:

CallPut
Binomial tree, 5 steps1.713066.98675
Black-Scholes1.705426.97911
Difference0.007650.00765
Market0.409.95

Two things are worth noticing.

The two models agree to under a cent, on a five-step tree. That is the binomial tree converging toward Black-Scholes — the tree is a discrete approximation of the same process, and even five steps gets most of the way there.

The difference is identical for both. That is not a coincidence: both models satisfy put-call parity,

C−P=S−Ke−rtC - P = S - K e^{-rt}C−P=S−Ke−rt

which gives −5.27369-5.27369−5.27369 for the tree and −5.27374-5.27374−5.27374 for Black-Scholes. Any error in one price shows up equally in the other, so the two gaps must match.

And both models are far from the market. They say the call is worth 1.71 when it trades at 0.40. As the binomial note put it, that is not free money — it is the models telling you that historical volatility of 11.1% is the wrong input for a contract six days from expiry. The market is pricing a much lower implied volatility. This is the concrete version of the theory note's rule: price with implied volatility, study the past with historical. Feed a model backward-looking volatility and it will confidently misprice a short-dated option.

Summary​

  • The spreadsheet is the theory formula, cell by cell: inputs, σ\sigmaσ from historical returns, d1d_1d1​ and d2d_2d2​, then NORM.S.DIST(·, TRUE) for N(⋅)N(\cdot)N(⋅).
  • σ\sigmaσ comes from STDEV.S of daily returns × √252; maturity is trading days / 252; rrr matches the option's tenor.
  • The sheet computes d2d_2d2​ as (r−σ2/2)(r - \sigma^2/2)(r−σ2/2) rather than d1−σtd_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}d1​−σt​ — algebraically identical, verified numerically.
  • Dividends → the Merton extension: qqq = four trailing quarters / spot = 1.25%, and the stock leg is discounted by e−qte^{-qt}e−qt, which lowers the call.
  • The workbook's dividend-put formula is wrong — it repeats N(−d2)N(-d_2)N(−d2​) instead of using N(−d1)N(-d_1)N(−d1​), pricing 3.88 against a correct 7.09.
  • Black-Scholes and a five-step binomial tree agree to 0.008, both satisfy put-call parity, and both sit far from the market price — because the input volatility is historical, not implied.

The Black-Scholes Model

The Black-Scholes model is a mathematical model for pricing European-style options.

It was developed by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in the early 1970s, and it provides a theoretical estimate of the price of a European-style option.

Why do we need it?​

Three reasons, and only the first is the obvious one.

a) Fair pricing. The model helps determine the fair market value of the option. This ensures neither the buyer nor the seller is at a disadvantage — there is a defensible number, rather than two parties guessing.

b) Regulatory compliance. Financial institutions are required to mark their positions to market and ensure they are accurately valued. Doing that at scale means using a quantitative model — and Black-Scholes is the one.

c) Hedging the portfolio. This is the link back to everything in the previous two modules:

BS model⟶price of option⟶Greeks⟶hedging\text{BS model} \longrightarrow \text{price of option} \longrightarrow \text{Greeks} \longrightarrow \text{hedging}BS model⟶price of option⟶Greeks⟶hedging

The model produces a price. Differentiate that price and you get the Greeks — delta, gamma, rho, theta, vega. Delta in particular:

Δ=change in option pricechange in the underlying\Delta = \frac{\text{change in option price}}{\text{change in the underlying}}Δ=change in the underlyingchange in option price​

where Black-Scholes supplies the numerator. And delta is what delta hedging runs on. So the chain closes: BS → price → Greeks → hedging. Without a pricing model there are no Greeks, and without Greeks there is no hedge.

Assumptions​

Six of them, and the lecturer flags this section hard — the assumptions are where the model's failures live.

(a) No dividends are paid during the life of the option.

(b) No transaction cost in buying the option.

(c) The returns of the underlying asset are normally distributed.

This is the one that breaks. The margin note names the counterexamples directly: 2007-2008 and the pandemic, where returns spiked far outside what a normal distribution allows. The response is either other models or tweak the existing model.

It is also the assumption most often misstated, so the notes draw the distinction carefully:

Returns are normally distributed — symmetric, taking negative and positive values alike. The stock price is not. A stock cannot go negative; it is floored at 0. So the price is log-normally distributed while the returns are normally distributed. Those are two different statements, and the model only claims the second.

(d) The model can be used only for European options — options exercised only at maturity. An American option can be exercised at any point in time, so Black-Scholes does not apply to it. The notes mark this with an explicit ✗.

(e) The risk-free rate and the volatility of the underlying are known and constant. In the geometric Brownian motion that underlies the model, these are the two parameters:

(f) Markets are random, so market movements cannot be predicted. Which is why Black-Scholes is a stochastic model — it does not forecast the price, it describes the distribution the price is drawn from.

Volatility​

Volatility (σ\sigmaσ) is a measure of how much the price of the underlying asset is expected to fluctuate over a year.

Higher volatility means the asset price can move more dramatically. It comes in two kinds:

LooksIs
Historical volatilitybackwardHow the price of the underlying has fluctuated in the past — computed from historical data
Implied volatilityforwardDerived from the market price of the option; represents the market's expectation of future volatility

When to use historical volatility​

a) Historical analysis — when performing any analysis based on past price movements, and for backtesting a trading strategy or risk management.

b) Comparative study — when you compare past volatility across different assets.

When to use implied volatility​

a) Forward-looking analysis — when the goal is to incorporate the market's expectation of future volatility, and for pricing options.

b) Trading and hedging — for a trader it is very important to understand market sentiment and expectation. This is the branch that feeds Black-Scholes → hedging.

The division is simple: you price with implied, you study the past with historical.

What N(d₁) and N(d₂) actually mean​

This is the part worth slowing down on, because the two look similar and are not.

N(d₁) — the probability of finishing in the money​

N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) is the probability that the stock price will be higher than the strike price at maturity — the probability the option expires ITM.

For a call:

ST>K  ⟹  In the MoneyS_T > K \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{In the Money}ST​>K⟹In the Money

And the reason a CDF appears at all:

N(⋅)⟶CDF of the standard normal⟶probability⟶ITM⟶exercisedN(\cdot) \longrightarrow \text{CDF of the standard normal} \longrightarrow \text{probability} \longrightarrow \text{ITM} \longrightarrow \text{exercised}N(⋅)⟶CDF of the standard normal⟶probability⟶ITM⟶exercised

Example. N(d1)=60%N(d_1) = 60\%N(d1​)=60%, or 0.6 — there is a 60% chance the option expires in the money, i.e. that ST>KS_T > KST​>K.

N(d₂) — the probability of making money​

N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) is the probability that the stock price will end up above the strike price, but adjusted to account for the time value of money.

It is also a CDF of the standard normal. The distinction is what it is the probability of: that you will actually make money from the option.

Example. N(d2)=55%N(d_2) = 55\%N(d2​)=55%, or 0.55 — there is a 55% chance you will actually make money from the option.

The gap between 60% and 55% is not noise — it is time and the risk-free rate. Finishing above the strike is not the same as profiting, because the strike you pay is paid later, and must be discounted:

N(d2) e−rTN(d_2)\,e^{-rT}N(d2​)e−rT

An option can expire in the money and still lose you money. N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) does not know that; N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) does.

Reading the formula term by term​

With that, the call formula stops being opaque:

C=S0 N(d1)⏟what you get  −  Ke−rTN(d2)⏟what you payC = \underbrace{S_0\,N(d_1)}_{\text{what you get}} \;-\; \underbrace{K e^{-rT} N(d_2)}_{\text{what you pay}}C=what you getS0​N(d1​)​​−what you payKe−rTN(d2​)​​

First term — S0N(d1)S_0 N(d_1)S0​N(d1​). The present value of the expected payoff if the option is exercised, weighted by the probability N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) that it finishes ITM. This is the asset you receive.

Second term — Ke−rTN(d2)K e^{-rT} N(d_2)Ke−rTN(d2​). The present value of the strike price, weighted by N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​). This is the cash you hand over, discounted back from expiry because you do not pay it today.

So the price of a call is what you expect to receive minus what you expect to pay, each weighted by the probability that you receive or pay it at all. The put formula is the same idea with the two sides swapped and the probabilities mirrored, N(−d1)N(-d_1)N(−d1​) and N(−d2)N(-d_2)N(−d2​).

Summary​

  • Black-Scholes prices European options only — Black and Scholes, early 1970s — and exists for fair pricing, regulatory mark-to-market, and hedging.
  • It is the head of the chain BS → price → Greeks → hedging: the model supplies the option price that every Greek is a derivative of.
  • Six assumptions: no dividends, no transaction costs, normally distributed returns, European exercise only, known and constant rrr and σ\sigmaσ, and random markets.
  • Returns are normal; the stock price is log-normal — because a price cannot go below zero.
  • C=S0N(d1)−Ke−rTN(d2)C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2)C=S0​N(d1​)−Ke−rTN(d2​), with d2=d1−σtd_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{t}d2​=d1​−σt​ and N(⋅)N(\cdot)N(⋅) the standard normal CDF.
  • N(d1)N(d_1)N(d1​) is the probability of expiring in the money; N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) is the probability of making money. The difference between them is the time value of money.
  • Match rrr to the option's maturity, and use implied volatility to price, historical volatility to study the past.

One Step Binomial Tree & No Arbitrage Argument (Theory)

Binomial tree​

Binomial Tree is a mathematical model which is used to price derivatives (European Option, American Option).

It basically assumes that stock prices can move in 2 directions, i.e. up direction or down direction. This is the assumption.

One step binomial model and no arbitrage argument​

  • Stock price → $20.
  • It is known that at T = 3 months, the stock price will be 22 or 18.
  • Interest → value a call option: buy the stock at $21 at t = 3 months.
  • K = $21.

The stock-price and option-payoff tree is:

                                 Stock = $22; K = $21
                                / Option payoff = $1
             Stock price = $20
                                \ Stock = $18; K = $21
                                  Option payoff = $0
             Today                t = 3 months

When the stock is $22, the option payoff is:

Option payoff=max⁡(ST−K,0)=max⁡(22−21,0)=1.\begin{aligned} \text{Option payoff} &= \max(S_T-K,0) \\ &= \max(22-21,0) \\ &= 1. \end{aligned}Option payoff​=max(ST​−K,0)=max(22−21,0)=1.​

When the stock is $18, the option payoff is:

Option payoff=max⁡(ST−K,0)=max⁡(18−21,0)=0.\begin{aligned} \text{Option payoff} &= \max(S_T-K,0) \\ &= \max(18-21,0) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned}Option payoff​=max(ST​−K,0)=max(18−21,0)=0.​

Consider a portfolio​

Consider a portfolio consisting of:

  • Long position in Δ shares of stock.
  • Short position in 1 call option.

If the stock price moves from $20 to $22:

Total value of portfolio=22Δ−1.\text{Total value of portfolio} = 22\Delta-1.Total value of portfolio=22Δ−1.

If the stock moves down from $20 to $18, the option is 0:

Total value of portfolio=18Δ−0=18Δ.\begin{aligned} \text{Total value of portfolio} &= 18\Delta-0 \\ &= 18\Delta. \end{aligned}Total value of portfolio​=18Δ−0=18Δ.​

No arbitrage → value of portfolio (up) = value of portfolio (down).

Choose Δ to make these two portfolio values equal:

22Δ−1=18Δ22Δ−18Δ=14Δ=1Δ=0.25.\begin{aligned} 22\Delta-1 &= 18\Delta \\ 22\Delta-18\Delta &= 1 \\ 4\Delta &= 1 \\ \Delta &= \boxed{0.25.} \end{aligned}22Δ−122Δ−18Δ4ΔΔ​=18Δ=1=1=0.25.​​

A riskless portfolio​

A riskless portfolio is:

  • Long = Δ shares = 0.25 share.
  • Short = 1 option = 1 option, whose price today is f.

If stock price goes up ($20 → $22):

Portfolio value=22Δ−1=22×(0.25)−1=5.5−1=$4.5.\begin{aligned} \text{Portfolio value} &= 22\Delta-1 \\ &= 22\times(0.25)-1 \\ &= 5.5-1 \\ &= \boxed{\$4.5}. \end{aligned}Portfolio value​=22Δ−1=22×(0.25)−1=5.5−1=$4.5​.​

If stock price goes down ($20 → $18):

Portfolio value=18Δ=18×(0.25)=$4.5.\begin{aligned} \text{Portfolio value} &= 18\Delta \\ &= 18\times(0.25) \\ &= \boxed{\$4.5}. \end{aligned}Portfolio value​=18Δ=18×(0.25)=$4.5​.​

Regardless of whether the stock price goes up or down, the value of the portfolio is always $4.5 at the end of the life of the option.

Portfolio value=$4.5.\text{Portfolio value} = \$4.5.Portfolio value=$4.5.

Present value of the portfolio​

Present value of portfolio=4.5(e−rT).\text{Present value of portfolio} = 4.5\left(e^{-rT}\right).Present value of portfolio=4.5(e−rT).

The risk-free rate and time are:

r=4%,T=3 months=312=0.25.\begin{aligned} r &= 4\%, \\ T &= 3\text{ months} \\ &= \frac{3}{12} = 0.25. \end{aligned}rT​=4%,=3 months=123​=0.25.​

So the portfolio value is:

Portfolio value=4.5×(e−0.04×312),PVtoday≈4.455.(1)\begin{aligned} \text{Portfolio value} &= 4.5\times\left(e^{-0.04\times\frac{3}{12}}\right), \\ \text{PV}_{\text{today}} &\approx \boxed{4.455.} \qquad (1) \end{aligned}Portfolio valuePVtoday​​=4.5×(e−0.04×123​),≈4.455.​(1)​

Option price today​

The value of our stock price today is $20.

f → option price. The margin also records the notation reminder “John C Hull → BT → f”.

Value of portfolio today=20Δ−f=20×(0.25)−f=5−f.(2)\begin{aligned} \text{Value of portfolio today} &= 20\Delta-f \\ &= 20\times(0.25)-f \\ &= 5-f. \qquad (2) \end{aligned}Value of portfolio today​=20Δ−f=20×(0.25)−f=5−f.(2)​

Equating (1) and (2):

5−f≈4.455,f≈0.545.\begin{aligned} 5-f &\approx 4.455, \\ f &\approx \boxed{0.545.} \end{aligned}5−ff​≈4.455,≈0.545.​​

This is the option price today. In the absence of arbitrage opportunities, the value of the option must be $0.545, to the precision used here.

Generalization: one step binomial tree​

S0⟶stock price today,f⟶option price today.\begin{aligned} S_0 &\longrightarrow \text{stock price today}, \\ f &\longrightarrow \text{option price today}. \end{aligned}S0​f​⟶stock price today,⟶option price today.​
  • If the stock price moves up, its price is S₀u; the payoff from the option is fᵤ.
  • If the stock price moves down, its price is S₀d; the payoff from the option is f_d.
                        S₀u
                       / fᵤ
             S₀
             f         \ S₀d
             t = 0       f_d

The up and down factors written beside the tree are:

u=eσΔt,d=1u,d=e−σΔt.\boxed{u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}}, \qquad d=\frac{1}{u}, \qquad \boxed{d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}}.u=eσΔt​​,d=u1​,d=e−σΔt​​.

Here u = up factor, d = down factor; σ is volatility and Δt is the length of a time step. For this one-step tree, Δt = T.

If there is an up movement in stock price, the portfolio value is:

S0uΔ−fu.(1)\boxed{S_0u\Delta-f_u}. \qquad (1)S0​uΔ−fu​​.(1)

If there is a down movement, the portfolio value is:

S0dΔ−fd.(2)\boxed{S_0d\Delta-f_d}. \qquad (2)S0​dΔ−fd​​.(2)

No arbitrage condition and the hedge ratio​

Irrespective of the stock going up/down, the value of the portfolio will remain the same. The notes label this the no arbitrage condition: (1) = (2).

S0uΔ−fu=S0dΔ−fd.S_0u\Delta-f_u=S_0d\Delta-f_d.S0​uΔ−fu​=S0​dΔ−fd​.

Rearrange:

S0uΔ−S0dΔ=fu−fd.S_0u\Delta-S_0d\Delta=f_u-f_d.S0​uΔ−S0​dΔ=fu​−fd​.

Factor out Δ:

Δ[S0u−S0d]=fu−fd.\Delta\left[S_0u-S_0d\right]=f_u-f_d.Δ[S0​u−S0​d]=fu​−fd​.

Therefore:

Δ=fu−fdS0u−S0d.\boxed{\Delta=\frac{f_u-f_d}{S_0u-S_0d}}.Δ=S0​u−S0​dfu​−fd​​​.

The earlier example gives Δ = 0.25. The margin beside the general formula leaves fᵤ = and f_d = as unfilled reminders and boxes S₀u.

Clarification: choosing this Δ makes the portfolio riskless in the two-state model. No arbitrage then requires that riskless portfolio to earn the risk-free rate; it does not require every portfolio to have equal up/down values.

Value today and cost of setting the portfolio​

The value of portfolio (today), using the up outcome, is:

(S0uΔ−fu)e−rT.\left(S_0u\Delta-f_u\right)e^{-rT}.(S0​uΔ−fu​)e−rT.

The cost of setting the portfolio is:

S0Δ−f.S_0\Delta-f.S0​Δ−f.

Equate these:

S0Δ−f=(S0uΔ−fu)e−rT.S_0\Delta-f=\left(S_0u\Delta-f_u\right)e^{-rT}.S0​Δ−f=(S0​uΔ−fu​)e−rT.

Expand the right-hand side:

S0Δ−f=S0uΔe−rT−fue−rT.S_0\Delta-f=S_0u\Delta e^{-rT}-f_u e^{-rT}.S0​Δ−f=S0​uΔe−rT−fu​e−rT.

Rearrange to isolate f:

S0Δ−S0uΔe−rT+fue−rT=f.S_0\Delta-S_0u\Delta e^{-rT}+f_u e^{-rT}=f.S0​Δ−S0​uΔe−rT+fu​e−rT=f. f=S0Δ[1−ue−rT]+fue−rT.f=S_0\Delta\left[1-u e^{-rT}\right]+f_u e^{-rT}.f=S0​Δ[1−ue−rT]+fu​e−rT.

Substitute the value of Δ in the above equation​

Δ=fu−fdS0u−S0d.\Delta=\frac{f_u-f_d}{S_0u-S_0d}.Δ=S0​u−S0​dfu​−fd​​.

Substitute:

f=S0[fu−fdS0u−S0d][1−ue−rT]+fue−rT.f=S_0\left[\frac{f_u-f_d}{S_0u-S_0d}\right] \left[1-u e^{-rT}\right]+f_u e^{-rT}.f=S0​[S0​u−S0​dfu​−fd​​][1−ue−rT]+fu​e−rT.

Cancel S₀:

f=[fu−fdu−d][1−ue−rT]+fue−rT.f=\left[\frac{f_u-f_d}{u-d}\right] \left[1-u e^{-rT}\right]+f_u e^{-rT}.f=[u−dfu​−fd​​][1−ue−rT]+fu​e−rT.

Expand the numerator:

f=fu(1−ue−rT)u−d−fd(1−ue−rT)u−d+fue−rT.f=\frac{f_u\left(1-u e^{-rT}\right)}{u-d} -\frac{f_d\left(1-u e^{-rT}\right)}{u-d} +f_u e^{-rT}.f=u−dfu​(1−ue−rT)​−u−dfd​(1−ue−rT)​+fu​e−rT.

Take out the negative from the f_d term and group the fᵤ terms:

f=fu(1−ue−rTu−d+e−rT)+fd(ue−rT−1)u−d.f=f_u\left(\frac{1-u e^{-rT}}{u-d}+e^{-rT}\right) +\frac{f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}.f=fu​(u−d1−ue−rT​+e−rT)+u−dfd​(ue−rT−1)​.

Put the fᵤ terms over a common denominator:

f=fu[1−ue−rT+ue−rT−de−rTu−d]+fd(ue−rT−1)u−d.f=f_u\left[\frac{1-u e^{-rT}+u e^{-rT}-d e^{-rT}}{u-d}\right] +\frac{f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}.f=fu​[u−d1−ue−rT+ue−rT−de−rT​]+u−dfd​(ue−rT−1)​.

Cancel the opposite u terms:

f=fu(1−de−rT)+fd(ue−rT−1)u−d.\boxed{ f=\frac{f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right)+f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d} }.f=u−dfu​(1−de−rT)+fd​(ue−rT−1)​​.

The risk-neutral probability​

The probability of up movement is:

p=erT−du−d.p=\frac{e^{rT}-d}{u-d}.p=u−derT−d​.

The probability of down movement is:

1−p=u−erTu−d.1-p=\frac{u-e^{rT}}{u-d}.1−p=u−du−erT​.
Handwritten sign correction

On pages 8–9, the handwritten down-probability expression uses a negative exponent:

1−p=u−e−rTu−d.1-p=\frac{u-e^{-rT}}{u-d}.1−p=u−du−e−rT​.

Page 9 also writes:

(1−p)(u−d)=u−e−rT.(1-p)(u-d)=u-e^{-rT}.(1−p)(u−d)=u−e−rT.

These two source expressions have a sign error. Subtracting the stated p from 1 gives u − eʳᵀ, with a positive exponent. The derivation below uses that correction; the original expressions are retained here so the discrepancy is visible.

Let's simplify the fᵤ term​

Start with:

fu(1−de−rT).f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right).fu​(1−de−rT).

Expand p:

p=erT−du−d.p=\frac{e^{rT}-d}{u-d}.p=u−derT−d​. p(u−d)=erT−d.p(u-d)=e^{rT}-d.p(u−d)=erT−d.

Multiply by e⁻ʳᵀ:

p(u−d)e−rT=1−de−rT.p(u-d)e^{-rT}=1-d e^{-rT}.p(u−d)e−rT=1−de−rT.

So:

1−de−rT=p(u−d)e−rT.1-d e^{-rT}=p(u-d)e^{-rT}.1−de−rT=p(u−d)e−rT.

Multiply by fᵤ:

fu(1−de−rT)=p(u−d)e−rTfu.\boxed{f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right)=p(u-d)e^{-rT}f_u}.fu​(1−de−rT)=p(u−d)e−rTfu​​.

Simplify the f_d term​

Now take:

fd(ue−rT−1).f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right).fd​(ue−rT−1).

Use the down probability, with the sign correction above:

1−p=u−erTu−d.1-p=\frac{u-e^{rT}}{u-d}.1−p=u−du−erT​. (1−p)(u−d)=u−erT.(1-p)(u-d)=u-e^{rT}.(1−p)(u−d)=u−erT.

Multiply the whole equation by e⁻ʳᵀ:

(1−p)(u−d)e−rT=ue−rT−1.(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}=u e^{-rT}-1.(1−p)(u−d)e−rT=ue−rT−1. ue−rT−1=(1−p)(u−d)e−rT.u e^{-rT}-1=(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}.ue−rT−1=(1−p)(u−d)e−rT.

Let's multiply by f_d:

fd(ue−rT−1)=fd(1−p)(u−d)e−rT.\boxed{f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)=f_d(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}}.fd​(ue−rT−1)=fd​(1−p)(u−d)e−rT​.

Substitute both terms into the option value​

Return to:

f=fu(1−de−rT)+fd(ue−rT−1)u−d.f=\frac{f_u\left(1-d e^{-rT}\right)+f_d\left(u e^{-rT}-1\right)}{u-d}.f=u−dfu​(1−de−rT)+fd​(ue−rT−1)​.

Substitute the two expressions:

f=fup(u−d)e−rT+fd(1−p)(u−d)e−rTu−d.f=\frac{f_u p(u-d)e^{-rT}+f_d(1-p)(u-d)e^{-rT}}{u-d}.f=u−dfu​p(u−d)e−rT+fd​(1−p)(u−d)e−rT​.

Cancel u − d:

f=fupe−rT+fd(1−p)e−rT.f=f_u p e^{-rT}+f_d(1-p)e^{-rT}.f=fu​pe−rT+fd​(1−p)e−rT.

The one step binomial tree formula is:

f=e−rT[pfu+(1−p)fd].\boxed{f=e^{-rT}\left[p f_u+(1-p)f_d\right]}.f=e−rT[pfu​+(1−p)fd​]​. 
                       p          S₀u
                        /         fᵤ
             S₀
             f          \         S₀d
                       1 − p      f_d

The tree labels the up branch p and the down branch 1 − p; the option values fᵤ and f_d are brought back to f using the formula above.

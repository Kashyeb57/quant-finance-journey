VaR — the Monte Carlo Method

The third and most general method. Where variance-covariance assumes a shape and the historical method reuses the past, Monte Carlo generates the future — thousands of times.

Why reach for it​

The deciding factor is the kind of instrument in the portfolio:

Instrument typeExamplesMethod
LinearForwards, futuresSimpler models suffice
Non-linearOptionsMonte Carlo

The Monte Carlo method for calculating VaR involves simulation techniques to model the stock price (GBM), providing a probabilistic assessment of the prices that the stock can take.

The Monte Carlo method is useful when dealing with complex portfolios (e.g. options) for which the market risks are not captured by simpler models.

The engine — geometric Brownian motion​

St=S0exp⁡[(μ−σ22)t+σWt]S_t = S_0 \exp\left[\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]St​=S0​exp[(μ−2σ2​)t+σWt​]

the same equation built in Module 4:

TermMeaning
μ\muμ (drift)Average return of the asset over time
σ\sigmaσ (volatility)Measures the variation or dispersion of the asset's return
WtW_tWt​ (Wiener process / Brownian motion)Random variable generated from a standard normal distribution

Why generate random variables at all?​

The random variable represents the random fluctuation in the stock price.

And those fluctuations are driven by market sentiment, news and other unpredictable factors — the same list the Vasicek note gave for interest rates. Unpredictable by definition, so modelled as noise rather than forecast.

The part that is specific to portfolios: correlation​

Simulating one stock is straightforward. Simulating three at once is where this method earns its complexity.

It is very important to model the correlation between different assets (stocks).

Ignore correlation between assets → inaccurate result.

A standard normal generator gives uncorrelated draws. If you feed three independent random numbers into three GBMs, you get three stocks that wander independently — which is not the portfolio you own. JPM, MS and BAC move together, and a simulation that pretends otherwise will understate the chance that they all fall on the same day.

How: Cholesky decomposition​

Using Cholesky decomposition.

Σ=L⋅LT\Sigma = L \cdot L^{T}Σ=L⋅LT

where Σ\SigmaΣ is the correlation matrix and LLL the Cholesky decomposition matrix — a lower triangular matrix.

For two stocks:

Σ=[1ρρ1]L=[L110L21L22]\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} L_{11} & 0 \\ L_{21} & L_{22} \end{bmatrix}Σ=[1ρ​ρ1​]L=[L11​L21​​0L22​​]

Multiplying out LLTL L^TLLT and matching entries gives three equations:

L112=1  ⟹  L11=1L_{11}^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; L_{11} = 1L112​=1⟹L11​=1 L11⋅L21=ρ  ⟹  L21=ρL_{11} \cdot L_{21} = \rho \;\Longrightarrow\; L_{21} = \rhoL11​⋅L21​=ρ⟹L21​=ρ L212+L222=1  ⟹  ρ2+L222=1  ⟹  L22=1−ρ2L_{21}^2 + L_{22}^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho^2 + L_{22}^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; L_{22} = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}L212​+L222​=1⟹ρ2+L222​=1⟹L22​=1−ρ2​   L=[10ρ1−ρ2]  \boxed{\;L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1-\rho^2} \end{bmatrix}\;}L=[1ρ​01−ρ2​​]​

This is the identical derivation done in Module 4's Cholesky note, now put to a different use — there it correlated two simulated stocks, here it correlates the risk factors inside a VaR calculation.

The pipeline​

stock price→GBM→uncorrelated random numbers⏟standard normal→× L→correlated random variables⏟feed into GBM\text{stock price} \rightarrow \text{GBM} \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{uncorrelated random numbers}}_{\text{standard normal}} \rightarrow \times\, L \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{correlated random variables}}_{\text{feed into GBM}}stock price→GBM→standard normaluncorrelated random numbers​​→×L→feed into GBMcorrelated random variables​​

Generate independent noise, multiply by LLL, and the output carries the correlation structure measured from the historical data.

Advantages​

a) Flexibility. The method can model complex portfolios with multiple risk factors and capture the interaction between them. Nothing in the procedure cares whether the payoff is linear — you simulate the path and read the value off it.

b) Tail risk. It is effective in estimating the risk of extreme losses, especially when the portfolio includes non-linear instruments. Unlike the historical method, it is not limited to losses that have already happened; it can generate a worse day than any in the sample.

Limitations​

a) Computationally intensive. It requires significant computational resources, especially as the number of simulations and portfolio complexity increase. Going from 1,000 to 100,000 simulations, or from 3 stocks to 8, is a cost and a time factor — and unlike the other two methods, the answer is not instant.

b) Model risk.

Assuming something in a model = a chance of failing = model risk.

The Monte Carlo method is heavily dependent on the assumption of the probability distribution and the correlation between the risk factors. Incorrect assumptions can lead to misleading results.

This is the honest cost of the flexibility. The historical method assumes only that the past repeats. Monte Carlo assumes a distribution, a correlation structure, and a process — three places to be wrong instead of one.

The full procedure​

Step
aDownload the data (JPM, MS, BOA from Yahoo Finance)
bCalculate the returns → gives the mean and standard deviation
cGenerate random variables from the standard normal distribution — these are uncorrelated
dConvert uncorrelated into correlated by multiplying the Cholesky matrix with the uncorrelated random variables
eSimulate the stock price with GBM
fCalculate the returns of each simulated path, then the portfolio return w1r1+w2r2w_1 r_1 + w_2 r_2w1​r1​+w2​r2​
gArrange the returns in ascending order → take the VaR return by the percentile approach (99%, 95%, 90%)
hVaR return × portfolio value = VaR

Note where this lands: steps g and h are exactly the historical method. Once the simulation has produced its cloud of returns, they are sorted and read off in the same way. The difference is only in where the returns came from — generated rather than observed.

Which is the neat summary of all three methods:

MethodSource of the return distribution
HistoricalThe returns that actually happened
Variance-covarianceA normal curve fitted to mean and variance
Monte CarloReturns generated from a simulated process

Three different answers to the same question, and the Excel note shows how far apart they land.

Summary​

  • Monte Carlo VaR simulates the stock price with GBM to get a probabilistic assessment of the prices the stock can take, then reads VaR off the simulated distribution.
  • It is the method for non-linear instruments (options), where simpler models do not capture the risk.
  • The random draws must be correlated. Independent noise would produce a portfolio whose stocks never fall together — ignoring correlation gives an inaccurate result.
  • Cholesky supplies the correlation: Σ=LLT\Sigma = LL^TΣ=LLT, giving L=[10ρ1−ρ2]L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\end{bmatrix}L=[1ρ​01−ρ2​​] for two assets; multiply the uncorrelated draws by LLL.
  • Advantages: flexibility with multiple interacting risk factors, and real tail-risk estimation — it can generate losses worse than any observed.
  • Limitations: computationally intensive, and model risk — it assumes a distribution and a correlation structure and a process.
  • The last two steps are identical to the historical method — sort and multiply. Only the origin of the returns differs.

Variance-Covariance VaR in Excel

The theory note argued that summing weighted volatilities is wrong. This sheet computes it both ways, side by side — so the size of the error is measurable rather than asserted.

The setup​

Same portfolio as the historical sheet:

JPMMSBAC
Weight0.30.40.3
Stock value$300,000$400,000$300,000

on a $1,000,000 investment, over 251 daily returns.

Prices are pulled from dedicated per-stock sheets (=JPM!F2, =MS!F2, =BAC!F2), returns are =(B7/B6)-1, and the portfolio return is again =SUMPRODUCT(V7:X7,$AG$7:$AI$7).

Step 3 — the portfolio mean, computed twice​

The sheet works it out two different ways as a check:

RouteFormulaResult
Average the portfolio returns=AVERAGE(AR7:AR256)0.00073708
Weight the individual means=SUMPRODUCT(AV32:AX32, AV33:AX33)0.00073708

with the individual mean returns being JPM 0.00155329, MS 0.00062937, BAC 0.0000645.

They agree exactly, and they must: averaging and weighting are both linear operations, so their order does not matter.

1T∑t(∑iwiri,t)=∑iwi(1T∑tri,t)\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \left(\sum_i w_i r_{i,t}\right) = \sum_i w_i \left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t r_{i,t}\right)T1​t∑​(i∑​wi​ri,t​)=i∑​wi​(T1​t∑​ri,t​)

Step 4 — the two portfolio volatilities​

The wrong one, computed explicitly​

JPMMSBAC
Volatility =STDEV.S(...)0.016255970.017164900.01821889
BJ8 = SUMPRODUCT(BJ6:BL6, BJ7:BL7)   →  0.01720842

The sheet labels this "Portfolio Volatility". It is the weighted sum of the individual volatilities — the formula the theory note marked ✗.

The right one, via the covariance matrix​

The diagonal uses VAR.P, the off-diagonal entries are ρσXσY\rho\sigma_X\sigma_YρσX​σY​ (the sheet computes CORREL(JPM, MS) = 0.66461117 to show where they come from):

Σ=[0.000263200.000184440.000240800.000184440.000293460.000233740.000240800.000233740.00033061]\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 0.00026320 & 0.00018444 & 0.00024080 \\ 0.00018444 & 0.00029346 & 0.00023374 \\ 0.00024080 & 0.00023374 & 0.00033061 \end{bmatrix}Σ=​0.000263200.000184440.00024080​0.000184440.000293460.00023374​0.000240800.000233740.00033061​​

and then BJ24, an array formula, computes wTΣw\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}wTΣw​:

wTΣw=0.0002441037⟹σp=0.0002441037=0.01562379w^T \Sigma w = 0.0002441037 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0002441037} = \mathbf{0.01562379}wTΣw=0.0002441037⟹σp​=0.0002441037​=0.01562379

Recomputed independently from the matrix above, that gives 0.01562382 — matching the sheet to seven decimals.

What the difference is worth​

Portfolio volatility
Naive (weighted sum)0.01720842
Correct (covariance matrix)0.01562379
Naive overstates by10.14%

Carried through to the VaR figures on $1M:

CIVaR (correct)VaR (naive)Overstated by
99%−$35,609.28−$39,295.69$3,686
95%−$24,961.76−$27,568.25$2,606
90%−$19,285.60−$21,316.40$2,031

That $3,686 is the diversification benefit. It is the amount of risk that does not exist because JPM, MS and BAC are correlated at about 0.66 rather than 1.0 — and it is exactly what the naive formula throws away. For a bank sizing capital against this number, ignoring the covariance matrix means holding 10% more capital than the portfolio warrants.

Note also that all three banks are highly correlated (0.66 between JPM and MS) because they are the same sector. A genuinely diversified portfolio would show a much larger gap between the two volatilities.

NORM.S.INV(0.01) = −2.326 is the standard normal quantile — the point with 1% of the distribution below it. This is the payoff of the normality assumption: the tail is a lookup, not a sort.

The sheet mixes sample and population estimators

The individual volatilities in BJ7:BL7 use STDEV.S — the sample standard deviation, dividing by n−1n-1n−1. The covariance matrix diagonal uses VAR.P — the population variance, dividing by nnn.

JPM volatilityValue
STDEV.S (sample)0.01625597
SQRT(VAR.P) (population)0.01622356
Ratio1.001998

That ratio is exactly n/(n−1)=251/250=1.001998\sqrt{n/(n-1)} = \sqrt{251/250} = 1.001998n/(n−1)​=251/250​=1.001998, confirming the two estimators are the only difference.

At 0.2% it changes nothing material here, and it only affects the naive volatility (which is not used for the final answer anyway). But the two blocks are not measuring quite the same thing, and with a shorter sample the gap would widen — with 20 observations it would be 2.6%.

Summary​

  • Same JPM/MS/BAC portfolio: weights 0.3 / 0.4 / 0.3, $1M, 251 returns.
  • The portfolio mean (0.00073708) computes identically whether you average the portfolio returns or weight the individual means — averaging and weighting commute.
  • The sheet computes both portfolio volatilities: the naive weighted sum (0.01720842) and the covariance-matrix answer (0.01562379), verified independently as wTΣw\sqrt{w^T\Sigma w}wTΣw​.
  • The naive method overstates volatility by 10.14% and 99% VaR by $3,686 — that gap is the diversification benefit, and it exists because the three banks correlate at ~0.66 rather than 1.
  • VaR =(μp+σpZ)×= (\mu_p + \sigma_p Z) \times=(μp​+σp​Z)× portfolio value, with ZZZ from NORM.S.INV(alpha): −$35,609.28 at 99%.
  • Minor: the sheet uses STDEV.S in one block and VAR.P in another — a 0.2% estimator mismatch at this sample size.

VaR — the Variance-Covariance Method

Where the historical method assumed nothing about the shape of returns, this one assumes everything.

Definition​

The variance-covariance method, also known as the parametric method, is one of the most commonly used approaches to calculate VaR.

Parametric means assuming a certain shape:

We assume that the returns are normally distributed — the bell-shaped curve.

Why assume normality?​

The variance-covariance method assumes the asset returns are normally distributed because it simplifies the calculation of VaR, as the distribution is characterised by mean and variance.

That is the entire trade. A normal distribution is completely described by two numbers — its mean and its standard deviation. Once you accept that shape, you do not need 251 sorted observations; you need μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ, and every quantile follows from them.

Three stocks — JPM, MS, BOA — pulled from Yahoo Finance, using adjusted close.

Step 2 — calculate the returns​

Return=StSt−1−1\text{Return} = \frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}} - 1Return=St−1​St​​−1
Why returns and not prices

The notes make the case with two examples:

The same dollar move is a very different event depending on the price level. Returns normalise that away, which is what makes stocks comparable and combinable in a portfolio.

Step 3 — compute the mean of the portfolio​

  Portfolio mean=w1r1+w2r2+w3r3  \boxed{\;\text{Portfolio mean} = w_1 r_1 + w_2 r_2 + w_3 r_3\;}Portfolio mean=w1​r1​+w2​r2​+w3​r3​​

where w1,w2,w3w_1, w_2, w_3w1​,w2​,w3​ represent the weights of each stock in the portfolio and r1,r2,r3r_1, r_2, r_3r1​,r2​,r3​ the returns of the individual stocks.

w=invested in individual stocktotal portfolio valuew = \frac{\text{invested in individual stock}}{\text{total portfolio value}}w=total portfolio valueinvested in individual stock​

Worked. A $100 portfolio: A = $50, B = $30, C = $20.

Step 4 — compute the standard deviation of the portfolio​

This is the step that gives the method its name, and the notes teach it by showing the wrong answer first.

The wrong method​

σp=w1sd1+w2sd2+w3sd3✗\sigma_p = w_1 sd_1 + w_2 sd_2 + w_3 sd_3 \qquad \textbf{✗}σp​=w1​sd1​+w2​sd2​+w3​sd3​✗

What is wrong with this method?

This method doesn't account for the interdependence (correlation or covariance) between the stocks in the portfolio.

Weighting the individual standard deviations and adding them treats each stock as if it moved in complete lockstep with the others. It does not — and the whole value of a portfolio comes from the fact that it does not.

What the wrong formula secretly assumes

The naive sum is not arbitrary — it is the correlation = 1 case of the correct formula. The general expression is

σp2=∑i∑jwiwjρij σiσj\sigma_p^2 = \sum_i \sum_j w_i w_j \rho_{ij}\, \sigma_i \sigma_jσp2​=i∑​j∑​wi​wj​ρij​σi​σj​

Set every ρij=1\rho_{ij} = 1ρij​=1 and it factorises into (∑iwiσi)2\left(\sum_i w_i \sigma_i\right)^2(∑i​wi​σi​)2, so σp=∑iwiσi\sigma_p = \sum_i w_i \sigma_iσp​=∑i​wi​σi​ — exactly the wrong formula.

So using it means assuming every stock moves perfectly together. Since real correlations are below 1, the naive formula overstates portfolio risk. The gap between the two is precisely the diversification benefit.

The right method​

1) Define the portfolio weights.

w=[w1w2w3]=[30%40%30%]w = \begin{bmatrix} w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 30\% & 40\% & 30\% \end{bmatrix}w=[w1​​w2​​w3​​]=[30%​40%​30%​]

2) Build the covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ.

Σ=[Cov(A,A)Cov(A,B)Cov(A,C)Cov(B,A)Cov(B,B)Cov(B,C)Cov(C,A)Cov(C,B)Cov(C,C)]\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \text{Cov}(A,A) & \text{Cov}(A,B) & \text{Cov}(A,C) \\ \text{Cov}(B,A) & \text{Cov}(B,B) & \text{Cov}(B,C) \\ \text{Cov}(C,A) & \text{Cov}(C,B) & \text{Cov}(C,C) \end{bmatrix}Σ=​Cov(A,A)Cov(B,A)Cov(C,A)​Cov(A,B)Cov(B,B)Cov(C,B)​Cov(A,C)Cov(B,C)Cov(C,C)​​

Every entry comes from one relation:

Cov(X,Y)=ρX,Y σX σY\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho_{X,Y}\,\sigma_X\,\sigma_YCov(X,Y)=ρX,Y​σX​σY​

The diagonal collapses. A stock is perfectly correlated with itself, so ρA,A=1\rho_{A,A} = 1ρA,A​=1 and:

Cov(A,A)=1×σA×σA=σA2=Var(A)\text{Cov}(A,A) = 1 \times \sigma_A \times \sigma_A = \sigma_A^2 = \text{Var}(A)Cov(A,A)=1×σA​×σA​=σA2​=Var(A)

and likewise Cov(B,B)=σB2\text{Cov}(B,B) = \sigma_B^2Cov(B,B)=σB2​, Cov(C,C)=σC2\text{Cov}(C,C) = \sigma_C^2Cov(C,C)=σC2​. The diagonal is the variances; the off-diagonal is the correlations. Written out:

Σ=[σA2ρA,BσAσBρA,CσAσCρA,BσAσBσB2ρB,CσBσCρA,CσAσCρB,CσBσCσC2]\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_A^2 & \rho_{A,B}\sigma_A\sigma_B & \rho_{A,C}\sigma_A\sigma_C \\ \rho_{A,B}\sigma_A\sigma_B & \sigma_B^2 & \rho_{B,C}\sigma_B\sigma_C \\ \rho_{A,C}\sigma_A\sigma_C & \rho_{B,C}\sigma_B\sigma_C & \sigma_C^2 \end{bmatrix}Σ=​σA2​ρA,B​σA​σB​ρA,C​σA​σC​​ρA,B​σA​σB​σB2​ρB,C​σB​σC​​ρA,C​σA​σC​ρB,C​σB​σC​σC2​​​

The matrix is symmetric — Cov(A,B)=Cov(B,A)\text{Cov}(A,B) = \text{Cov}(B,A)Cov(A,B)=Cov(B,A), because correlation does not have a direction.

3) Portfolio variance.

Variancep=σp2=wT Σ w\text{Variance}_p = \sigma_p^2 = w^{T}\,\Sigma\,wVariancep​=σp2​=wTΣw

4) Portfolio standard deviation.

  σp=Variance=wT Σ w  \boxed{\;\sigma_p = \sqrt{\text{Variance}} = \sqrt{w^{T}\,\Sigma\,w}\;}σp​=Variance​=wTΣw​​
A missing square root on page 29

The step-by-step recap at the end of the deck writes "std devp=wTΣw\text{std dev}_p = w^T \Sigma wstd devp​=wTΣw" — without the square root. Page 28 has it correctly as wTΣw\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}wTΣw​.

wTΣww^T \Sigma wwTΣw is the variance; the standard deviation is its square root. The earlier page is right.

Step 5 — compute VaR​

  VaR=μp+Z×σp  \boxed{\;\text{VaR} = \mu_p + Z \times \sigma_p\;}VaR=μp​+Z×σp​​

where ZZZ corresponds to the confidence interval — the number of standard deviations out into the tail that the chosen CI sits.

This is where the normality assumption pays off. Because the distribution is fully described by μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ, the quantile is just a fixed multiple of σ\sigmaσ away from the mean. No sorting, no ranking, no counting — one lookup and one multiplication.

Conclusion — the method in five lines​

Assume: asset returns are normally distributed → characterised by mean and variance.

Step
1Gather the data
2Calculate the returns — StSt−1−1\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}} - 1St−1​St​​−1
3Calculate the portfolio return — w1r1+w2r2+w3r3w_1 r_1 + w_2 r_2 + w_3 r_3w1​r1​+w2​r2​+w3​r3​
4Calculate the portfolio standard deviation — wTΣw\sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}wTΣw​
5VaR =μp+Z×σp= \mu_p + Z \times \sigma_p=μp​+Z×σp​

Summary​

  • The variance-covariance method is parametric — it assumes returns are normally distributed, which reduces the whole distribution to a mean and a standard deviation.
  • Returns, not prices, because a $5 move is 50% on a $10 stock and 5% on a $100 one.
  • The portfolio mean is the weighted sum of the individual returns.
  • The portfolio standard deviation is NOT the weighted sum of the individual standard deviations — that ignores correlation, and is secretly the ρ=1\rho = 1ρ=1 case, which overstates risk.
  • The correct route is the covariance matrix: Cov(X,Y)=ρσXσY\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho\sigma_X\sigma_YCov(X,Y)=ρσX​σY​, with variances on the diagonal (since ρ=1\rho = 1ρ=1 with itself) and correlations off it.
  • σp=wTΣw\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^T \Sigma w}σp​=wTΣw​, and then VaR =μp+Z×σp= \mu_p + Z \times \sigma_p=μp​+Z×σp​ — one lookup instead of a sort.

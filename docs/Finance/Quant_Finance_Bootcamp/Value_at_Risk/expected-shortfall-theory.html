Expected Shortfall

The first note in this module ended on an unanswered question. VaR says there is a 1% chance of losing more than $5M — but more by how much? $10M? $100M? $1B?

That is something that VaR doesn't tell you.

Expected Shortfall is the answer.

Why it was introduced​

ES was introduced as a risk measure to address some of the significant shortcomings of the VaR model.

There are two, and they are quite different in character.

Shortcoming 1 — tail risk ignorance​

Take 1-day VaR at 95% CI = $1M. That number is the potential loss of the portfolio at 95% CI in 1 day — and the remaining 5% is simply labelled "worst possible loss" and left there.

VaR provides no information about the size of loss exceeding the VaR threshold.

It only indicates the maximum loss expected within a certain confidence interval, leaving the risk manager or user unaware of the potential losses that could occur beyond this point.

Two portfolios can have exactly the same VaR and completely different tails. VaR marks where the tail starts; it is blind to everything past that line.

Shortcoming 2 — lack of subadditivity​

The subtler failure, and the one regulators care about.

A subadditive risk measure satisfies

Risk(A)+Risk(B)≥Risk(A+B)\text{Risk}(A) + \text{Risk}(B) \ge \text{Risk}(A + B)Risk(A)+Risk(B)≥Risk(A+B)

— combining two portfolios should never create more risk than holding them apart. That is just diversification, formalised.

VaR does not always satisfy this.

VaR is not subadditive ⇒ the VaR of a combined portfolio can be higher than the VaR of the individual portfolios.

VaR3>(VaR1+VaR2)— possible\text{VaR}_3 > \left(\text{VaR}_1 + \text{VaR}_2\right) \quad \text{— possible}VaR3​>(VaR1​+VaR2​)— possible

which is absurd as a statement about risk: it says merging two books made you riskier than the sum of their parts. A measure that can say that will occasionally penalise diversification, which is the opposite of what a risk measure should do.

The diversification example in the notes

The deck builds the intuition with three portfolios:

PortfolioHoldings
P1P_1P1​2 technology stocksconcentrated
P2P_2P2​2 technology + 2 financerisk profile reduced — diversification benefit
P3P_3P3​2 technology + 2 finance + 2 energybest diversification benefit

P3P_3P3​ is the most robust because the sectors respond differently to the same shock. COVID is the worked case: it caused reduced spending, which hit technology and finance — but "we all need electricity", so energy held up.

That is what diversification is for, and a coherent risk measure must reward it. VaR sometimes does not.

What Expected Shortfall is​

Expected Shortfall / Conditional Value at Risk (C-VaR).

ES estimates the expected loss in cases where the loss exceeds the VaR threshold.

Essentially, ES is calculated by taking the average of losses greater than the VaR threshold.

  ESα=E[ L∣L>VaRα ]  \boxed{\;ES_\alpha = E\left[\,L \mid L > VaR_\alpha\,\right]\;}ESα​=E[L∣L>VaRα​]​

Read the notation directly: the expected value of the loss LLL, conditional on LLL having exceeded VaR. It is an average — but an average taken only over the bad tail.

Average of the losses beyond the VaR threshold=ES\text{Average of the losses beyond the VaR threshold} = ESAverage of the losses beyond the VaR threshold=ES

So VaR is a boundary and ES is an average. VaR asks where does the worst 1% begin? ES asks and when we are in that worst 1%, how bad is it typically?

Worked example​

100 days, VaR at 95% CI. The rank is

n×α=100×5100=5n \times \alpha = 100 \times \frac{5}{100} = \mathbf{5}n×α=100×1005​=5

so VaR sits at rank 5. The sorted returns:

Rank12345
Return−5%−4.7%−4.6%−3%−2.1%

VaR is the rank-5 return, −2.1%. ES averages everything worse than it:

ES=(−5%)+(−4.7%)+(−4.6%)+(−3%)4=−4.325%ES = \frac{(-5\%) + (-4.7\%) + (-4.6\%) + (-3\%)}{4} = \mathbf{-4.325\%}ES=4(−5%)+(−4.7%)+(−4.6%)+(−3%)​=−4.325%

and on a $1M portfolio:

ES=−4.325%×$1M=−$43,250ES = -4.325\% \times \$1\text{M} = \mathbf{-\$43{,}250}ES=−4.325%×$1M=−$43,250

against a VaR of −$21,000. The average bad day in the tail is more than twice the VaR. That gap is precisely the information VaR was withholding.

Which observations go into the average

The notes divide by 4, not 5 — averaging ranks 1–4 and excluding the VaR observation itself at rank 5. That is the strictly exceeds reading of L>VaRαL > VaR_\alphaL>VaRα​, and it matches the formula as written.

The other common convention includes the VaR observation (dividing by 5 here). Both are used in practice; they converge as the sample grows. Just be consistent — and know which one your number represents.

Advantages of ES​

1) Risk of extreme losses.

ES provides an average of the losses in the tail beyond the VaR level, offering a clearer picture of the potential extreme risk.

This is shortcoming 1, solved directly.

2) Subadditivity.

ES is subadditive. Basically it means that when you calculate ES:

ES(S1)+ES(S2)≥ES(S1+S2)ES(S_1) + ES(S_2) \ge ES(S_1 + S_2)ES(S1​)+ES(S2​)≥ES(S1​+S2​)

Combining always helps, or at worst does nothing — the diversification benefit is guaranteed to show up. This is shortcoming 2, solved.

A slip on the last page

The deck writes "Unlike ES → which was not subadditive" when introducing this point. It means "unlike VaR" — VaR is the measure that fails subadditivity, and the whole point of the sentence is that ES does not. The surrounding lines make the intent clear.

3) Regulatory approval.

The Basel Committee also agrees that ES is a more coherent risk measure than VaR.

"Coherent" is a technical term: a risk measure is coherent if it satisfies monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and subadditivity. VaR fails the last one, so it is not coherent. ES is. That is the formal reason Basel moved market-risk capital from VaR to ES.

Summary​

  • Expected Shortfall (Conditional VaR) estimates the expected loss given that the loss exceeds VaR: ESα=E[L∣L>VaRα]ES_\alpha = E[L \mid L > VaR_\alpha]ESα​=E[L∣L>VaRα​] — the average of the losses beyond the VaR threshold.
  • It was introduced to fix two shortcomings of VaR:
    • Tail risk ignorance — VaR gives no information about the size of losses beyond the threshold; two portfolios with identical VaR can have wildly different tails.
    • Lack of subadditivity — the VaR of a combined portfolio can exceed the sum of the parts, penalising diversification.
  • VaR is a boundary; ES is an average taken past that boundary.
  • Worked: with VaR at rank 5 (−2.1%), ES averages ranks 1–4 to −4.325%, or −$43,250 on $1M — more than twice the VaR.
  • ES is subadditive (ES(S1)+ES(S2)≥ES(S1+S2)ES(S_1) + ES(S_2) \ge ES(S_1+S_2)ES(S1​)+ES(S2​)≥ES(S1​+S2​)), which makes it a coherent risk measure, and is why the Basel Committee prefers it.

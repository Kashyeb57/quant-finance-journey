Backtesting — the Traffic Light Approach

Three methods have now produced three VaR numbers. None of them has been checked against reality. That is what backtesting is for.

What backtesting is​

Backtesting is a technique used to evaluate the accuracy and effectiveness of the VaR number produced by VaR models.

Backtesting is like doing a practice test before you go for the real exam.

The stakes if you skip it: if the VaR number is not accurate, the portfolio might face a higher loss than what was expected.

Three reasons to do it​

1) To test for accuracy. We use backtesting to make sure that the model's predictions about the potential financial loss are accurate. If a model predicts you could lose $100,000, backtesting helps verify whether such a prediction holds true based on past data.

2) To improve the models. If backtesting shows that the model often gets it wrong — VaR says $10,000 but the actual loss exceeds $10,000 frequently — then the model needs work.

3) To comply with rules. Regulators require financial institutions to backtest their models. This is to ensure models are robust and institutions are not underestimating the risk.

Two methods are covered: the Traffic Light Approach (Basel) and the Kupiec Method.

The Traffic Light Approach​

Recommended by the regulators.

The Traffic Light Approach is a method used to assess the risk level of an investment using colour-coded signals — red, yellow, green.

This approach helps financial institutions and risk managers quickly understand the risk exposure and decide on necessary action.

How it applies to VaR​

1) Consider 250 days of trading data — the past 1 year.

2) Check if losses exceed VaR. With VaR = $10,000: every day you need to check if your portfolio loss is less than or greater than $10,000.

3) If the loss > $10,000 ⇒ a breach (violation).

4) Count the total number of breach days and classify into colour signals.

The three zones​

1) Green Zone (low risk) — 0 to 4 breaches.

Risk levels are low and the frequency of breaches is within the expected range, suggesting that the model is performing well and is within acceptable limits.

Action: normal monitoring and operation can continue without immediate concern.

2) Yellow Zone (moderate risk) — 5 to 9 breaches.

Risk levels are moderate. The frequency of breaches is higher and nearing the upper tolerance, indicating that the model might not be able to capture some risk effectively.

Action: increase scrutiny, review the portfolio strategies, and perform daily monitoring.

3) Red Zone (high risk) — 10 or more breaches.

Risk levels are high with a significant frequency of breaches, suggesting that the model is inadequate or there are severe risk management issues.

ZoneRiskBreaches
GreenLow0 – 4
YellowModerate5 – 9
RedHigh10 +

Example: 3 breaches → Green Zone — meaning that on 3 days in the last year, losses exceeded VaR.

Why those numbers, and a gap in the source

The boundaries are not arbitrary. At 99% confidence over 250 days, the expected number of breaches is

250×1%=2.5250 \times 1\% = 2.5250×1%=2.5

So 0–4 is "about what you'd expect", 5–9 is "more than expected but could be bad luck", and 10+ is "too many to be chance."

The source writes the red zone as "breach > 10", which leaves exactly 10 in a gap between yellow (5–9) and red. Basel's actual rule puts 10 in the red zone, so the boundary is 10 or more — as written in the summary table above.

The Excel backtest​

The Backtesting (Traffic Light) sheet runs all three VaR numbers against the same 251 days of actual portfolio history.

ColumnHoldsFormula
G:IIndividual stock returns=B4/B3-1
QPortfolio return=SUMPRODUCT(G4:I4,$L$3:$N$3)
RActual PnL=Q4*1000000
S / T / UBreach flags per method=$X$4>R4
X12:X14Breach counts=COUNTIF(S4:S254,TRUE)

The breach test =$X$4>R4 reads: is the VaR threshold greater than the actual PnL? Since both are negative, "greater" means the actual loss was worse than VaR — a breach.

Results​

MethodVaR @ 99%BreachesZone
Variance-Covariance−$35,609.283🟢 Green
Historical Simulation−$36,780.772🟢 Green

All three pass, and all three sit close to the theoretical expectation of 2.51 breaches over 251 days. That is the outcome you want: the models are neither underestimating risk (too many breaches) nor being needlessly conservative (zero breaches would suggest VaR is set too wide and the firm is holding excess capital).

Note also that the historical method — with the largest VaR of the three at $36,781 — produced the fewest breaches, which is exactly the relationship you would expect. A wider threshold is crossed less often.

The sheet backtests a different number than the summary reports

The backtest pulls the historical VaR from 'Historical Simulation'!AK11, which is the percentile-approach figure (−$36,780.77). But the workbook's Summary Table reports AF11, the rank-approach figure (−$36,253.51).

Both are legitimate estimates — see the historical Excel note — but the same one should be used in both places. Testing one number and reporting another means the published VaR has not, strictly, been the number validated.

At these values it makes no practical difference: both thresholds produce the same 2 breaches. But on a book where the two estimates straddle a zone boundary, it would matter.

Summary​

  • Backtesting evaluates the accuracy of the VaR number — "a practice test before the real exam" — and is done to test accuracy, improve models, and satisfy regulators.
  • The Traffic Light Approach counts breaches (days where the actual loss exceeded VaR) over 250 trading days.
  • 0–4 = green (normal monitoring), 5–9 = yellow (increase scrutiny, daily monitoring), 10+ = red (model inadequate).
  • Those bands are calibrated to the 2.5 breaches expected at 99% over 250 days.
  • On the real portfolio: variance-covariance 3, historical 2, Monte Carlo 3 — all green.
  • The method with the widest VaR produced the fewest breaches, as it should.
  • Minor: the sheet backtests the percentile-approach historical VaR while the summary reports the rank-approach one.

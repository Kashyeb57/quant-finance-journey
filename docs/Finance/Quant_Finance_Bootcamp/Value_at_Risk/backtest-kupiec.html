Backtesting — the Kupiec Test

The traffic light sorts a breach count into three buckets. Kupiec asks a sharper question: is this breach count statistically consistent with the confidence level you claimed?

What it is​

The Kupiec Test, referred to as the Proportion of Failure (POF) test, is a statistical method used to evaluate the accuracy of a VaR model.

The model was developed by Paul Kupiec in 1995.

This test specifically checks whether the number of times losses exceeded the VaR number is consistent with the confidence interval set by the model.

The idea in one example​

1,000 days, 1-day VaR at 95% CI. How many breaches should there be?

Alpha(5%)×n(1000)=5100×1000=50\text{Alpha}(5\%) \times n(1000) = \frac{5}{100} \times 1000 = \mathbf{50}Alpha(5%)×n(1000)=1005​×1000=50

The model specified that 50 breaches are allowed.

Observe 50 and the model is behaving exactly as advertised. Observe 5, or 200, and something is wrong. Kupiec turns "how far from 50 is too far?" into a formal test.

How the test works​

a) Define the parameters.

VaR CIe.g. 95% — losses should exceed the VaR estimate only 5% of the time
Time horizonhow many trading days are we considering

b) Count the number of breaches — days where loss > VaR number.

c) Apply the formula.

  LRPOF=−2ln⁡[px (1−p) n−xp^ x (1−p^) n−x]  \boxed{\;LR_{POF} = -2\ln\left[\frac{p^{x}\,(1-p)^{\,n-x}}{\hat{p}^{\,x}\,(1-\hat{p})^{\,n-x}}\right]\;}LRPOF​=−2ln[p^​x(1−p^​)n−xpx(1−p)n−x​]​

xxxNumber of violations (failures)

with

p^=xn\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}p^​=nx​

and ppp set by the confidence level:

CIppp
99% VaR1%
95% VaR5%
90% VaR10%

What the ratio is doing. The numerator is the likelihood of seeing xxx breaches if the model's claimed rate ppp is true. The denominator is the likelihood under the rate you actually observed. If the model is right, the two are close and the ratio is near 1, so the log is near 0 and LRLRLR is small. A large LRLRLR means the observed breach rate is hard to reconcile with the claimed one.

Reading the result​

The LRLRLR follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom.

ResultVerdict
p-value > 5%Model is performing adequately
p-value < 5%Model is not performing adequately
Why 1 degree of freedom, and why chi-square

Why 1 degree of freedom? Because we are testing a single parameter. In the Kupiec test the key parameter being tested is the rate at which actual losses exceed the VaR number — one number, one degree of freedom.

Why chi-square? The likelihood ratio test statistic involves comparing two models by looking at how much more likely the data is under one model compared to the other. That comparison results in a test statistic that is not normally distributed, because it involves a ratio — which naturally leads to the chi-square distribution.

What the numbers mean​

All three p-values are around 0.74–0.76, nowhere near the 5% threshold. In plain terms: if the models were perfectly calibrated, you would see breach counts at least this far from 2.51 about three-quarters of the time. Two or three breaches is entirely unremarkable.

For reference, the chi-square critical value at 1 degree of freedom and 5% is 3.8415 — the observed LRLRLR values of 0.09 and 0.11 are far below it.

Note the shape of the test: Monte Carlo and variance-covariance give identical results because they had identical breach counts (3). Kupiec sees only the count, not the VaR level that produced it — a model with a much larger VaR that happened to be breached 3 times would score the same.

What "reliable" does and does not mean

The sheet's own footnote makes the point, and it is worth stating plainly: a high p-value is a failure to reject, not a proof of adequacy.

With n=251n = 251n=251 and an expected 2.51 breaches, this test has very little power. It would take a substantial number of breaches before the p-value dropped below 5%. Passing Kupiec means "the data gives no evidence the model is broken" — which is not the same as "the model is right."

This is also the test's known weakness: it looks only at how many breaches occurred, not when. Three breaches spread across the year and three breaches on three consecutive days score identically, though the second is far more alarming. Tests of independence (such as Christoffersen's) exist to cover that gap.

Traffic light versus Kupiec​

Traffic LightKupiec
TypeRule of thumb, bucketedStatistical hypothesis test
OutputGreen / yellow / redp-value
BasisFixed breach bandsLikelihood ratio, chi-square
Adapts to nnn and CI?No — bands assume 250 days at 99%Yes — ppp and nnn are inputs

The traffic light is designed to be applied uniformly by a regulator across many institutions without argument. Kupiec is the more flexible instrument: change the confidence level or the sample length and the test adjusts, where the traffic light's bands would have to be recalculated.

Both agreed here — all three models pass.

Summary​

  • The Kupiec (POF) test, from Paul Kupiec, 1995, checks whether the observed breach count is statistically consistent with the claimed confidence level.
  • LRPOF=−2ln⁡[px(1−p)n−xp^x(1−p^)n−x]LR_{POF} = -2\ln\left[\frac{p^x(1-p)^{n-x}}{\hat{p}^x(1-\hat{p})^{n-x}}\right]LRPOF​=−2ln[p^​x(1−p^​)n−xpx(1−p)n−x​], with p^=x/n\hat{p} = x/np^​=x/n and ppp = 1%, 5% or 10% for 99%, 95%, 90% CI.
  • LRLRLR follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom — one, because a single parameter (the exceedance rate) is being tested.
  • p-value > 5% → adequate; < 5% → not adequate.
  • On the real portfolio all three models pass comfortably: LR ≈ 0.09–0.11, p-values ≈ 0.74–0.76, against a 5% critical value of 3.8415. All values independently verified.
  • Passing is a failure to reject, not proof of correctness — and Kupiec sees only the breach count, never whether the breaches clustered.

Expected Shortfall in Excel

This one is not in the workbook

Every other sheet in this module exists in Value-at-Risk-Model-QFI.xlsx. There is no Expected Shortfall sheet — the deck covers ES in theory and stops there.

So this note builds it, on the same portfolio and the same 251 returns the rest of the module used, so the ES numbers sit directly alongside the VaR ones.

What is already there​

The Historical Simulation sheet has done most of the work:

RangeHolds
Q3:Q253Portfolio returns, =SUMPRODUCT(returns, weights)
AB3:AB253The same 251 returns, sorted ascending
AF8The VaR rank, =ROUNDUP(n*alpha, 0)
AF9The return at that rank
AF11VaR, =AF10*AF9

ES needs one more cell.

The one formula​

ES is the average of every return worse than the VaR return. Since AB is already sorted and AF9 already holds the VaR return:

AF12 = AVERAGEIF($AB$3:$AB$253, "<"&AF9)     ' ES return
AF13 = AF10 * AF12                            ' ES in dollars

AVERAGEIF with the criterion "<"&AF9 averages exactly the observations strictly below the VaR return — ranks 1 to k−1k-1k−1 — which is the convention the notes use when they divide by 4 rather than 5.

If you prefer the convention that includes the VaR observation, use "<=" instead.

The two conventions differ most at 99%, where the average is taken over only 2 observations and adding a third moves it noticeably. By 90% — averaging over 25 observations — the choice barely matters. The smaller the tail, the more the convention matters.

What this actually tells you​

At 99%, VaR says $36,254 and ES says $43,819. If the bad day arrives, the typical loss is about 21% worse than the number the risk report quoted. That extra $7,565 was always there in the data — VaR simply does not report it.

Then notice the pattern in the ratio: it grows as the confidence level falls.

CIES / VaRTail averaged over
99%1.21×2 observations
95%1.33×12 observations
90%1.41×25 observations

At 99% the VaR boundary already sits deep in the tail, so the handful of losses beyond it are not much worse. At 90% the boundary is much closer to the middle of the distribution, and the average beyond it reaches all the way out to the −5.03% day. The lower the confidence level, the more of the extreme tail gets pulled into the ES average — so the gap between the two measures widens.

Which is worth knowing when reading a risk report: a 90% VaR is a much weaker statement about tail exposure than a 99% VaR, and ES makes that visible where VaR does not.

Adding it to the other two methods​

The same single formula works on the variance-covariance and Monte Carlo sheets, pointed at their own sorted-return columns. For variance-covariance there is also a closed form, since ES under normality has an analytic expression:

ESα=μp−σp⋅ϕ(Φ−1(α))αES_\alpha = \mu_p - \sigma_p \cdot \frac{\phi\left(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\right)}{\alpha}ESα​=μp​−σp​⋅αϕ(Φ−1(α))​

where ϕ\phiϕ is the standard normal density and Φ−1\Phi^{-1}Φ−1 its inverse CDF — the same NORM.S.INV the sheet already uses, with NORM.S.DIST(..., FALSE) supplying the density. That mirrors how variance-covariance replaced sorting with a lookup for VaR: assume normality and the tail average becomes a formula too.

Summary​

  • The workbook contains no ES sheet — this builds one on the existing sorted returns.
  • ES is one formula: AVERAGEIF($AB$3:$AB$253, "<"&VaR_return), then multiply by portfolio value.
  • On the real portfolio: 99% VaR −$36,254 against ES −$43,819 — the typical tail loss is 21% worse than VaR reports.
  • ES / VaR grows as confidence falls (1.21× at 99%, 1.41× at 90%) because a lower boundary pulls more of the extreme tail into the average.
  • The inclusive vs exclusive convention matters most when the tail holds few observations — a visible difference at 99%, negligible at 90%.
  • Under normality ES also has a closed form, so the variance-covariance sheet can compute it without sorting.

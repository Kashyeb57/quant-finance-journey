The CIR Model

The Vasicek model ended on a specific flaw: it can produce negative interest rates. CIR is the answer to that, and the fix is one square root.

Introduction​

The CIR model — Cox-Ingersoll-Ross — is a mathematical model which is used to model interest rate movement.

It was developed in 1985 by John C. Cox, Jonathan E. Ingersoll and Stephen A. Ross.

The CIR interest rate model is also called a "one factor model", because it describes interest rate movement as driven by a sole source of market risk.

Both models share that property:

CIR→f(market risk)Vasicek→f(market risk)\text{CIR} \rightarrow f(\text{market risk}) \qquad\qquad \text{Vasicek} \rightarrow f(\text{market risk})CIR→f(market risk)Vasicek→f(market risk)

One component, one factor. Which is a simplification, because in reality the interest rate responds to monetary policy, market sentiment, inflation and economic policy — four forces compressed into one term.

Why the square root fixes negative rates​

The square root of any number is always positive.

That is the whole argument, and it works through the size of the shock rather than its sign:

In Vasicek, the shock is σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​ — the same size at every rate level. So when rtr_trt​ is already near zero, a normal draw of the usual magnitude can push it straight through into negative territory.

In CIR, the shock is σrt dWt\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_tσrt​​dWt​. As rtr_trt​ falls toward zero, rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ falls toward zero too, so the randomness switches itself off exactly where it would do damage. At rt=0r_t = 0rt​=0 the diffusion term vanishes entirely and only the drift ab dtab\,dtabdt remains — which is positive, pushing the rate back up.

CIR →rt→\rightarrow \sqrt{r_t} \rightarrow→rt​​→ positive value →\rightarrow→ interest rates can't go negative.

The guarantee has a condition

The notes state non-negativity as absolute. Strictly, CIR keeps the rate strictly positive only when the Feller condition holds:

2ab≥σ22ab \ge \sigma^22ab≥σ2

If volatility is large relative to the mean-reversion pull, the rate can still touch zero (though it cannot pass through it). Worth knowing, and it is checked against the calibrated parameters in the CIR Excel note.

Assumptions​

1) It assumes mean reversion. This means the interest rate tends to move towards the long-term equilibrium level over time — rising when it sits below bbb, falling when above.

2) It assumes aaa, bbb and σ\sigmaσ are constant.

aaaspeed of mean reversion
bbblong-term mean
σ\sigmaσvolatility / standard deviation

The notes flag this immediately with a question: for a stock ABC, will volatility always remain constant? Obviously not — and the note beside it is blunt about what that means:

Assumption → limitation.

The long-term mean bbb in particular has to be obtained by calibrating the model to market data, and it is economy-specific — the US economy has one level, India and China another.

Which model to use is therefore an empirical question about the economy you are modelling, not a matter of taste:

EconomyRates observedUse
USA, India, ChinaPositiveCIR
Germany, JapanHave gone to 0 or negativeVasicek

The notes are direct about the mismatch: for economies whose rates are firmly positive, a model that generates negative rates "makes no sense". And conversely — if you need to represent a genuinely negative rate, CIR structurally cannot do it.

So the fix is not free. CIR trades away the ability to model negative rates in exchange for never producing spurious ones.

Limitations​

a) It assumes constant aaa, bbb and σ\sigmaσ. In reality these parameters might change due to changes in market and economic conditions. The assumption that made the model tractable is the same one that makes it wrong over long horizons.

b) It is not suitable for modelling negative interest rates. The rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ term makes the interest rate non-negative — which is a feature in the US and a defect in Germany.

c) It is a single-factor model.

drt=a(b−rt) dt⏟+σrt dWt⏟random fluctuationdr_t = \underbrace{a(b - r_t)\,dt}_{} + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,\underbrace{dW_t}_{\text{random fluctuation}}drt​=a(b−rt​)dt​​+σrt​​random fluctuationdWt​​​

with rtr_trt​ as the sole market risk factor. But interest rates can rely on multiple factors — market sentiment and economic policies among them — and a one-factor model cannot separate them.

d) No jumps or discontinuities.

The CIR model assumes a continuous path for the interest rate. This may not reflect real-world market behaviour, which can include jumps or sudden changes due to unexpected events.

A central bank announcement moves rates in a step, not a wiggle. Brownian motion has continuous paths by construction, so no amount of parameter tuning will reproduce that.

Other interest rate models​

The two in this module are the start of a family:

Model
1Vasicek
2CIR
3Hull-White (John C. Hull)
4Black-Karasinski
5Libor Market Model
6Heath-Jarrow-Morton

Each relaxes one of the assumptions above — time-varying parameters, log-normal rates, or modelling the whole forward curve rather than a single short rate.

Summary​

  • CIR (Cox, Ingersoll and Ross, 1985) is a one-factor stochastic interest rate model: drt=a(b−rt) dt+σrt dWtdr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_tdrt​=a(b−rt​)dt+σrt​​dWt​.
  • It is Vasicek plus rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ in the diffusion term. The drift, and therefore the mean-reversion behaviour, is unchanged.
  • That square root is what prevents negative rates: as rtr_trt​ approaches zero the shock shrinks to nothing, leaving only the upward drift. (Strictly, under the Feller condition 2ab≥σ22ab \ge \sigma^22ab≥σ2.)
  • Use CIR where rates are positive (US, India, China); use Vasicek where they can go negative (Germany, Japan). Neither dominates.
  • Limitations: constant parameters, cannot model negative rates, only one risk factor, and no jumps — real rates move in steps that a continuous path cannot reproduce.
  • The wider family includes Hull-White, Black-Karasinski, the Libor Market Model and Heath-Jarrow-Morton.

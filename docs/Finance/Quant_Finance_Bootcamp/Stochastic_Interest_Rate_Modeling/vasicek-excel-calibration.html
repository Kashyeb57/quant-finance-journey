Calibrating Vasicek with MLE

The theory note gives the model. It does not give the numbers. The three parameters — aaa, bbb and σ\sigmaσ — have to come from observed data, and the method for extracting them is maximum likelihood estimation.

What MLE is​

MLE is a method which is used to find the best-fitting parameters for a mathematical model, based on observed data.

Applied here, that means finding aaa, bbb and σ\sigmaσ in

drt=a(b−rt) dt+σ dWtdr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_tdrt​=a(b−rt​)dt+σdWt​

The principle in the notes' own words:

Adjust the parameters in such a way that the values generated from the Vasicek model closely align with the actual data.

So there are two columns of numbers — what the rate actually did, and what the model says it should have done — and calibration is the search for the parameters that make the gap between them as small (as likely) as possible.

The five steps​

1. Calculate drtdr_tdrt​ — for both the actual data and the Vasicek model.

Actual rateVasicekΔr\Delta rΔr actualΔr\Delta rΔr Vasicek
TTT5.0%4.5%
T−1T-1T−15.1%4.75%5.0% − 5.1%4.5% − 4.75%
T−2T-2T−25.05%4.6%5.1% − 5.05%4.75% − 4.6%
T−3T-3T−35.15%⋮⋮⋮

2. Calculate the residual — the error term:

Residual=ΔrActual−ΔrVasicek\text{Residual} = \Delta r_{\text{Actual}} - \Delta r_{\text{Vasicek}}Residual=ΔrActual​−ΔrVasicek​

3. Take a normal distribution of the residual, with

Mean=0Std deviation=σ\text{Mean} = 0 \qquad\qquad \text{Std deviation} = \sigmaMean=0Std deviation=σ

The residual is assumed normally distributed around a mean of 0. This is not an arbitrary assumption — it falls straight out of the model. The deterministic part is what the model predicted; whatever is left over is the realisation of σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​, and dWtdW_tdWt​ is normal with mean zero by construction. So the residuals should look like normal draws, and σ\sigmaσ is exactly the parameter that says how wide they should be.

4. Take the log likelihood. Each residual gets a probability density from that normal distribution; take the log of each.

5. Sum all the logs, and maximise the sum — by changing aaa, bbb and σ\sigmaσ.

We are trying to maximise the likelihood of the function by changing the parameters aaa, bbb, σ\sigmaσ.

Why take the logarithm?​

Since we are working with multiple time steps, the total likelihood of the entire dataset is the product of the likelihood of each individual time step.

L=A×B×C×⋯L = A \times B \times C \times \cdotsL=A×B×C×⋯

And a product of many probability densities is awkward — multiply 251 small numbers together and you underflow to zero. The logarithm turns it into something tractable:

log⁡(A×B×C×⋯ )=log⁡(A)+log⁡(B)+log⁡(C)+⋯\log(A \times B \times C \times \cdots) = \log(A) + \log(B) + \log(C) + \cdotslog(A×B×C×⋯)=log(A)+log(B)+log(C)+⋯

A product becomes a sum. Since the logarithm is monotonically increasing, whatever maximises the log-likelihood also maximises the likelihood — so nothing is lost by optimising the easier quantity.

The Excel build​

The data is the US Treasury yield curve — the workbook carries full 2024 and 2023 sheets with the 1 Mo through 10 Yr columns. The calibration uses the 3-month rate, 252 observations.

The Vasicek Parameter Est (MLE) sheet is the five steps as seven columns:

Two details worth noticing.

Column G has no random term. It is only a(b−rt−1) dta(b - r_{t-1})\,dta(b−rt−1​)dt — the deterministic part. That is correct and it is the point: the model's prediction is the drift, and the residual in column H is what the stochastic term must have supplied. Calibration then asks which σ\sigmaσ makes those residuals most plausible.

NORMDIST(..., FALSE) returns the density, not the cumulative probability. The FALSE is what selects the PDF — exactly what a likelihood needs.

Did the calibration work?​

Worth checking rather than assuming, and there is a clean test. If MLE is doing its job, the fitted σ\sigmaσ should come out close to the actual standard deviation of the daily rate changes — because that is what the residuals are.

Value
Std deviation of daily changes in the 3-month rate0.00018294
Calibrated σ\sigmaσ0.00018280

They agree to four significant figures. The calibration recovered the volatility correctly — a small but real confirmation that the likelihood was set up right.

Where the data is thinner than it looks

Two things about the input are worth knowing.

The 3-month rate is quoted to two decimal places, so 86 of the 251 daily changes (34%) are exactly zero — the rate simply printed the same number two days running. That rounding does not break the fit above, but it means the "daily volatility" being measured is partly a quantisation artifact.

aaa and bbb are weakly identified over one year. The fitted a=0.1023a = 0.1023a=0.1023 implies a mean-reversion half-life of

ln⁡2a=0.6930.1023≈6.8 years\frac{\ln 2}{a} = \frac{0.693}{0.1023} \approx 6.8 \text{ years}aln2​=0.10230.693​≈6.8 years

Over a single year of data, a process reverting on a 6.8-year timescale barely reverts at all — so the data contains very little information about where bbb actually is. That is why the fitted bbb (5.74%) sits noticeably above the sample average rate (5.49%) without the fit objecting. σ\sigmaσ is pinned down by 251 observations; aaa and bbb are not.

This is a general feature of short-rate calibration, not a mistake in the sheet — mean reversion is a slow phenomenon and short samples struggle to see it.

Simulating the calibrated model​

The Vasicek Simulation (MLE) sheet takes the three fitted parameters and runs the model forward — 10 simulations across columns E:N, 252 days down the rows.

CellHolds

E2:N2=$B$4 — every path starts at r0r_0r0​

and the step itself:

E3 = $B$1*($B$2-E2)*$B$6 + $B$3*SQRT($B$6)*NORM.S.INV(RAND()) + E2

which reads directly as the Euler discretisation of the SDE:

rt+Δt=a(b−rt) Δt⏟drift+σΔt Z⏟shock+rt⏟where we werer_{t+\Delta t} = \underbrace{a(b - r_t)\,\Delta t}_{\text{drift}} + \underbrace{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z}_{\text{shock}} + \underbrace{r_t}_{\text{where we were}}rt+Δt​=drifta(b−rt​)Δt​​+shockσΔt​Z​​+where we werert​​​

The shape of that step is right — drift scaled by Δt\Delta tΔt, shock by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ — unlike the Monte Carlo workbooks in Module 4, which scaled the drift by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ too. But the units of σ\sigmaσ do not line up between the two sheets.

The simulation is 15.9× too smooth — a units mismatch

The calibration sheet fits σ\sigmaσ with

=NORMDIST(H2, 0, $O$4, FALSE)

where H2 is a one-day residual. So the fitted σ\sigmaσ is already a per-day standard deviation — and indeed it came out at 0.00018280 against a measured daily-change standard deviation of 0.00018294.

The simulation sheet then multiplies it by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ again:

$B$3*SQRT($B$6)*NORM.S.INV(RAND())

which treats that same number as an annualised volatility. The result:

Daily shock std
Actual 3-month rate0.00018294
Simulated by the sheet0.00001152
Ratio0.0629 — that is 1/2521/\sqrt{252}1/252​

The simulated paths are 15.9× less volatile than the data they were calibrated on. You can see it in the saved values: the first path moves 0.0552 → 0.05518 → 0.05520 in steps of about 2×10⁻⁵, where the real rate moves in steps of about 1.8×10⁻⁴.

Either fix works, not both:

  • calibrate against the scaled quantity — NORMDIST(H2, 0, $O$4*SQRT($O$7), FALSE) — so σ\sigmaσ is annualised, or
  • drop the Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ from the simulation — $B$3*NORM.S.INV(RAND()) — keeping σ\sigmaσ per-day.

Because the rate is added back rather than compounded, the process is arithmetic — which is precisely why it can cross zero into negative territory, the limitation the CIR model sets out to fix.

Summary​

  • MLE finds the best-fitting parameters for a model given observed data — here aaa, bbb and σ\sigmaσ for Vasicek.
  • The five steps: compute drdrdr for actual and model → take the residual → assume it is normal with mean 0 and std σ\sigmaσ → take logs → sum and maximise by changing the parameters.
  • The log turns a product of 251 densities into a sum, which is both numerically stable and easier to maximise; being monotonic, it does not change where the maximum is.
  • The model column carries only the drift; the residual is what σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​ must have contributed.
  • Calibrated on the 3-month Treasury: aaa = 0.1023, bbb = 0.0574, σ\sigmaσ = 0.00018280, log-likelihood 1804.10.
  • The fit checks out — σ\sigmaσ matches the sample standard deviation of daily changes (0.00018294) to four significant figures. But aaa and bbb are weakly identified, because a 6.8-year half-life barely shows up in one year of data.
  • The simulation sheet has the right Euler shape but a units mismatch: the calibration returns a per-day σ\sigmaσ and the simulation multiplies it by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ again, making the paths 15.9× too smooth. Fix it at one end or the other, not both.

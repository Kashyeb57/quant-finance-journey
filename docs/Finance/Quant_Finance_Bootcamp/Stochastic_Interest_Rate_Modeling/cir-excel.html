CIR in Excel

The CIR theory note adds one term to Vasicek. This note builds it on the same data, and the interesting part is how little of the workbook changes — and where that turns out to be a problem.

Calibration​

The CIR Parameter Est (MLE) sheet is laid out identically to the Vasicek one: 252 observations of the 3-month Treasury rate, with the same seven-column pipeline.

Column G is a(b−rt−1) dta(b - r_{t-1})\,dta(b−rt−1​)dt — the same as Vasicek, which is right, because CIR and Vasicek share a drift. The square root lives only in the diffusion.

The calibrated parameters​

Two things stand out.

The two σ\sigmaσ values are almost identical — 0.00018093 against 0.00018280, a 1% difference. For models whose diffusion terms differ by a factor of rt≈0.235\sqrt{r_t} \approx 0.235rt​​≈0.235, that is suspicious. It is the first sign of the problem below.

The two aaa values are not remotely similar. Vasicek fits 0.102, CIR fits 11.08 — a factor of over 100, on the same data. In half-life terms:

Half-life ln⁡2/a\ln 2 / aln2/a
Vasicek6.8 years
CIR16 trading days

One model says the rate reverts over years, the other over three weeks. Both "fit" the data about equally well (log-likelihoods 1804 and 1807). That is weak identification — as noted in the Vasicek note, one year of data contains very little information about mean reversion, so the optimiser is free to land almost anywhere without the likelihood objecting.

The Feller check​

The theory note flagged that CIR's non-negativity guarantee requires the Feller condition:

2ab≥σ22ab \ge \sigma^22ab≥σ2

With the fitted parameters:

2ab=2×11.0786×0.054897=1.2164σ2=3.27×10−82ab = 2 \times 11.0786 \times 0.054897 = 1.2164 \qquad\qquad \sigma^2 = 3.27 \times 10^{-8}2ab=2×11.0786×0.054897=1.2164σ2=3.27×10−8

Comfortably satisfied — by seven orders of magnitude. The simulated rate cannot reach zero, so CIR's headline property holds here.

The likelihood forgot the square root​

Look again at column I, in both workbooks:

Vasicek:  =NORMDIST(H2, 0, $O$4, FALSE)
CIR:      =NORMDIST(H2, 0, $O$4, FALSE)

They are the same formula. But under CIR the residual is not σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​ — it is σrt dWt\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_tσrt​​dWt​, so its standard deviation varies with the rate level and the density should be

NORMDIST(H,  0,  σrt−1Δt,  FALSE)\text{NORMDIST}\left(H,\; 0,\; \sigma\sqrt{r_{t-1}}\sqrt{\Delta t},\; \text{FALSE}\right)NORMDIST(H,0,σrt−1​​Δt​,FALSE)

Because the rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ is missing, the sheet is fitting a Vasicek likelihood and labelling the answer σCIR\sigma_{CIR}σCIR​. That is why the two volatilities came out nearly equal — they are estimates of the same thing.

The consequence appears when the simulation does apply the square root:

Simulation​

The CIR Simulation (MLE) sheet is the Vasicek simulation with one insertion — 10 paths across E:N, 252 days down the rows, all starting at r0=0.0552r_0 = 0.0552r0​=0.0552:

E3 = $B$1*($B$2-E2)*$B$6 + $B$3*SQRT(E2)*SQRT($B$6)*NORM.S.INV(RAND()) + E2

Set against the Vasicek version, the difference is exactly SQRT(E2):

rt+Δt=a(b−rt)Δt+σrt⏟CIR onlyΔt Z+rtr_{t+\Delta t} = a(b - r_t)\Delta t + \sigma\underbrace{\sqrt{r_t}}_{\text{CIR only}}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z + r_trt+Δt​=a(b−rt​)Δt+σCIR onlyrt​​​​Δt​Z+rt​

E2 is the previous row's rate, so SQRT(E2) is rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ — the theory implemented literally, and correctly.

The two defects compound — CIR paths come out 68× too smooth

This sheet inherits the units mismatch documented in the Vasicek note (a per-day σ\sigmaσ multiplied by Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ a second time), and adds the missing-r\sqrt{r}r​ calibration error above. Together:

Daily shock std
Actual 3-month rate0.00018294
Vasicek simulation0.00001152 — 15.9× too smooth
CIR simulation0.00000268 — 68× too smooth

The 68 is the 15.9 from the units mismatch multiplied by the 4.3 from the missing rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ in the likelihood.

To fix it properly, both ends have to agree: put rt−1Δt\sqrt{r_{t-1}}\sqrt{\Delta t}rt−1​​Δt​ into the calibration's NORMDIST so the fitted σ\sigmaσ is a true annualised CIR volatility, and leave the simulation formula as it stands.

The structure of both sheets is right — the columns, the mean reversion, the square root in the simulation, the Feller-safe parameters. It is the scaling that does not survive the trip from one sheet to the other.

The module's real lesson is in that last row. Two models with materially different dynamics fit the same year of Treasury data almost equally well, and their mean-reversion speeds differ by a factor of 100. Choosing between them cannot be done on goodness-of-fit alone — it has to be done on the structural question the theory note posed: can the rate you are modelling go negative or not?

That is the honest state of short-rate modelling. The maths is exact; the calibration is not.

Summary​

  • The CIR calibration sheet is structurally identical to the Vasicek one — same seven columns, same drift in column G, since CIR and Vasicek share a drift.
  • Calibrated: aaa = 11.0786, bbb = 0.054897, σ\sigmaσ = 0.00018093, log-likelihood 1806.81.
  • The Feller condition holds easily (2ab=1.222ab = 1.222ab=1.22 vs σ2=3.3×10−8\sigma^2 = 3.3\times10^{-8}σ2=3.3×10−8), so the simulated rate stays positive.
  • The likelihood omits rt\sqrt{r_t}rt​​ — it is a Vasicek likelihood, which is why the two fitted volatilities are nearly equal, and it understates σCIR\sigma_{CIR}σCIR​ by 4.3×.
  • Combined with the inherited units mismatch, CIR's simulated paths are 68× less volatile than the data.
  • The simulation's SQRT(E2) is the theory implemented correctly — the scaling, not the structure, is what breaks.
  • aaa differs by 100× between the two models on identical data while the log-likelihoods barely move: mean reversion is weakly identified in one year of observations.

The Vasicek Model

Every model so far has been about a stock. This module changes the underlying: the thing being modelled is the interest rate.

Why a stochastic model at all​

Look at daily data for, say, the US 3-year Treasury rate and you see the same thing you see in a stock chart — a jagged, unpredictable line.

The pattern that we see in the interest rate is random in nature.

So it needs the same treatment: for modelling the randomness of interest rates, we use a stochastic interest rate model.

This module covers two of them — Vasicek and CIR — in theory, and then in Excel, where there are two separate jobs: calibrate the model (with MLE) and then implement it.

The model​

The Vasicek model was introduced by Oldrich Vasicek in 1977. It is a stochastic interest rate model used to model how interest rates will move in the future.

  drt=a(b−rt) dt+σ dWt  \boxed{\;dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\;}drt​=a(b−rt​)dt+σdWt​​

Term by term:

SymbolMeaning
rtr_trt​The interest rate at time ttt
drtdr_tdrt​The change in the interest rate
aaaSpeed of mean reversion — how fast the rate returns to the mean
bbbLong-term average rate
dtdtdtSmall change in time — 1/252
σ\sigmaσVolatility of the interest rate
dWtdW_tdWt​Wiener process / Brownian motion

Where bbb comes from: take the historical data — the notes use 3-year Treasury data — and average it. That average is bbb.

Why 1/252: a year has 365 days, minus Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, leaving 252 trading days.

dt=1252dt = \frac{1}{252}dt=2521​

What dWtdW_tdWt​ is doing: it represents the random, unpredictable fluctuation that you see in the interest rate over time, and in practice it is generated exactly as in the previous module:

dWt=Zt×ΔtdW_t = Z_t \times \sqrt{\Delta t}dWt​=Zt​×Δt​

where ZtZ_tZt​ is a random number generated from a standard normal distribution.

Why rates fluctuate in the first place​

The notes list the real-world causes that dWtdW_tdWt​ is standing in for:

  • economic news
  • policy change
  • market sentiment

None of them are predictable, which is why they are modelled as Brownian motion rather than forecast.

Two parts, doing two different jobs​

The equation splits cleanly, and this split is the whole model:

TermNameJob
a(b−rt) dta(b - r_t)\,dta(b−rt​)dtDeterministic part / mean reversionDrives the interest rate toward the long-term bbb
σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​Stochastic partCaptures the random and unpredictable deviation from the deterministic trajectory

The first term knows where the rate should go. The second term is everything that stops it getting there in a straight line.

Mean reversion, in two cases​

The mean-reverting component is a(b−rt) dta(b - r_t)\,dta(b−rt​)dt, and its sign is what matters. Note that aaa and dtdtdt are both always positive — dt=1/252dt = 1/252dt=1/252, and aaa is a speed. So the sign of the whole term is decided by (b−rt)(b - r_t)(b−rt​) alone.

Case 1 — the rate is above the mean (rt>br_t > brt​>b).

rt>b  ⟹  (b−rt)<0  ⟹  drt<0r_t > b \;\Longrightarrow\; (b - r_t) < 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; dr_t < 0rt​>b⟹(b−rt​)<0⟹drt​<0

The change is negative, so the model adjusts the short rate downward.

Case 2 — the rate is below the mean (rt<br_t < brt​<b).

rt<b  ⟹  (b−rt)>0  ⟹  drt>0r_t < b \;\Longrightarrow\; (b - r_t) > 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; dr_t > 0rt​<b⟹(b−rt​)>0⟹drt​>0

The change is positive, so the model adjusts the short rate upward.

The direction of the short rate depends on the values of bbb and rtr_trt​.

Direction
rt>br_t > brt​>bdrt<0dr_t < 0drt​<0Downwards
rt<br_t < brt​<bdrt>0dr_t > 0drt​>0Upwards

That is mean reversion in one line: the further the rate is from bbb, and the larger aaa is, the harder it is pulled back.

Strengths​

a) It is intuitive. The concept of mean reversion aligns with how many people think about interest rates — returning to a normal level after a period of being high or low. The model's central assumption is one most practitioners already hold.

b) It has an analytical solution. It is possible to derive a closed-form solution for certain use cases — notably bond pricing and risk management.

The notes make "closed form" concrete with the simplest possible example: x+2=5x + 2 = 5x+2=5 solves directly to x=3x = 3x=3. No iteration, no simulation. Vasicek admits that kind of solution, which is why it survived — you can price a bond with a formula rather than a Monte Carlo run.

Limitations​

a) It can produce negative interest rates.

It can produce a negative interest rate, which can be unrealistic in the case where interest rates are high.

The culprit is identifiable: the stochastic part σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​ is responsible for negative interest rates. Since dWtdW_tdWt​ is normally distributed, it is unbounded below — a large enough negative draw can push rtr_trt​ through zero, and nothing in the equation stops it.

Whether that is a flaw depends on where you are:

Rates
USA, India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, ChinaPositive
Germany, JapanHave seen negative rates

So negative rates are not impossible in reality — but a model that produces them in a high-rate economy is producing nonsense.

This is exactly what the CIR model fixes, and it is why the next note exists.

b) It is a single-factor model.

The model is driven by market risk, which is the only factor affecting the change in the interest rate.

One source of randomness, one dWtdW_tdWt​. Everything that moves rates in reality — policy, inflation, credit, liquidity — is compressed into that single term.

Summary​

  • Interest rates are random in nature, so they are modelled with a stochastic interest rate model.
  • Vasicek (Oldrich Vasicek, 1977): drt=a(b−rt) dt+σ dWtdr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_tdrt​=a(b−rt​)dt+σdWt​.
  • aaa is the speed of mean reversion, bbb the long-term average rate (the historical average), σ\sigmaσ the volatility, dt=1/252dt = 1/252dt=1/252, and dWt=ZtΔtdW_t = Z_t\sqrt{\Delta t}dWt​=Zt​Δt​ the Brownian shock.
  • The equation is deterministic mean reversion + a stochastic deviation: one term pulls toward bbb, the other knocks it off course.
  • Above the mean the drift is negative, below it positive — the rate is always being pulled back toward bbb.
  • Strengths: intuitive, and it has a closed-form solution usable for bond pricing and risk management.
  • Limitations: it can generate negative rates (the σ dWt\sigma\,dW_tσdWt​ term is unbounded below), and it is a single-factor model.

Model Development, Implementation and Use

Model risk management begins with robust model development, implementation and use. Another essential element is sound model validation.

Note the order. Validation comes second. You cannot validate your way out of a badly built model — which is why this section comes before Section 5.

The third leg is model governance, which

sets an effective framework with defined roles and responsibilities for clear communication of model assumptions and limitations.

What an effective development process starts with​

a) A clear objective — a statement of purpose.

b) Design, theory and logic underlying the model, which should be

well documented and generally supported by published research and sound industry practice.

That documentation has to cover four things:

Mathematical specificationthe equations themselves
Numerical techniquehow they are solved
Approximationwhat shortcuts were taken
Strengths / weaknesses→ the model's limitations

The developer should ensure that the components of the model are appropriate for the intended business use case.

Consider the alternatives​

Comparison with alternate theories and approaches is a fundamental component of a sound modelling process.

This is a requirement people routinely skip. It is not enough to build M1M_1M1​ and show it works — you have to be able to say why M1M_1M1​ and not M2M_2M2​, M3M_3M3​ or M4M_4M4​:

M1  ✓⟶implementedM2,  M3,  M4⏟considered and rejected⟶justifyM_1 \;\checkmark \longrightarrow \text{implemented} \qquad \underbrace{M_2,\; M_3,\; M_4}_{\text{considered and rejected}} \longrightarrow \textbf{justify}M1​✓⟶implementedconsidered and rejectedM2​,M3​,M4​​​⟶justify

This module has already done exactly that several times — the three VaR methods disagreeing by 12.6% at 95%, or Vasicek versus CIR fitting the same data with mean-reversion speeds 100× apart. In both cases the choice between models mattered and could not be settled by fit alone. Writing down why you chose one is the documentation SR 11-7 is asking for.

Data​

Rigorous assessment of data quality and relevance is required.

The developer should be able to demonstrate that the data and information are suitable for the model and that they are consistent with the use case.

The lecturer's shorthand is the old one, and it is exactly right:

“Garbage In, Garbage Out”\textbf{“Garbage In, Garbage Out”}“Garbage In, Garbage Out”

When the data does not exist​

If data isn't available, data proxies are used — something with the same behaviour.

But a proxy is a substitution, and substitutions are where risk hides:

Proxies must be carefully identified, justified and documented.

Otherwise the proxy itself becomes a source of model risk — the model looks fine, and the thing it is actually measuring is not the thing you think.

Testing​

Testing various components of the model, and its overall functioning, is evaluated to determine whether the model is performing as intended or not.

And which tests apply depends entirely on what the model assumes:

Statistical tests depend on specific distribution assumptions.

For a linear regression model:

#Test
1Linearity — QQ plot
2Homoskedasticity
3Autocorrelation between residuals
4Multicollinearity — VIF
5Normality of residuals

For a time series model:

#Test
1Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test
2KPSS

For a Value at Risk model:

#Test
1Kupiec test
2Traffic Light Approach
3Christoffersen test
4Binomial test

Never rely on one test​

Different tests have different strengths and weaknesses under different conditions.

Do not rely on a single test.

Use a variety of tests to develop a sound model.

This is the exact gap Module 6 identified

The Kupiec note ended on a limitation: Kupiec counts breaches but cannot see whether they clustered. Three breaches spread across a year and three on consecutive days score identically — and the second is far more alarming.

Christoffersen's test is the one that closes that gap, by testing independence of breaches rather than just their number. Which is precisely why SR 11-7 lists all four together and says not to rely on any single one. The guidance is not being vague; it is describing a real blind spot that a second test covers.

Model use​

Models should be used only for the purpose for which they were designed and validated.

Black-Scholes⟶European option  ✓\text{Black-Scholes} \longrightarrow \text{European option} \;\checkmarkBlack-Scholes⟶European option✓

Misusing a model beyond its intended scope leads to incorrect decisions and heightened risk → model risk.

What is required of users​

Users must understand the assumptions, limitations and weaknesses of the model.

Users must interpret the model output correctly and critically.

This includes understanding the sensitivity of the result to input changes and assumptions.

Why the guidance cares about the reader, not just the model

The deck makes this concrete with a small scenario. A model reports VaR = $5M. Two people read that number:

Reads it as
User A (technical background)a potential loss at a stated confidence level
User Ba profit figure

Same output, opposite conclusions. The model was correct in both cases.

This is why "model risk" is defined as arising from incorrect or misused outputs. A validated model handed to someone who misreads it produces exactly the same adverse decision as a broken one — and no amount of validation prevents it. Only documentation, training and clear reporting do.

Summary​

  • MRM begins with development, implementation and use — validation is the second line of defence, not the first.
  • A sound build starts with a clear objective and documented design, theory and logic, supported by published research and industry practice, covering specification, numerical technique, approximations and limitations.
  • Comparison with alternative approaches is required — justify why this model and not another.
  • Data must be assessed for quality and relevance ("garbage in, garbage out"), and proxies must be identified, justified and documented.
  • Test against the model's own assumptions — regression diagnostics, ADF and KPSS for time series, four different backtests for VaR.
  • Never rely on a single test; different tests fail in different ways. Kupiec counts breaches, Christoffersen catches clustering.
  • Use the model only as designed, and users must understand its assumptions and interpret output critically — a correct model misread produces the same bad decision as a wrong one.

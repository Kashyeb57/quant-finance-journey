SR 11-7 — Overview of Model Risk

Every previous module in this course built a model. This one asks the question regulators started asking after 2008: how do you know the model is any good, and who is accountable when it is not?

What SR 11-7 is​

SR 11-7 is the Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management, issued jointly by the Federal Reserve Board (FRB) and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) in 2011.

Dated 4 April 2011, it is the standard reference for model risk management in US banking, and it is what a model validation team is measured against.

Why it was issued​

Lessons learned from the 2008 financial crisis.

Failures in model reliability during the financial crisis highlighted the need for robust model risk management practices.

Introduction​

Banks rely heavily on quantitative analysis and models for most of their decision making.

The guidance lists the range: underwriting credits, valuing exposures, measuring risk, managing client assets, determining capital and reserve adequacy, and many other activities.

The expanding use of models in all aspects of banking reflects the extent to which models can improve business decisions — but models also come with a cost.

CostWhat it is
Direct costthe resources spent building all the models
Indirect costthe consequences of relying on the outcomes of the model

And the indirect cost is the one that matters here:

Decision⟶model gives an incorrect result⟶banks have faced large financial losses\text{Decision} \longrightarrow \text{model gives an incorrect result} \longrightarrow \textbf{banks have faced large financial losses}Decision⟶model gives an incorrect result⟶banks have faced large financial losses

which is why it is important to actively manage model risk.

Section 2 — Purpose and scope​

The purpose of this document is to provide comprehensive guidance for banks on model risk management.

Rigorous model validation plays a critical role in MRM. However, sound development, implementation and use of the model are also vital elements.

That balance is the guidance's central posture, and it is worth stating plainly: validation is not a substitute for building the model properly. The chain runs:

Model development⏟build the model⟶Model validation⏟validate the same model⟶Approve the model⏟\underbrace{\text{Model development}}_{\text{build the model}} \longrightarrow \underbrace{\text{Model validation}}_{\text{validate the same model}} \longrightarrow \underbrace{\text{Approve the model}}_{}build the modelModel development​​⟶validate the same modelModel validation​​⟶Approve the model​​

with two further requirements on the far side: the user must know the model's usage, and the model must be implemented the right way.

Section 3 — What counts as a model​

The definition is precise, and it is broader than most people expect:

The term model refers to a quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories, techniques and assumptions to process input data into quantitative estimates.

The three components​

A model consists of three components.

#ComponentRole
1Information inputdelivers assumptions and data to the model
2Processingtransforms inputs into estimates
3Reportingtranslates the estimates into useful business information
Input⟶Processing⟶Output / Reporting\text{Input} \longrightarrow \text{Processing} \longrightarrow \text{Output / Reporting}Input⟶Processing⟶Output / Reporting
The definition is deliberately wide

Two consequences worth noticing.

A spreadsheet is a model. Nothing in the definition requires code, a database, or sophistication — the Black-Scholes sheet from Module 4 meets it in full.

Expert judgement can be an input. The guidance explicitly covers "quantitative approaches whose inputs are partially or wholly qualitative or based on expert judgment, provided that the output is quantitative in nature." So a scorecard driven by analyst opinion is still a model if it produces a number.

Models are simplifications​

Models are simplified representations of real-world relationships. Simplification is inevitable, due to the complexity of those relationships, but also intentional — to focus attention on the aspects considered most important for a given application.

Model quality can be measured in many ways: precision, accuracy, discriminatory power, robustness, stability and reliability, to name a few.

Which metric applies depends on the model's job:

Model typeRelevant metrics
Regression modelRMSE, MSE, MAE, MAPE
VaR modelTraffic Light Approach, Kupiec Test

That second row is the direct link back to Module 6: backtesting a VaR model is model validation, done by the standards this document sets.

Models are never perfect, and the appropriate metric of quality depends on the situation.

Model risk, and the two ways it arises​

The use of models invariably presents model risk, which is the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model outputs and reports.

It can lead to financial loss, poor business and strategic decision making, or damage to a bank's reputation.

Reason 1 — the model may have fundamental errors.

The model may have fundamental errors and may produce inaccurate output when viewed against the design objective and intended use.

Errors can enter anywhere along the chain:

  • the mathematical calculation and quantification
  • the choice of sample design
  • the selection of inputs and estimation
  • the implementation

Errors can occur at any point from design through implementation.

The lecturer's example is an implementation error you can see: the Vasicek model is drt=a(b−rt)dt+σ dWtdr_t = a(b - r_t)dt + \sigma\,dW_tdrt​=a(b−rt​)dt+σdWt​, but deployed as drt=a(b−rt)σ dt+σ dWtdr_t = a(b - r_t)\sigma\,dt + \sigma\,dW_tdrt​=a(b−rt​)σdt+σdWt​ — the theory is right and the code is not. Is the model deployed correctly? is a separate question from is the model correct?

Reason 2 — the model may be used incorrectly or inappropriately.

The example here is one this course has already built: Black-Scholes prices a European option. Point it at an American option and it will return a number — a wrong one, because the model assumes exercise only at maturity.

Black-Scholes→European  ✓Black-Scholes→American  ✗→model risk\text{Black-Scholes} \rightarrow \text{European} \;\checkmark \qquad \text{Black-Scholes} \rightarrow \text{American} \;✗ \rightarrow \textbf{model risk}Black-Scholes→European✓Black-Scholes→American✗→model risk

It is very important to understand the limitations of a model, to avoid using it in ways that are not consistent with the original intent.

That is the sharpest practical lesson in the whole document: a model does not fail loudly when misused. It returns a plausible number.

How model risk behaves​

  • Model risk should be managed like any other kind of risk — identified, measured, controlled.
  • Banks should identify the source of risk and assess the magnitude.
  • Model risk increases with greater model complexity.
  • Aggregate model risk is affected by interaction and dependencies among models — where one model's output is another's input, errors propagate:
M1→  output  M2→  output  decisionM_1 \xrightarrow{\;\text{output}\;} M_2 \xrightarrow{\;\text{output}\;} \text{decision}M1​output​M2​output​decision

It cannot be eliminated​

Even with skilled modelling and robust validation, model risk cannot be eliminated.

So the guidance turns to containment instead:

  • establish limits on model use
  • monitor model performance
  • adjust or revise the model over time
  • ad-hoc analysis — extra checks to make sure everything is correct

Monitoring runs on a defined cycle — monthly, quarterly, semi-annual, annual, or two-yearly — and the outcome is either to adjust the model or build a new one.

This is the same posture the hedging module took: you do not remove the risk, you decide how much of it to carry and check on it regularly.

Summary​

  • SR 11-7 is the Fed/OCC Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management (2011), issued after the 2008 crisis exposed failures in model reliability.
  • A model is any quantitative method processing input data into quantitative estimates, with three components: input, processing, reporting. The definition covers spreadsheets and expert-judgement inputs.
  • Models are deliberate simplifications and never perfect; quality metrics depend on the job — RMSE for a regression, traffic light and Kupiec for a VaR model.
  • Model risk is the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model outputs.
  • It arises two ways: fundamental errors (anywhere from design through implementation) and incorrect or inappropriate use (Black-Scholes on an American option).
  • It increases with complexity and compounds across linked models.
  • It cannot be eliminated — so it is contained with limits on use, monitoring, revision and ad-hoc analysis.

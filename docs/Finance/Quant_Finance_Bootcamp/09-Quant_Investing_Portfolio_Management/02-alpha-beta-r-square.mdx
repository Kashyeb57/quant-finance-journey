The previous note measured a portfolio **on its own terms**. These three
measure it **against a benchmark** — and all three come out of a single
regression of portfolio returns on market returns.

## Alpha

> **Alpha measures the excess return of an investment relative to a benchmark
> index.**

> It indicates whether the investment **outperformed or underperformed the
> market, after accounting for risk.**

| Alpha | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive** | The investment **outperformed the market** |
| **Negative** | The investment **underperformed the market** |
| **Zero** | The investment **performed in line with the market** |

Alpha is the part of the return the benchmark does not account for. If the S&P
500 returned 15% and your portfolio returned 15%, you produced no alpha —
whatever skill you applied, the market would have given you the same result.

## Beta

> **Beta measures the sensitivity of the investment return to the market's
> movement.**

| Beta | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $\beta > 1$ | The investment is **more volatile than the market** |
| $\beta < 1$ | The investment is **less volatile than the market** |
| $\beta = 1$ | The investment **moves in line with the market** |

**Worked**: a stock with $\beta = 1.2$ —

- when the market **rises by 10%**, the stock is expected to **rise by 12%**
- when the market **falls by 10%**, the stock is expected to **fall by 12%**

Beta cuts both ways. It is **amplification, not advantage** — a high-beta
portfolio in a rising market looks like skill and is not.

:::note Beta is the systematic risk from Module 6

This is the same quantity that
[Treynor's ratio](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Investing_Portfolio_Management/performance-ratios)
divides by in the next note, and conceptually the same split the
[variance-covariance VaR note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/var-variance-covariance-theory)
relied on: risk that comes from the market as a whole cannot be diversified
away, and risk specific to the holding can be.

Beta measures only the first kind.

:::

## R-squared

> **R-squared measures the percentage of an investment's movement that can be
> explained by the movement in the benchmark index.**

It comes from the same **linear regression** of portfolio returns against the
benchmark.

| $R^2$ | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **100%** (or 1) | The portfolio's returns are **fully explained by the benchmark** |
| **0** | The portfolio's returns **aren't explained by the benchmark at all** |
| **85%** (or 0.85) | **85% of the portfolio's returns are explained by the benchmark, while 15% are due to unique factors** |

$R^2$ is the honesty check on the other two. **A beta computed against a
benchmark that explains almost nothing is not telling you much** — the
regression line exists, but the relationship it describes is weak.

## The real regression

The workbook regresses the **50/50 JPM + MS portfolio** against **SPY** over
1,256 daily observations:

| Regression statistic | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Multiple R** | 0.768685 |
| **R Square** | **0.590877** |
| Adjusted R Square | 0.590551 |
| Standard Error | 0.013086 |
| **Observations** | **1,256** |

| Coefficient | Value | Standard error | t Stat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Intercept** | 1.000176 | 0.00037 | 2705.66 |
| **X Variable 1 (beta)** | **1.188091** | 0.027918 | **42.56** |

Recomputed independently from the underlying return columns: **β = 1.1881,
R² = 0.5911, correlation = 0.7689** — matching the sheet to three decimals (the
small difference is a two-row range discrepancy).

### Reading the results

**β = 1.1881.** The portfolio is **19% more volatile than the market.** When SPY
moves 10%, this portfolio is expected to move about 11.9%. That is unsurprising
for two investment banks — they are levered to the economic cycle in a way the
broad index is not.

**R² = 0.5909.** **59% of the portfolio's movement is explained by SPY**, and
**41% comes from factors specific to JPM and MS** — bank-sector news, earnings,
regulation. So the benchmark explains most of the story but nowhere near all of
it.

**α = 0.0176% per day.**

:::note Where the intercept of 1.0002 comes from

The regression intercept is **1.000176**, not the near-zero you would expect,
and the sheet then computes alpha as `=(Y51-1)*100`.

The reason: the regression was run with **(1 + portfolio return)** as the
dependent variable against the **market return** as the independent one. So

$$
1 + r_p = a + \beta r_m
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
r_p = (a - 1) + \beta r_m
$$

which makes the true alpha $a - 1 = 0.000176$. Verified directly:
regressing $r_p$ on $r_m$ gives an intercept of **0.000167**, and $1 + 0.000167
= 1.000167$ against the sheet's 1.000176.

It is a valid setup, just an unusual one — and it means **the intercept must
have 1 subtracted before it means anything.** Reading 1.000176 as "alpha" would
be wildly wrong.

:::

:::warning Alpha is daily; everything beside it is annualised

The summary table reports **α = 0.0176%** alongside an **annualised return of
23.03%**, **annualised volatility of 32.47%** and a Sharpe ratio built from
annual figures.

But that alpha is a **daily** number. Annualised it is

$$
0.00017612 \times 252 = 0.0444 = \mathbf{4.44\%}
$$

which is a materially different claim — 4.44% of annual outperformance, not
0.018%. The ratios in the
[next note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Investing_Portfolio_Management/performance-ratios)
are internally consistent (all annual); **alpha is the one figure left in daily
units** while sitting in an annual table.

Worth knowing which convention a reported alpha is in before comparing it to
anything.

:::

## Putting the three together

| Measure | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Alpha** | Did we beat the benchmark? |
| **Beta** | How much market risk did we take to do it? |
| **R²** | Is the benchmark even the right comparison? |

Read alone, each is misleading. A high alpha with a beta of 1.9 is mostly
leverage. A confident beta with an R² of 0.1 is a number computed against an
irrelevant index. **The three are one answer, not three.**

## Summary

- **Alpha** is **excess return relative to a benchmark** — positive means
  outperformance, negative underperformance, zero means in line.
- **Beta** is **sensitivity to market movement**: $\beta > 1$ more volatile,
  $\beta < 1$ less, $\beta = 1$ in line. A **β of 1.2 moves 12% when the market
  moves 10%** — in both directions.
- **R²** is the **percentage of movement explained by the benchmark**; the
  remainder is **unique factors.**
- On the real JPM/MS portfolio against SPY: **β = 1.1881** (19% more volatile
  than the market), **R² = 0.5909** (59% explained, 41% bank-specific), and
  **α = 0.0176% daily**, all independently verified.
- The **intercept is 1.000176 because (1 + rₚ) was regressed on r_m** — subtract
  1 to get alpha.
- **That alpha is daily while the rest of the table is annualised**; annualised
  it is **4.44%.**

---
title: Sharpe, Sortino and Treynor Ratios
description: 'Three risk-adjusted return measures with the same numerator and three different denominators — total risk, downside risk, and market risk. Worked on the real portfolio, and the closing note of the bootcamp.'
---

Return alone says nothing. **23% earned by taking enormous risk is not the same
achievement as 23% earned quietly**, and these three ratios are how that
difference is made visible.

All three share the same numerator — **excess return** — and differ only in what
they divide it by.

## 1 — Sharpe ratio

> The Sharpe ratio measures the **excess return earned per unit of total risk.**

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Sharpe ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}\;}
$$

| | |
| --- | --- |
| $R_p$ | **Portfolio return** |
| $R_f$ | **Risk-free rate** |
| $\sigma_p$ | **Standard deviation of the portfolio** |

The numerator $R_p - R_f$ is the **excess return** — what you earned *above*
what you could have had risk-free. There is no credit for matching the Treasury
rate.

> A **higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted return.**

| Sharpe | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| **> 1** | **Good investment** — generating decent return relative to the risk |
| **< 1** | **Bad investment** — poor; the return you are generating **does not justify the level of risk you are taking** |

The notes' illustration: a portfolio returning **12%** with **20% volatility**
has a Sharpe below 1 — you are being paid 12 for carrying 20.

## 2 — Sortino ratio

> The Sortino ratio **refines the Sharpe ratio by considering only the downside
> risk** — negative volatility.

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Sortino ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_d}\;}
$$

where $\sigma_d$ is the
[**downside volatility**](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Investing_Portfolio_Management/returns-volatility-correlation).

> **This metric is more significant for risk-averse investors.**

> A **high Sortino ratio indicates better return relative to the downside
> risk.**
>
> **Reason: it penalises the investment only for negative deviation from the
> threshold.**

That is the whole argument for it. Sharpe's denominator punishes a portfolio for
going *up* unexpectedly, which no investor actually minds. Sortino removes that
penalty.

## 3 — Treynor ratio

> The Treynor ratio measures the **excess return per unit of systematic risk
> (beta).**

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Treynor ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p}\;}
$$

where $\beta_p$ is the
[**portfolio beta**](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Investing_Portfolio_Management/alpha-beta-r-square).

> **Goal: the Treynor ratio focuses on how well the portfolio compensates the
> investor for market risk rather than total risk.**

The distinction matters for anyone holding more than one portfolio. Risk
specific to a holding can be diversified away across a wider book; **market risk
cannot.** So for a portfolio that is one sleeve of something larger, market risk
is the only risk that genuinely survives — and Treynor is the ratio that prices
it.

## The three side by side

The deck closes with this comparison, and it is the cleanest summary of the
module:

| Ratio | Risk measured | Formula | Denominator is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sharpe** | **Total risk** ($\sigma$) | $\dfrac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma}$ | Standard deviation |
| **Sortino** | **Downside risk** ($\sigma_d$) | $\dfrac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_d}$ | Downside standard deviation |
| **Treynor** | **Market risk** ($\beta$) | $\dfrac{R_p - R_f}{\beta}$ | Beta |

**Same numerator, three denominators.** Choosing between them is choosing which
risk you think the investor actually bears.

## The real portfolio

Computed on the **50/50 JPM + MS** portfolio, with a **risk-free rate of
4.323%**:

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Annualised return $R_p$ | **23.031%** |
| Risk-free rate $R_f$ | **4.323%** |
| **Excess return** | **18.708%** |
| Annualised volatility $\sigma$ | 32.465% |
| Annualised downside deviation $\sigma_d$ | 21.608% |
| Beta $\beta$ | 1.188091 |

$$
\text{Sharpe} = \frac{0.23031 - 0.04323}{0.324653} = \mathbf{0.5762}
$$

$$
\text{Sortino} = \frac{0.23031 - 0.04323}{0.216081} = \mathbf{0.8658}
$$

$$
\text{Treynor} = \frac{0.23031 - 0.04323}{1.188091} = \mathbf{0.1575}
$$

All three reproduce when recomputed independently from the return series.

### What they say

**Sharpe of 0.576 is below 1** — by the deck's own rule of thumb, the return
does not justify the total risk taken. 23% sounds excellent until you notice it
came with 32% volatility.

**Sortino of 0.866 is markedly higher than Sharpe** — a 50% improvement. That
gap is informative: it means **a large share of this portfolio's volatility was
upside**, which Sharpe penalised and Sortino does not. Downside deviation
(21.6%) is only two-thirds of total volatility (32.5%).

**Treynor of 0.158 is not comparable to the other two** — its denominator is a
beta, not a percentage, so the number lives on a different scale entirely.
Treynor is only meaningful **compared against another portfolio's Treynor**,
never against a Sharpe.

:::note One number to check before you compare anything

These three ratios are internally consistent — all use **annualised** return,
volatility and risk-free rate.

But as the [previous note](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Investing_Portfolio_Management/alpha-beta-r-square)
flagged, **alpha in the same summary table is left in daily units** (0.0176%,
or 4.44% annualised). Mixing conventions inside one table is the kind of quiet
error that survives review, because every individual number looks reasonable.

It is precisely what
[SR 11-7's process verification](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Model_Risk_SR_11_7/model-validation)
is meant to catch.

:::

## Module 9, and the bootcamp, in closing

This module measured investments; the previous eight built the things being
measured. The through-line across all nine is narrower than it looks:

$$
\textbf{return is only meaningful once divided by the risk taken to get it}
$$

- **Modules 1-3** priced derivatives and hedged the Greeks — managing risk one
  exposure at a time.
- **Modules 4-5** modelled the processes underneath, and found that
  [three pricing methods agree to within a cent](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Quant_Modeling_Stochastic_Processes/monte-carlo-barrier-option)
  when built correctly.
- **Module 6** put a number on the tail, and showed
  [what VaR refuses to tell you](/docs/Finance/Quant_Finance_Bootcamp/Value_at_Risk/expected-shortfall-theory).
- **Module 7** valued the swaps that convert one risk into another.
- **Module 8** asked who is accountable when the model is wrong.
- **Module 9** asks, finally, whether any of it was worth doing — and answers
  with a ratio.

## Summary

- All three ratios share the numerator **excess return $R_p - R_f$** and differ
  only in the denominator.
- **Sharpe** divides by **total risk** ($\sigma$): **above 1 is good, below 1
  means the return does not justify the risk.**
- **Sortino** divides by **downside risk** ($\sigma_d$), penalising **only
  negative deviation from the threshold** — better suited to **risk-averse
  investors.**
- **Treynor** divides by **beta**, pricing **market risk rather than total
  risk** — the right measure when the portfolio sits inside a larger,
  diversified book.
- On the real portfolio: **Sharpe 0.576, Sortino 0.866, Treynor 0.158**, from a
  23.03% return against a 4.323% risk-free rate.
- **Sortino exceeding Sharpe by 50%** shows much of the volatility was
  **upside** — which Sharpe wrongly penalised.
- **Treynor is not comparable to Sharpe or Sortino** — different denominator,
  different scale.

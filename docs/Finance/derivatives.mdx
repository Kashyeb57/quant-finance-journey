# Derivatives

Forwards, futures, options, and swaps — the core derivative instruments and how they are priced and used.

A **derivative** is a contract whose value *derives* from something else (a stock, an index, a rate).
The fastest way to build intuition is to play with payoffs — so play:

## 1. Option payoffs, hands-on

Pick a strategy, drag the strike and premium, and watch how the profit/loss profile changes.
Green is where you make money at expiry, red is where you lose it.

<PayoffDiagram />

A few things to try:

- **Long Call** — slide the premium up. Notice the breakeven moves right: the stock must rally *further* just to cover what you paid.
- **Short Put** — this is how "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller" looks: small capped profit, big downside.
- **Long Straddle** — you don't care about direction, only *movement*. Both tails are green.
- **Bull Call Spread** — selling the higher strike finances the lower one, but caps your upside.

## 2. Pricing: the Black–Scholes lab

Payoff diagrams show value *at expiry*. Before expiry an option's price is usually above its
intrinsic value — that extra is **time value**. Black–Scholes prices **European** options, which
can only be exercised at expiry:

$$
C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad P = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S\,N(-d_1)
$$

$$
d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}
$$

Here $S$ is the spot price, $K$ the strike, $T$ the time to expiry in years, $r$ the risk-free
rate, $\sigma$ the volatility, and $N$ the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

:::note Time value can be negative

"Usually" matters. A deep in-the-money European **put** can be worth *less* than its intrinsic
value. Switch the lab to Put with S = 50, K = 100, T = 1, σ = 20% and r = 5%: it prices the put at
about **\$45.13**, below its **\$50** intrinsic value. The holder cannot exercise early, so they
wait a year to receive the strike, and money received later is worth less today. An American put
could be exercised immediately for \$50, so it never trades below intrinsic value.

:::

<BlackScholesLab />

Try these experiments:

1. Slide **T → 0** and watch the blue curve collapse onto the dashed hockey stick — time value melting is *theta decay*.
2. Double **σ** — the option gets more valuable even though the spot never moved. Options are long volatility.
3. Set the spot deep in-the-money and check **Delta**. A call's delta approaches **+1**: it starts behaving like the stock itself. A put's approaches **−1**: it behaves like a short position in the stock.

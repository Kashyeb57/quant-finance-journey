Jane Street’s $15 billion month

July 2026 · the most profitable firm on Wall Street has its first losing month in a decade

← All Big Events
Post-mortemCompiled August 2026 · ~12 min read · by Joyeb Kashyeb

Jane Street is the quiet colossus of modern markets — a secretive quant firm that out-earns every bank trading desk on Earth. In July 2026 it did something it hadn’t done in roughly a decade: it lost money. About $15 billion of it. The loss didn’t come from the market-making machine that made it famous. It came from its other side — the hedge-fund-style directional bets it has quietly grown, including a stake in the same Situational Awareness fund that got wiped out in the very same storm.

The short version

Jane Street makes markets — it quotes buy and sell prices across thousands of instruments and earns the spread, mostly market-neutral. But it has increasingly added longer-term directional bets “in line with a hedge fund” (its own words). In July 2026 that hedge-fund side got caught in the AI-infrastructure rout: its AI-adjacent longs fell, its bets in Asian equities went the wrong way, its stake in the leveraged Situational Awareness fund was marked down as that fund was force-sold to Citadel, and its put-option hedges paid little against a slow, month-long grind rather than one clean crash.

The result was a ~$15B loss — among the largest single-firm trading losses ever. And yet: Jane Street barely flinched. It funds itself with ~$45B of its own capital (no outside investors to redeem, no prime broker to margin-call it), 2026 is still its best year ever at $40B+ in net trading revenue, and within days it sold $14.6B of bonds to eager buyers. Same July storm as Situational Awareness — opposite ending. The difference wasn’t the thesis. It was the structure: scale, diversification, and capital that can’t be yanked away.

Terms used on this page
Market maker
A firm that continuously quotes both a buy and a sell price and earns the spread between them, providing liquidity. Done well it is close to market-neutral — it makes money on volume and flow, not on the market going up or down. This is Jane Street’s core business.
Proprietary (prop) trading
Trading the firm’s own capital for its own profit, rather than executing for clients. Jane Street is a prop firm: the money at risk is its own.
Directional bet
A position that only profits if the market moves a particular way. Unlike market-making, it takes a view. This is the “hedge-fund side” of Jane Street — and where the July loss came from.
Members’ equity
The partners’ own capital retained inside the firm (~$45B). Jane Street funds its trading from this instead of outside investors — which means no external LP can pull money and no prime broker can margin-call the firm out of a position.
Put option
The right to sell an asset at a fixed price — insurance against a fall. It pays off in a sharp crash, but bleeds value (theta) and pays little in a slow, grinding decline. That gap is why Jane Street’s hedges “offered limited protection” in July.
Net trading revenue
The headline metric for a trading firm: trading gains minus losses and costs. Jane Street’s $39.6B in 2025 was the largest of any firm on Wall Street.
Situational Awareness
The ~4×-levered AI hedge fund (run by ex-OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner) that Jane Street had invested in. It blew up in the same July rout and was force-sold to Citadel — the subject of a separate post-mortem on this site.
Margin call
A lender’s demand for more collateral against a falling position. It is what destroyed Situational Awareness — and precisely the mechanism Jane Street’s all-internal capital structure is built to never face.

1. The black box that out-earns every bank

Founded in 2000, Jane Street is the most profitable trading firm almost nobody outside finance has heard of. With only ~3,500 employees, it makes markets across equities, bonds, options and ETFs on more than 200 exchanges in ~45 countries, and it dominates ETF liquidity in particular. It is famously secretive, notoriously quantitative, and staffed by people who talk about probability the way other firms talk about quarterly targets.

The numbers are almost hard to believe. In 2025 it booked $39.6 billion in net trading revenue — more than Citadel Securities (~$12.2B) and Hudson River Trading (~$12.3B) combined, and more than JPMorgan’s entire trading division (~$35.8B). That works out to roughly $11M of revenue per employee. Since 2016 its members’ equity — the partners’ own capital, which funds the trading — has grown nearly 2,000%, to about $45 billion. It takes no outside investors.

It is also no stranger to controversy. In July 2025 India’s regulator SEBI barred the firm from Indian markets and ordered it to disgorge ~₹4,844 crore (~$565M) — its largest impounding ever — over alleged index-options manipulation. Jane Street disputed the finding, parked the money, and had access restored. The episode is a useful backdrop for July 2026: this is a firm large and sophisticated enough to absorb blows that would end almost anyone else.

2. The two Jane Streets

To understand the loss you have to separate two businesses living under one roof. The first is market-making: quoting a bid and an ask on thousands of instruments and pocketing the spread. Done at Jane Street’s scale and speed, it is close to market-neutral — a money machine that runs on volume and volatility, not on calling the market’s direction.

The second is newer and quieter: directional bets. In its own bond-offering language, Jane Street described its strategy as “evolving to also include longer-term bets, in line with a hedge fund.” That is the part of the firm that actually takes a view — that AI infrastructure will keep compounding, that a basket of Asian equities will move a certain way. For years, that hedge-fund side printed money alongside the market-making. In July 2026 it was the source of the entire loss.

3. What happened in July

July 2026 was the month the AI-infrastructure trade cracked. SK Hynix’s U.S. listing and Meta’s “Meta Compute” reveal set off a rout across memory, power and the small “neocloud” GPU-rental names. Jane Street’s directional book was exposed to it on multiple fronts at once:

  • AI-adjacent longs fell with the broad AI-infrastructure complex.
  • Asian equities — a set of non-AI directional positions — moved against the firm at the same time.
  • Its stake in the leveraged Situational Awareness fund was marked down hard as that fund was force-sold (more below).
  • The put-option hedges meant to cover the longs paid only a fraction, because the decline came as a slow grind rather than a single sharp crash the options were priced for.

Add it up and the firm lost about $15 billion over the month — roughly $650 million per trading day. For a firm that had gone ~10 years without a single losing month, that is a genuine shock. It is also, in the same breath, a number the firm could afford.

4. The Situational Awareness link

This event doesn’t stand alone. Jane Street was an investor in Situational Awareness LP — the ~4×-levered AI hedge fund run by former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner that, in this very same July, was margin-called by three prime brokers and forced to sell its entire public book to Citadel in a single block trade, ending the month down ~67%.

So the two stories are the same storm seen from two altitudes. For Situational Awareness, the rout was terminal: concentrated, leveraged, and unable to meet the margin calls. For Jane Street, the same rout was a line item — painful, but its stake reportedly finished the year roughly flat and remained profitable over its life. One fund died; its backer took a bruise. That contrast is the entire lesson, and it’s worth reading the two pages side by side.

5. The hedge that didn’t — a familiar flaw

One detail rhymes exactly with the Situational Awareness blow-up. Jane Street was hedged — it held put options against its exposures. But puts are built for a crash: they pay off spectacularly when the market gaps down in a hurry. July wasn’t a gap; it was a slow, grinding, month-long decline. Against that, options bleed their time value while the underlying drips lower, so the insurance “offered limited protection” exactly when it was supposed to work.

It is the same family of error that turned a bad month into a catastrophe for Situational Awareness: a hedge that looks like insurance on paper but pays pennies against the specific way the loss actually arrives. The difference is that Jane Street’s hedge underperforming cost it a slice of a record year, not its existence.

6. Why a $15B loss didn’t kill them

The whole point of this post-mortem is the part that didn’t happen: no collapse, no fire sale, no margin call. Set the loss against the numbers that absorbed it and the reason is obvious.

This is the quiet superpower of being your own bank. Situational Awareness died because other people’s money — prime-broker loans — could be recalled at the worst possible moment. Jane Street can’t be margin-called out of a position, because nobody is lending it the position in the first place. When you can choose whether and when to sell, a $15B loss is a bad month. When you can’t, a far smaller loss is the end.

7. Among the biggest trading losses in history

Stripped of context, ~$15 billion in one month from one firm is staggering — it dwarfs the trading disasters that have their own Wikipedia pages and Hollywood treatments. What sets it apart isn’t just the size; it’s that the firm survived it comfortably.

8. The response — and a $14.6B vote of confidence

The firm’s public tone was matter-of-fact. Partner Turner Batty put it plainly:

🗣️ Partner statementTurner Batty, Jane Street · mid-August 2026
“July was a bad month.”
“We’ve closed a significant portion of our risk in the specific areas we lost on in July, and have also reduced risk-taking in other strategies.”

As reported by Bloomberg and others citing the firm in August 2026.

The market’s verdict was even more telling. In the very week the loss was reported, Jane Street sold $14.6 billion of bonds — led by JPMorgan, with PIMCO, Capital Group and Fidelity among the buyers — to refinance floating-rate debt and reorganize its ~$11B capital stack. A firm that just posted the largest trading loss in memory was met not with a run, but with demand for its paper. Nothing signals “this was a dent, not a wound” more clearly than that.

9. Same storm, two fates

The cleanest way to see the lesson is to put the two July casualties next to each other. They were hit by the identical event — the AI-infrastructure sell-off — and even shared a position (Jane Street was in Situational Awareness). Their outcomes could not have been more different, and the reason is entirely structural.

Why it matters — the lessons

  • Market-making isn’t risk-free once you bolt a hedge fund onto it. Jane Street’s loss came entirely from its directional “hedge-fund side,” not the neutral market-making that built it. The moment you take a view, you inherit the view’s tail risk.
  • Structure decides survival, not conviction. Situational Awareness and Jane Street held the same kind of trade in the same month. One is gone and one shrugged. The difference was leverage, diversification, and internal capital — not who was smarter about AI.
  • Be your own bank if you can. Funding trading from ~$45B of your own equity means no redemptions and no margin calls. Nobody can force you to sell at the bottom, which converts “terminal” into “temporary.”
  • Puts hedge crashes, not grinds. Option insurance pays for a sudden drop and bleeds against a slow one. Both firms learned in July that a hedge only works against the specific shape of loss it’s built for.
  • Absolute size is meaningless without context. $15B is one of the largest trading losses in history and a rounding item against a record year. A loss only matters relative to the capital and income that stand behind it.

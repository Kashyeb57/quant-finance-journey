The Situational Awareness fund collapse

July 2026 · Anatomy of a six-day, ~4×-levered blow-up

← All Big Events
Post-mortemCompiled August 2026 · ~15 min read · by Joyeb Kashyeb

The person who wrote the most influential essay on AI’s trajectory built a hedge fund on exactly that thesis, compounded it at +439%, and then lost roughly two-thirds of it in six trading days. The thesis wasn’t what failed. The structure was: extreme concentration, ~4× leverage, and hedges that weren’t really hedges.

The short version

Leopold Aschenbrenner — the 24-year-old ex-OpenAI researcher behind the viral “Situational Awareness” essay — ran a hedge fund that made a concentrated, heavily leveraged bet on the picks-and-shovels of the AI boom: memory, power, and the small “neocloud” GPU-rental companies. It worked spectacularly, returning a reported +439% and ballooning from ~$225M to a reported $20–45B.

In July 2026 the AI-infrastructure trade cracked. Because the book was ~4× leveraged and packed into a handful of correlated names — while its “hedges” were built on different stocks that didn’t fall in step — a ~30% drop in the longs became a fund-ending loss. Three prime brokers called for collateral on the same day, and when a rescue raise failed, the whole public book was sold to Citadel in a single block. The stocks bounced 20–30% the moment the forced seller was gone — proof it was a liquidity blow-up, not a verdict on AI.

Terms used on this page
Gross leverage
The size of your total positions divided by your own capital. At ~4× (≈400%), $1 of investor money controls ~$4 of market exposure — so every move is felt four times over.
Margin call
When a prime broker demands more collateral because a leveraged position is losing. The fund can’t refuse and doesn’t control the timing or the exit price.
Prime broker
The bank that lends a hedge fund money and custodies its assets. Situational Awareness used three: Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Bank of America.
Neocloud
A new breed of GPU-rental company (CoreWeave, IREN, Nebius) that buys Nvidia chips and rents out AI compute. Small, richly valued, and highly sensitive to the AI-capex narrative.
Beta hedge
A hedge built from a related-but-different instrument (e.g. shorting large-cap semis to protect small-cap “neocloud” longs). It only works if the two move together — which, in a panic, they often don’t.
13F
A quarterly SEC filing that discloses a fund’s U.S. long stock positions — the window through which outsiders reconstructed this book.
Block trade
A single, privately negotiated sale of an entire position or portfolio to one buyer, done off-exchange to avoid crashing the price on the open market.
Illiquid
An asset you can’t sell quickly for a fair price — like a private stake in Anthropic. Worth a lot on paper; useless for meeting a margin call today.

1. The man and the manifesto

Aschenbrenner is a German-born prodigy (born ~2001) who graduated top of his class at Columbia at 19. He joined OpenAI in 2023 on the “Superalignment” team — the group tasked with keeping smarter-than-human AI under control. OpenAI fired him in April 2024, citing an alleged information leak; he maintains the real reason was a memo warning that OpenAI wasn’t secure against foreign espionage.

In June 2024 he published “Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead” — a ~165-page argument that AI would hit human level around 2027, trigger an intelligence explosion, and become the central national-security issue of the decade. It was everywhere. Then he did the rare thing an essayist almost never does: he put billions of (mostly borrowed) dollars behind the thesis.

2. The fund and the run

Situational Awareness LP launched in late 2024 with a reported ~$225 million, backed by Stripe’s Patrick and John Collison, Daniel Gross and Nat Friedman. Its strategy was a concentrated wager on the AI supply chain, and for eighteen months it was one of the best-performing funds on the planet: a reported +439% net return through June 30, 2026, with assets swelling to a reported $20–45 billion.

3. The book: concentration as a feature — then a bug

The fund’s edge was conviction, and its Q1-2026 13F showed just how much: a $1.86 billion disclosed long book across only 26 names, with the top five making up ~76%. This wasn’t a diversified AI fund; it was a handful of very large, very correlated bets on memory, power, and the small GPU-rental “neoclouds.”

Owning 8% of a company’s float is easy to buy and brutal to sell: there simply isn’t anyone on the other side when you need to exit in a hurry. Concentration is what produced the +439%. It’s also what made an orderly exit impossible.

4. The hedges that weren’t

Aschenbrenner wasn’t naked-long — he thought he was protected. Against the longs he ran a large short/optioned book: roughly $8.5 billion in put options across Nvidia, AMD, ASML, TSMC, Broadcom, Oracle and Micron, about $3.0B notional in puts on the SMH semiconductor ETF, ~$1.6B against Nvidia, and software shorts like Adobe.

The flaw was subtle but fatal. His longs were small, high-volatility neoclouds and miners; his hedges were on large-cap semis and software. That’s a beta hedge across two different volatility regimes. When the AI complex sold off, the small, fragile longs fell far more than the big, liquid names he was short — so the hedge recovered only a fraction of the loss, while the software shorts moved against him at the same time. A “long/short double whammy.” The insurance paid pennies on the dollar exactly when it was needed most.

5. Leverage: the multiplier in both directions

6. The trigger: July’s AI-infrastructure crack

The unwind had two fuses. On July 10, SK Hynix — one of the largest longs — listed in the U.S., which set off an unwind of crowded, leveraged Korean equity positions; Korea’s Kospi eventually shed about a third. Then on July 17, Meta unveiled “Meta Compute,” and the market panicked that hyperscalers would steamroll the small neocloud renters at the heart of the fund’s book.

The damage compounded fast. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell ~28.6% from its June peak; a momentum-tech basket dropped over 50%; the Nasdaq-100 lost 10%+. The fund’s own names cratered — the core longs each fell 27–54% from their June peaks to the July 29 forced-sale lows. Underneath it all was a genuine question investors had started asking out loud: will the hundreds of billions being poured into AI infrastructure actually turn into near-term profits?

7. Six days: the letter and the margin calls

What makes this a classic isn’t just the loss — it’s the timing. On July 24, already deep in the red, Aschenbrenner sent investors a letter framing the crash as “one of the best buying opportunities in over a year” and invited fresh capital starting August 1. He was doubling down, not de-risking. The new money never came.

On July 29, the core holdings printed fresh yearly lows, and all three prime brokers — Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Bank of America — issued margin calls on the same day. That’s the moment control leaves the building: once the banks want collateral, the fund no longer decides when or at what price it sells. A scramble for rescue capital and lenders (described as ad-hoc, not coordinated) failed, and even an offer of the prized Anthropic stake to investors couldn’t plug the hole in time.

7½. The letters: five days, two completely different realities

There were two letters to investors. Reading them back to back is the most concise possible education in what leverage does to time horizons. The first was written five days before the margin calls. The second was written the day after the forced sale.

📩 Investor letterJuly 24, 2026 — five days before the margin calls

With the fund already deep in the red, Aschenbrenner wrote to his investors framing the sell-off not as a crisis but as an opportunity. He called it:

“One of the best buying opportunities in over a year” — specifically, the best since April 2025.

He was not reducing risk. He was doubling down, asking investors to contribute fresh capital starting August 1. The fundraise never happened — the margin calls arrived on July 29, five days later. There was no August 1.

This is not evidence that Aschenbrenner was incompetent. His AI thesis may well prove correct over years. The letter is evidence of something more dangerous: at extreme leverage, even a correct thesis can be timed out of existence. You can be right and still not survive to collect.

Source: reported by Reuters, CNBC, Inc. and others citing the investor communication dated July 24, 2026.

📩 Investor letterJuly 31, 2026 — one day after the forced sale to Citadel

After the entire public book had been sold to Citadel and the fund had finished July down 67%, Aschenbrenner sent a second letter. The tone could not have been more different:

“We let you down this month. We came closer to permanent capital impairment than is acceptable to us.”
“While we ultimately found a solution that protected the fund and you as investors, our intention in running the fund is to never find ourselves in such a position in the first place.”
“Volatility is the price of long-term investment returns. Over the past two years, we have delivered outstanding results, despite occasional sharp pullbacks. But our fund must always be structured such that we can take a loss and fight another day. I will make it my mission to ensure that we learn the necessary lessons from this experience.”

He took full responsibility, pledged to stop borrowing from banks to amplify public-market bets, and committed to restructuring the fund’s risk management. He noted the fund remained up roughly +80% for 2026 due to the first half’s gains — a number that gives some context to the damage but does not undo the six-day experience for anyone who joined closer to the peak.

Direct quotes as reported by Business Insider, Financial Express and others citing the investor communication dated July 31, 2026. The full letter has not been publicly released; these are excerpts reported by news outlets.

The five-day gap between those two letters is the whole story in miniature. Conviction, leverage, and a wrong estimate of how much time you have left are a combination that even the most accurate thesis cannot survive.

8. The exit: one block, one buyer

Before the open on July 30, the fund sold its entire public book — longs and shorts together — in a single negotiated block to Ken Griffin’s Citadel (per the Wall Street Journal). Selling it piecemeal on the open market would have flooded the tape with 8% of Core Scientific’s float and other outsized positions, overwhelming buyers and triggering sympathetic selling across the whole AI-infra complex. One block to one deep-pocketed buyer contained the damage — a notably cleaner unwind than the chaotic, multi-bank fire sale that defined Archegos in 2021.

9. The tell: the bounce

Here is the most revealing part. The instant the forced seller was gone, the exact same stocks ripped higher — by 3 p.m. that day Nebius was +27%, IREN +26%, Bloom Energy +26%, SanDisk +25%, CoreWeave +22%. Even names the fund never owned (Cipher Mining +28%, Hut 8 +22%) jumped in sympathy. Meanwhile large-caps like Nvidia — which the fund was short as a hedge — barely twitched (+1.9%). The rebound kept going: by August 3, CoreWeave was +41% and IREN +36% off their July-29 lows.

That gap between the forced-seller price and where willing owners valued the same shares a day later is the whole lesson in one number. The market didn’t decide AI infrastructure was worthless. A single over-levered holder had to sell into a vacuum, and price briefly detached from value. The fund didn’t die because it was wrong; it died because it couldn’t survive being early.

10. The aftermath

July closed with the fund down about 67%. What survived was largely illiquid: a private stake in Anthropic reported around $5 billion, acquired earlier in 2026 — valuable, but impossible to sell into a margin call. The firm began trying to raise fresh capital on August 1. The cruel irony wrote itself: the man who literally authored “Situational Awareness” was undone by a blind spot in his own — the tail risk hiding inside his leverage and his hedges.

11. The version that survives: how a risk desk would have caught it

None of this required hindsight. Every guardrail that would have saved the fund is standard practice on any institutional risk desk. The thesis didn’t have to change — only the sizing.

  • Concentration limits. A hard cap of ~10% gross per name and ~35% across the top five forces real diversification. SA ran its top five at ~76% and held 8% of one company’s float — a position you cannot exit without moving the price against yourself.
  • Liquidity-adjusted leverage. Leverage should scale to how fast you can get out. 4× on small, thinly-traded neoclouds is a different animal from 4× on mega-caps. A liquidity-weighted limit would have pinned this book closer to ~2×.
  • Correlation-aware hedging. Hedge with the thing you actually own. Shorting low-beta mega-cap semis to cover high-beta small-caps is a hedge ratio well under 1 — it was always going to pay pennies in a panic.
  • Stress tests, not just averages. One scenario — “AI complex −30%, correlations → 1” — run at 4× prints a >100% equity loss. That is a number a risk committee vetoes before the trade is ever put on.

The counterfactual: at 2× instead of 4×, that same ~30% drop is roughly a 60% gross hit — brutal, but with the hedges clawing part of it back, survivable. No margin call forces the door; you live to see the very next day, when the same names bounced 20–30%. Halving the leverage doesn’t just halve the loss — it changes the outcome from terminal to temporary. That is the whole game: size so that being early is survivable.

Why it matters — the lessons

  • Being right isn’t enough; you have to survive to collect. The AI thesis may be entirely correct over a decade and still wipe you out over a week, because leverage sets a clock the market doesn’t care about. Solvency, not accuracy, decides who’s still standing.
  • Leverage is perfectly symmetric. The +439% and the −67% came off the same ~4× dial. There is no version of the strategy that keeps the upside and skips the downside.
  • A hedge is only a hedge if it’s truly correlated. Shorting large-cap semis to protect small-cap neoclouds looked like insurance and behaved like a second losing bet when the panic hit. Different volatility regimes don’t offset.
  • Concentration and liquidity are the same risk wearing two hats. Owning 8% of a company’s float is wonderful until you must exit — then there’s no one to sell to except at a crushing discount.
  • Margin calls, not managers, choose the exit. Once three prime brokers call on the same morning, timing and price are out of your hands.
  • Forced-seller prices aren’t fair value. The 20–30% same-day bounce proved the crash was a plumbing failure, not a fundamental verdict.

It rhymes with history. Long-Term Capital Management (1998) — run by Nobel laureates — blew up on enormous leverage and correlated bets that all went wrong together. Archegos (2021) vaporized $20 B in days on concentrated, levered swaps. Different decade, same physics: brilliance + leverage + concentration, detonated by a move the model assumed couldn’t happen.

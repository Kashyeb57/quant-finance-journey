The KOSPI leverage crash

Summer 2026 · How single-stock leveraged ETFs turned a 116% boom into a 38.6% bust

← All Big Events
Post-mortemCompiled August 2026 · ~12 min read · by Joyeb Kashyeb

South Korea spent a year cheering its citizens into the stock market. Then it handed them a loaded weapon: single-stock leveraged ETFs, stacked on top of record margin debt and pointed at just two chip stocks. The KOSPI ran +116% to an all-time high — and then fell 38.6% in five weeks, force-liquidating roughly 360,000 retail accounts. The thesis (an AI-memory boom) was real. The plumbing — leverage on leverage, aimed at a two-stock index — is what detonated.

The short version

Through 2025 and early 2026, a genuine boom in AI-memory chips (the high-bandwidth memory that feeds AI data centres) — plus a government “value-up” campaign that openly nudged households into equities — sent the KOSPI vertical. Retail investors didn’t just buy; they borrowed. Margin-loan balances hit a record ~₩60 trillion, and in May 2026 regulators let single-stock leveraged ETFs list to the public. By July, those products and their underlying shares were about 70% of daily trading volume.

The problem: the KOSPI’s two heaviest names, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, are more than half the index — so the whole market was a leveraged bet on two memory makers. When a soft Samsung report and the U.S. listing of SK Hynix cracked the trade in late July, leveraged ETFs and margined accounts sold in a cascade. The index fell ~11% in one day and ~6% the next, dropping 38.6% from its June peak. The government banned new leveraged listings, capped positions, and the finance minister publicly apologized for approving the products. Even so, the KOSPI ended the episode still up ~41.5% for the year — proof of how absurd the run had been, and of how unevenly the pain landed: the people wiped out were the ones who bought last, with borrowed money, near the top.

Terms used on this page
Leveraged ETF
A fund engineered to deliver a multiple of an index’s daily move — a 2× ETF aims to go up (or down) twice as much as its benchmark each day. The multiple resets every day, which quietly erodes returns in choppy markets (see “volatility decay”).
Single-stock leveraged ETF
The same idea aimed at one company instead of an index — e.g. a 2× Samsung or 2× SK Hynix product. Korea let these list to retail in May 2026; by July they and their underlying shares were ~70% of daily trading volume.
Volatility decay
Because a leveraged ETF resets daily, a stock that falls 10% then rises 11% (roughly flat) leaves a 2× ETF down. Over a violent month, the leveraged product loses far more than “twice the period move” — the crash made this textbook example real.
Margin loan
Money borrowed from a broker to buy more stock than your cash allows. It magnifies gains and losses, and the broker can demand repayment at the worst possible moment.
Margin call → forced liquidation
When a margined position falls enough, the broker demands more collateral. Miss it and the broker sells your holdings automatically — at whatever price the market offers — to repay the loan. You don’t choose the timing or the exit.
KOSPI
The Korea Composite Stock Price Index — the country’s benchmark. Its two heaviest members, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, together make up more than half its weight, so the index is really a bet on two memory-chip makers.
FSC / FSS
The Financial Services Commission (Korea’s top financial regulator) and the Financial Supervisory Service (its examiner) — the two bodies that scrambled to rewrite the leveraged-ETF rulebook mid-crash.

1. The state-sponsored bull

Korea’s crash didn’t start with a villain; it started with a policy. For over a year, the government ran a “value-up” programme to close the “Korea discount” — the market’s chronic cheapness — and encouraged ordinary households to move savings into domestic stocks. Layered on top was a real earnings story: the AI build-out made Korea’s memory-chip makers, Samsung and SK Hynix, indispensable suppliers of high-bandwidth memory.

The result was one of the most violent bull markets in modern history. The KOSPI ran a reported +116% to an all-time high of 9,385.59 on June 19, 2026, briefly making Korea the world’s sixth-largest equity market. Its volatility index hit a record 97.99 the same day — a benchmark that had spent decades below 30 was now swinging like a meme stock. That was the tell: a market this jumpy is a market running on borrowed money.

2. The accelerant: single-stock leveraged ETFs

A leveraged ETF is a fund built to deliver a multiple of an index’s daily move — a 2× fund tries to rise or fall twice as much as its benchmark each day. Because the multiple resets daily, these products quietly bleed value in choppy markets (see volatility decay in the glossary). They are, by design, short-term trading tools, not buy-and-hold investments.

In May 2026, Korean regulators let a more dangerous variant list to retail: the single-stock leveraged ETF — 2× exposure to onecompany. Assets across leveraged ETFs ballooned from about $5 billion at the start of the year toward $40 billion+, and by July the leveraged products plus their underlying shares made up roughly 70% of the exchange’s daily trading volume. A market that size, dominated by daily-reset leverage, is a market primed to move in violent, self-reinforcing steps.

3. A two-stock index

Here is the structural flaw that turned a product problem into a national one. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together make up more than half of the KOSPI’s weight. The most popular single-stock leveraged ETFs were built on exactly those two names. So the leverage wasn’t spread across a diversified market — it was piled onto the same two chip stocks that already were the index.

4. Leverage on leverage

Retail investors didn’t stop at the ETF’s built-in 2×. Many bought those leveraged funds with margin loans — borrowed money — on top. Margin balances hit a record ~₩60 trillion (roughly 2.6% of GDP), with retail margin trading around a third of activity at average leverage near 3×. Stack a 2× product on ~3× borrowed money and a household is carrying something like six times the exposure of the index it’s tracking — usually without realizing it.

5. The crack

The unwind had several fuses lit at once. On July 10, SK Hynix listed in the U.S., which helped start an unwind of crowded, leveraged Korean-equity positions. Then a soft Samsung earnings report collided with worries about global interest rates and China’s slowdown, and a genuine question the whole market had started asking: will the hundreds of billions pouring into AI infrastructure actually pay off soon?

The fall wasn’t orderly. By July 16 the KOSPI was already ~25% below its June peak. Then came the two sessions that defined the crash: −11% on July 28and −6% on July 29, wiping ~$2.18 trillion off Seoul’s market in two days — its steepest monthly drop on record.

6. The liquidation cascade

This is where leverage stops being a number and starts being a doom loop. As prices fell, margined accounts breached their thresholds; brokers issued margin calls; investors who couldn’t post more collateral were force-liquidated — their holdings sold automatically, at market, into a falling tape. Because everyone owned the same two stocks through the same leveraged products, that forced selling pushed those exact names lower, which triggered the next wave of calls. Reflexivity, in real time.

The toll was staggering and young. Over the episode, roughly 1.2 millionmargin accounts hit call thresholds and about 360,000 were force-liquidated, with total forced sales reported around ₩2.3 trillion. A reported 62% of those wiped out were under the age of 35 — the cohort most likely to have bought late, on borrowed money, into a product marketed as an easy way to double a bet. One Reuters dispatch was headlined with an investor’s words: “I couldn’t breathe.”

7. The rules change mid-crash

Regulators moved while the market was still falling — a sign of how fast the product had outgrown its safeguards. In two steps:

  • July 16 — the FSC hits the brakes. Korea’s Financial Services Commission halted new single-stock leveraged ETF listings and banned promotional events around them.
  • July 29 — the Finance Ministry rewrites the rulebook. With the index in free fall, the Ministry of Finance imposed a ~20% cap on how much of a portfolio can sit in a single leveraged product, raised deposit / margin requirements, required investors to complete simulated (paper) trading and a suitability quiz before trading them, lifted trading costs to discourage churn, and readied a legal framework for emergency market-stabilization.
  • The apology. Finance Minister Koo Yun-cheol publicly apologized for introducing single-stock leveraged ETFs, conceding they “had not been considered carefully enough.” The president — who had spent a year encouraging citizens into stocks — convened an emergency stabilization meeting.

Note what the rules did not do: they didn’t ban short-selling or blame speculators from abroad. Korea diagnosed this correctly as a domestic product-and-leverage failure — too much borrowed exposure, sold to people without the guardrails (position caps, suitability tests, decay warnings) that the products required. The fix was to make the leverage harder to reach, not to prop up the price.

8. The twin blow-up

The KOSPI crash wasn’t an isolated Korean story — it was one face of a single global unwind in the AI-infrastructure trade. The very same SK Hynix U.S. listing that helped light the fuse in Seoul also helped detonate a $20-billion American hedge fund the same month: Leopold Aschenbrenner’s Situational Awareness LP, which held leveraged Korean chip exposure among concentrated AI-infra bets and lost roughly two-thirds of its value in days. One macro crack; two blow-ups; the same lesson about leverage and concentration — one at the level of a marquee fund, the other spread across 360,000 households.

Why it matters — the lessons

  • Leverage on leverage is the real danger. A 2× ETF is a trading tool; a 2× ETF bought on 3× margin is a time bomb. The multiplier that made the boom feel effortless is the same one that erased the account in an afternoon.
  • Concentration hides inside “the index.” When two stocks are more than half the market, buying the benchmark — or a leveraged product on it — is a concentrated bet in disguise. Diversification you don’t actually have is the most dangerous kind.
  • Leveraged ETFs are not what they look like. Daily resetting means they don’t deliver “2× the period return” — in a volatile month they decay well past it. A product that’s fine for a day-trade is ruinous as a hold.
  • Forced selling is reflexive. When everyone owns the same names through the same leverage, margin calls feed on themselves: selling begets lower prices begets more selling. The crash was a plumbing failure, not (only) a change of mind about AI.
  • Averages hide the ruin. The KOSPI ended the episode still up ~41.5% for 2026, yet 360,000 accounts were wiped out. Index returns are a terrible measure of who got hurt: the losses concentrated on those who bought last, youngest, and most levered.
  • Product design is risk management. Letting single-stock leveraged ETFs loose on retail — without position caps or suitability tests — was the policy error the finance minister apologized for. The guardrails imposed mid-crash are the ones that should have shipped with the product.

It rhymes with every retail-leverage mania before it: a real story, a borrowing boom, a product that made the leverage effortless, and a reflexive unwind that punished the last ones in.
